🇨🇦 CANADA AT WAR
Lead story · August 25, 2026

Canada opens the next front: $27.6 billion in counter-tariffs

Ottawa has moved from warning to implementation, matching U.S. tariff rates on hundreds of American products while committing billions to keep Canadian firms and workers standing through a prolonged confrontation.

Canada’s trade war with the United States entered a new phase Tuesday morning when the federal government published the machinery of retaliation. Beginning September 8, Canada will impose tariffs of 15, 25 and 50 per cent on C$27.6 billion worth of American imports. The principle, Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne said, is deliberately simple: dollar for dollar and rate for rate.

The measure answers the United States’ decision to impose a 50-per-cent tariff on C$27.6 billion of Canadian goods under Section 338 and other tariff authorities. Ottawa’s list reaches across roughly 700 American product categories. Finance Canada says it has concentrated the response in sectors already being damaged by U.S. action, including steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper and electronics.

Three tariff bands — and a broad target

The 50-per-cent tier includes steel and aluminum products that had previously faced 25-per-cent Canadian counter-tariffs, as well as furniture and clothing or apparel. The 25-per-cent tier includes appliances, dairy products such as cheese, fish and seafood, and certain steel and aluminum derivatives. Other listed products face 15 per cent. Existing Canadian counter-tariffs on U.S. autos remain in place, so Tuesday’s announcement adds to the existing retaliation rather than replacing it.

The breadth is politically important. Washington’s latest action reaches far beyond the industries normally associated with trade disputes. American tariffs now touch more than 550 Canadian product categories, from honey and cosmetics to hockey sticks, wine, cement and electronics. Canada’s answer similarly spreads costs across a range of U.S. industries rather than concentrating retaliation in a single symbolic sector.

The second half of the strategy is fiscal

Ottawa paired the tariffs with C$7.5 billion in new and expanded support for workers and companies. That includes another C$1.5 billion through regional tariff-response programs, a new C$500-million Business Development Bank of Canada liquidity stream, broader access to BDC tariff programs, C$2 billion for the Canada Strong Diversification Fund and C$3.5 billion in rapid-response support for workers and employers.

Those measures build on nearly C$25 billion that the federal government says it has already deployed since U.S. tariffs began. That makes Tuesday’s announcement more than a retaliation list. It is an early outline of how the Carney government intends to finance resistance: use tariffs to impose reciprocal costs while using the federal balance sheet to stop temporary U.S. barriers from destroying otherwise viable Canadian businesses.

The distinction matters because trade wars are contests of endurance as much as leverage. A company can be competitive in normal conditions and still run out of cash while a 50-per-cent tariff blocks its principal market. Liquidity, wage support, retraining and capital for diversification can determine whether production survives long enough to reach a new customer or whether it disappears permanently.

A message to Washington — and to Canadian industry

The political language surrounding the package is harder than it was during earlier negotiating rounds. Ottawa says it negotiated in good faith but concluded that the final U.S. terms asked too much and offered too little. The government is now presenting the countermeasures as necessary to defend sovereignty, strategic industries and a level playing field.

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly also linked the strategy to the American political calendar, arguing that economic pressure can register in U.S. communities and businesses before the midterm elections. That is a reminder that Canada’s retaliation is designed not merely to collect tariff revenue but to create constituencies inside the United States with an incentive to oppose Washington’s policy.

There are risks. Canadian importers pay Canadian tariffs, and those costs can reach consumers and businesses that rely on American inputs. Retaliation can therefore damage the country using it. Ottawa is keeping a tariff-remission framework available for exceptional cases, an acknowledgement that targeting must be managed carefully.

The international significance

Reuters describes Tuesday’s package as a significant deterioration in the bilateral relationship. The confrontation now threatens not only particular industries but the assumptions underlying CUSMA and deeply integrated North American supply chains. Trump has separately threatened 50-per-cent tariffs on Canadian autos, parts and steel beginning in January 2027, which would take the dispute into industries where production crosses the border repeatedly before a finished vehicle reaches a consumer.

For now, major foreign governments remain cautious. There has been no comparable formal declaration from the EU, UK, Mexico or China endorsing Canada’s new retaliation. But international media are increasingly treating Canada as a test case: can a deeply integrated U.S. ally refuse American demands, absorb retaliation and emerge with meaningful policy autonomy intact?

Tuesday’s package is Canada’s first concrete answer. It does not settle that question. It does show that Ottawa is preparing for a fight measured in months rather than days.
