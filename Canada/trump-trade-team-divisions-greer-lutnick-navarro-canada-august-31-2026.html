Canada may not have been negotiating with one Washington.

New reporting describes a Trump trade team divided over what to offer Canada and who had authority to offer it. If that account is right, the last-minute changes that helped sink the deal were not simply bargaining tactics — they were the product of a U.S. government arguing with itself.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 2:00 p.m. ET

The most important new question about the collapse of the Canada–U.S. trade talks may not be what Canada rejected. It may be whether the United States ever had a single, stable offer to make.

The Guardian reported Monday on the competing centres of power inside Donald Trump’s trade team: U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and White House trade adviser Peter Navarro. The Washington Post had already reported a specific internal fight over Greer’s willingness to reduce a 50 per cent tariff on aluminum derivatives to 25 per cent in return for Canadian concessions.

According to the Post, Lutnick and Navarro objected strongly to Greer’s approach in a White House meeting because they believed the higher tariff was necessary to force more manufacturing into the United States. The Guardian’s account places that dispute inside a broader picture of a negotiating structure in which several powerful officials had different instincts and portfolios but Trump remained the ultimate authority.

Canada’s negotiators may have been bargaining not only across the table, but against a moving argument inside the U.S. government.

That possibility would help explain one of the central disputes since the talks collapsed on Aug. 22. Prime Minister Mark Carney says the United States introduced new terms late in the process that reduced the value and reliability of the agreement. Greer has publicly denied that Washington made last-minute demands and has said Canada was responsible for walking away.

Those two claims have been treated as mutually exclusive. But internal divisions in Washington create a third possibility: Greer may have believed he had authority to negotiate one set of terms, only to have other officials with control over key tariffs insist on harder conditions before the deal could be finalized.

The problem of divided authority

Greer’s office is the natural centre of a trade negotiation. He helped negotiate the original United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement during Trump’s first administration and has the formal trade-policy role. But several of the tariffs Canada most urgently wanted reduced — including measures affecting steel, aluminum and vehicles — involve authorities administered or heavily influenced by the Commerce Department.

That gives Lutnick enormous practical power. A trade representative can negotiate a concession, but if the department controlling the tariff does not support it, the concession may not survive.

Navarro adds another layer. He has long advocated aggressive tariffs and reshoring, and has publicly portrayed Canadian retaliation as evidence that Ottawa is behaving more like an adversary than an ally. His influence is not the same as Greer’s formal negotiating authority or Lutnick’s departmental control, but his views closely match Trump’s preference for tariffs as leverage.

The result is a structure in which Canada could hear one thing from a negotiator, another from a cabinet secretary and still face intervention from the president.

The aluminum fight

The Washington Post’s reporting offers the clearest concrete example. Greer was reportedly prepared to cut the tariff on aluminum derivatives from 50 to 25 per cent as part of a broader exchange. Lutnick and Navarro opposed that reduction, arguing that the higher tariff was important to U.S. manufacturing policy.

This was not a minor technical detail. Canadian officials have said the commercial value of the proposed deal depended on whether the tariff relief actually covered the products and sectors Canada needed protected. A headline tariff reduction could look significant while exclusions, product definitions or content rules stripped much of the benefit away.

Late in the negotiations, the United States also sought to exclude some heavy trucks produced in Ontario from tariff reductions, according to the Post. Canadian reporting has similarly described a dispute over medium- and heavy-duty trucks and over the fine print governing automotive relief.

That is precisely the kind of change that can transform a deal from politically difficult to economically unacceptable.

The French-language dispute

The same institutional confusion appears around culture and French-language protections. Carney has said U.S. demands reached into areas Canada considers sovereign and non-negotiable, including French-language and cultural policy. Greer has dismissed that account as false or exaggerated.

But the dispute is broader than whether one negotiator used a particular phrase. The underlying American concerns include Canadian rules affecting streaming services, online content and market access. Those issues can be framed in Washington as barriers to American companies even when Ottawa regards them as cultural policy.

When several U.S. officials are shaping the package, a Canadian negotiator needs to know not only what is being requested but whether any assurance survives the next internal review.

Reliability is itself a negotiating term

This is why the internal U.S. divisions matter beyond palace intrigue. Carney said the late changes called into question the reliability of the deal. Reliability is not an abstract diplomatic virtue. For a company deciding where to build a factory, or a government deciding whether to dismantle retaliatory tariffs, the durability of an agreement has economic value.

If Canada gives up leverage in exchange for tariff relief, it needs confidence that the relief will still exist after another White House meeting.

The U.S. system under Trump makes that confidence difficult. Trump deliberately centralizes decisions and frequently uses uncertainty as leverage. Officials may compete to interpret his preferences. Industries lobby different departments. Tariff authorities are scattered across statutes and agencies. A concession offered on Monday can become politically vulnerable by Friday.

That does not mean Canada’s negotiators were passive or faultless. Ottawa was seeking major concessions of its own, and the U.S. side says Canada expanded its demands late in the process. The point is narrower: the American account of a stable offer rejected by Canada is harder to sustain if the terms were still being fought over inside Washington.

What Canada should learn

If talks resume, Canada will need to negotiate differently. Every major concession should be tested against the official who actually controls the relevant tariff. Every verbal understanding should be translated into precise text early. And any final package should be judged not only by its tariff rates but by how difficult it would be for Washington to reopen or reinterpret it.

The lesson is not that Greer, Lutnick or Navarro is the “real” negotiator. The lesson may be that all of them are — until Trump decides otherwise.

That is an unusually difficult counterpart for any country. For Canada, whose factories and supply chains are built around predictable access to the U.S. market, it is a strategic problem.

The collapse of the talks therefore looks less like a single disagreement over one final offer and more like a collision between two kinds of sovereignty. Canada refused terms it believed reached too deeply into domestic policy. Inside Washington, competing officials were simultaneously fighting over how much economic pressure the United States should surrender.

That is the story behind the story: Ottawa was negotiating with the United States, but the United States was still negotiating with itself.
