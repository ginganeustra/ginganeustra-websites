MEXICO WATCHES CANADA’S TRADE WAR AND SEES ITS OWN WARNING

Mexican economists, writers and ministers are reading Ottawa’s rupture with Washington as a warning: formal trade rules are no longer the same thing as certainty.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 12:28 a.m. ET

Mexico is not speaking with Canada’s volume. It is not matching Ottawa’s defiance or turning its own talks with Washington into a public confrontation. But the Mexican voices now writing about Canada’s tariff crisis are not relaxed. They are reading it as a warning.

The collapse of Canada’s talks with the United States did more than produce another dispute between close trading partners. It exposed a negotiating method: tariffs first, pressure second, concessions demanded behind closed doors, and the terms of a supposed agreement capable of changing at the last moment. For Mexico, whose next round of negotiations with Washington is approaching, that is a live lesson.

Valeria Moy, the Mexican economist and El País México columnist, wrote after the Canada–U.S. talks broke down that the failure was not surprising. The warning signs had been present for weeks. Her account describes a sequence that Canada knows well: threat, apparent progress, optimism, a new demand, collapse and retaliation.

Canada had been told a deal was within reach. Then Washington’s terms changed. The result was 50 per cent tariffs on a broad range of Canadian goods and Ottawa’s decision to retaliate dollar for dollar. Moy’s larger point is the one Mexico must now absorb: a country cannot assume an agreement is safe simply because it is written down, because meetings have been productive, or because both sides would plainly benefit from avoiding damage.

In the present American approach, a treaty can be treated as a temporary instrument rather than a binding framework. Moy argues that Mexico should see Canada’s experience as a preview of the behaviour it may have to confront. Tariff threats can move a negotiation beyond ordinary trade questions and onto sovereignty, cultural policy, foreign relationships and domestic political choices.

That is why the Canadian fight matters well beyond the goods on the tariff lists. Canada did not simply refuse a technical adjustment. It rejected conditions its government described as unfair and uneconomic. The reported demands reached beyond immediate tariff relief into the country’s ability to make its own decisions. The details are contested; the pattern is not. Market access is being used as leverage over political ground.

Sonia Corona, reporting from Mexico for El País, describes a country watching the Washington–Ottawa clash with uncertainty while trying to protect its own position. Mexico is not a party to the immediate rupture, but it is very much a party to the larger question: whether CUSMA can still provide a stable basis for continental commerce.

President Claudia Sheinbaum has avoided a public escalation. That restraint is strategy, not indifference. She has sent Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard to Washington to assess the mood of the American administration and pursue relief from U.S. tariffs on Mexican steel, aluminum, vehicles and auto parts. Mexico’s public line is continued engagement and the hope that an agreement is still possible. Its actions show it understands the risk.

Mexico is already being asked to negotiate in an environment where the United States has preferred bilateral bargaining, even though the treaty is formally trilateral. Washington can identify each partner’s separate vulnerabilities, pursue different concessions, and use apparent progress with one country as leverage against the other. Canada and Mexico are left watching developments next door, calculating what precedent may appear at their own table.

Eyanir Chinea, also writing for El País México, reported that Mexico regarded any Canada–U.S. agreement as important information for its own CUSMA strategy. That was a cautious formulation before the talks collapsed. It now looks more urgent. Canada has supplied Mexico with information of a harsher kind: even after apparent progress, Washington may use timing and tariffs to demand more.

Ebrard has maintained a public posture of optimism, saying Mexico has made substantial proposals and expects serious agreements. Mexico has compelling reasons to avoid an immediate trade war. Its factories, auto-parts producers and export industries are tightly connected to the U.S. market. But optimism cannot by itself solve a structure in which one side is willing to turn trade access into a rolling political test.

Angélica Moreno Flores, a Mexican political scientist writing in El Universal, says attention has moved from the M to the C of T-MEC. For months the focus was Mexico’s tariff exposure and its own negotiations. Canada’s crisis is now the clearest picture of the continent’s new trade reality.

Moreno’s argument is not merely that tariffs are economically harmful. It is that tariffs have become instruments of political power. A tariff can protect an industry or raise revenue; it can also alter supply chains, force a bargaining concession and make an example of a country that refuses a demand. The question is not only who pays. It is what the threat is intended to obtain.

That is the sovereignty issue at the heart of the Canadian fight, and increasingly at the heart of Mexico’s anxiety. Mexico has faced American pressure over trade, migration, border enforcement, Chinese investment, industrial policy and security. The boundaries between those subjects are becoming less clear. A country seeking stable market access may be asked to demonstrate compliance in areas far removed from the product crossing the border.

Mexico and Canada do not face identical circumstances. Mexico’s economic integration with the United States is deeper in several sectors, and its government has used a less publicly confrontational style. Those differences may give Mexico room to manoeuvre. They do not give Mexico immunity. A more integrated relationship can make a country more valuable to the United States; it can also make it more vulnerable to coercion.

Moreno notes that Mexico has preserved tariff-free access for much of its exports to the United States. That is a real advantage. It should not be confused with certainty. Canada’s experience demonstrates how a country can remain inside the treaty framework yet face punitive tariffs, or be told that protection depends on accepting conditions outside its expected scope.

The annual-review system makes that distinction especially dangerous. The treaty remains in force, but every review creates another occasion for Washington to reopen pressure, demand more or threaten disruption. The old promise of continental trade was that known rules would replace arbitrary political interference. The Mexican writers now watching Canada see a different reality: the rules can still exist while political leverage dominates the result.

Sheinbaum and Ebrard are trying to manage a difficult balance. Mexico must defend its interests without supplying Trump with a public quarrel he can exploit. It must negotiate hard enough to protect its industries while keeping enough calm to maintain the talks. Canada’s refusal to accept a bad agreement may prove that Washington cannot automatically dictate terms to every partner. It may also increase the temptation to secure concessions elsewhere and declare victory.

The quiet from Mexico should therefore not be read as complacency. It is the quiet of a government studying a live demonstration of what may await it. Mexican writers are saying what official language cannot always say plainly: Canada’s experience is part of a recognizable pattern of negotiation by threat; tariffs have become a means of testing sovereignty; and Mexico is watching urgently because it knows its own turn is coming.

Mexico may not be on Canada’s side of the barricade today. But it is sitting very close to it. The question is not whether it can avoid every conflict with Washington. It is how much autonomy it can preserve when access to its central export market is made conditional on a continually changing political bargain. Canada has learned that lesson in public. Mexico is learning it in real time.
