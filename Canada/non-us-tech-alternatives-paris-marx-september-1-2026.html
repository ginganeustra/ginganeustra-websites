THE EXIT DOORS: A PRACTICAL GUIDE TO GETTING OFF AMERICAN TECH

Paris Marx’s revived guide is a useful starting point. We checked the alternatives, added the trade-offs, and built a category-by-category plan for Canadians who want less dependence on U.S. platforms without pretending digital purity is possible.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 4:17 a.m. ET · Analysis and service guide

OTTAWA — If you wanted a single image to explain Canada’s digital-sovereignty problem, Google supplied one last week: a map of Lake Ontario that suddenly said “Lake America” for users in the United States.

The map label did not make an American company uniquely powerful enough to rename Canadian geography. It did something subtler. It reminded millions of people that much of the digital layer through which they see the world — search, maps, email, cloud storage, office software, mobile operating systems and social networks — is controlled by a relatively small number of companies headquartered in the United States.

That is why a practical guide assembled by Canadian author and Tech Won’t Save Us host Paris Marx has suddenly become timely again. Marx’s “Getting off US tech” guide, first published in 2025 and updated Aug. 29, 2026, is not a manifesto for smashing every American device in the house. Its more useful premise is that digital dependence can be reduced category by category, even if eliminating it completely is nearly impossible.

The distinction matters. A Swiss email service may still rent servers or networking capacity from an American cloud provider. A Norwegian browser may use the open-source Chromium engine that grew out of Google’s browser project. A European search engine may supplement its own index with outside results. A Dutch phone maker may ship Android. “Non-American” can describe corporate control and jurisdiction without meaning every line of code, chip or server is outside the U.S. ecosystem.

For Canadians who want to make a practical start, the best strategy is therefore not purity. It is concentration risk: identify the services where one company has the most power over your data or daily habits and replace the easiest ones first.

1. Email, calendars and cloud files: Proton is the obvious first test

Email is one of the easier places to reduce dependence. Proton AG is a Swiss company based in the Geneva area and operates Proton Mail, Calendar, Drive, VPN, Pass and Docs. That makes it one of the few non-U.S. providers offering enough connected services to replace several parts of a Google or Microsoft account without forcing a family or small business to assemble six unrelated tools.

The practical migration is straightforward: move important correspondence first, keep the old Gmail or Outlook address forwarding for a while, export calendars and contacts, then decide whether cloud files also belong in Proton Drive. Canadians who prefer a domestic provider can also look at Canadian hosting companies for storage and web services, though there is no single Canadian consumer suite with the scale and polish of Google Workspace or Microsoft 365.

The larger lesson is that email lock-in is mostly habit. Your address can change without your social network disappearing. That makes it a better first target than messaging or social media.

2. Search: Qwant is European, but search independence is complicated

Qwant is headquartered in Paris and describes itself as a French, sovereign search engine. It is one of Marx’s most practical search alternatives. Its attraction is not merely location: Qwant emphasizes privacy and, with Ecosia, has been building European search-index technology rather than relying entirely on indexes owned by Google and Microsoft.

But this category illustrates why sovereignty is not binary. Building a full web index is extraordinarily expensive. Search engines have historically licensed results from one another, and a service can be European-owned while still depending partly on infrastructure or data supplied by a U.S. company. Qwant’s move toward more European indexing matters because it reduces that dependency rather than merely repainting the front door.

For an individual user, search is also the easiest experiment on this list. Change the default for a week. Try Qwant, Ecosia or another European service. If an obscure query fails, use Google when necessary. Reducing dependence does not require pretending every alternative is equally good at every task.

3. Browsers: Vivaldi moves corporate control to Norway

Vivaldi Technologies is headquartered in Oslo, with staff in Norway and Iceland. Its browser is highly customizable and gives users a serious alternative to Chrome, Edge and Safari. The caveat is important: Vivaldi uses Chromium, the open-source browser engine associated with Google’s Chrome project.

That does not make Vivaldi an American company. It does mean switching corporate jurisdiction is not the same thing as escaping the technological gravity of the U.S. browser ecosystem. Firefox is another powerful alternative engine, but Mozilla itself is American. For users whose priority is who controls the product and account relationship, Vivaldi is a clear non-U.S. option. For users whose priority is engine diversity, the calculation is different.

A sensible Canadian migration is to install Vivaldi alongside the existing browser, import bookmarks and passwords, and test banking, work applications and streaming sites before making it the default.

4. Maps: use several tools rather than hunt for one perfect Google replacement

Maps are one of the hardest categories because Google Maps combines road navigation, transit, business listings, reviews, Street View and a giant location database in one product. No single non-U.S. alternative matches every feature everywhere in Canada.

OpenStreetMap is the essential open geographic database underneath many alternatives. Organic Maps builds private, offline navigation on open map data. HERE WeGo comes from the European mapping company HERE Technologies. Transit, founded in Montreal, is particularly useful for public transit. The practical answer is not necessarily one substitute. It may be a small toolkit: one app for driving, another for transit and Google only when a specific business listing or Street View is indispensable.

The Lake Ontario fight demonstrates why that diversity matters. A country is less exposed when one company’s naming policy is not the only map most citizens ever see.

5. Phones: Fairphone changes the manufacturer, but not the entire software stack

Fairphone B.V. is based in Amsterdam and has built its reputation around repairable phones, longer useful life and more responsible sourcing. For people who want a non-U.S. hardware company, it is one of the clearest alternatives to Apple and Google-branded phones.

The limitation is Android. Most Fairphones still live inside the broader Android application ecosystem. That shows how deep platform dependence runs: you can change the company that designs and sells the phone without changing every service the phone expects.

For Canadians, availability, carrier support, warranty service and emergency features must be checked before importing or switching devices. This is a much more consequential migration than changing a search engine, so it belongs later in the process.

6. Office software: LibreOffice is the easiest zero-dollar experiment

LibreOffice is the mature open-source office suite stewarded by The Document Foundation, a German foundation based in Berlin. It can replace a large share of ordinary Word, Excel and PowerPoint use without a Microsoft subscription.

Compatibility is not perfect. Complex Excel macros, specialized corporate templates and collaborative Microsoft 365 workflows can make a full switch impractical. But for letters, reports, ordinary spreadsheets and presentations, the cost of testing is effectively zero.

Nextcloud, developed by a German company and an open-source community, can add self-hosted file collaboration. European providers such as Infomaniak also offer office and cloud services. Again, the objective is not to declare Microsoft unusable. It is to make sure Microsoft is not the only tool capable of opening your own work.

7. Translation and writing tools: DeepL is a serious European alternative

DeepL was founded in Cologne in 2017 and has become one of Europe’s best-known language-technology companies. Its translator and writing tools can replace a significant portion of the work many users send to Google Translate or American generative-AI services.

For Canadians, particularly in a bilingual country, translation is an example where European alternatives are not merely political substitutes; they can be competitive products in their own right. Reverso, headquartered in France, is another long-standing option.

8. AI: Europe has options; Canada has a capacity problem

Artificial intelligence is harder. France’s Mistral AI provides European large-language models and services, while open models can be run through non-U.S. hosting providers. Canada has world-class AI research and companies, but the consumer market remains dominated by American platforms and American cloud infrastructure.

This is where individual switching reaches its limit. If a Canadian organization replaces ChatGPT with a European model but runs the workload on an American hyperscale cloud, one layer of dependence has changed and another has not. Governments that care about digital sovereignty eventually have to address compute, data centres, chips, procurement and public-sector standards — not just which chatbot employees open in a browser.

9. Hosting and cloud: move the server, then ask who owns the infrastructure underneath

For websites and business infrastructure, Canadian firms such as FullHost can keep the contractual relationship and data hosting in Canada. Outside Canada, OVHcloud in France, Hetzner in Germany and Infomaniak in Switzerland are established alternatives to Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud. Nextcloud can provide self-hosted file collaboration.

But hosting is the clearest example of why a shopping list is not a national strategy. Data centres need power, networking equipment, processors, security tooling and global connectivity. A Canadian website can be physically hosted in Canada while depending on U.S.-designed chips, U.S. security services or U.S.-controlled software libraries. Digital sovereignty is a supply-chain question, not a flag beside a server rack.

10. Messaging and social networks: network effects beat ideology

Messaging is where many people will stop. A privacy-conscious user can prefer a different service, but a messaging app is useful only if the people one needs to reach are there. Signal is American. WhatsApp is owned by Meta. iMessage is Apple. Replacing them can mean asking an entire family, newsroom, hockey team or workplace to move at once.

The same is true of social media. Mastodon and the wider fediverse reduce dependence on a single corporation because users can join independently operated servers. But the people, organizations and breaking-news sources a person follows may still be concentrated on American-owned networks.

Marx’s guide is strongest when it refuses to pretend those network effects do not exist. The aim is to create alternatives where they are viable and to make dependence visible where it is not.

A realistic Canadian migration plan

For a household or small business, a sensible sequence is: first change search; then move primary personal email; then replace the browser; then test LibreOffice or another non-U.S. productivity suite; then move cloud files; then try alternative maps for routine trips; and only after that consider the harder categories such as phones, messaging and social networks.

Do not cancel the old service before confirming exports, contacts, two-factor authentication and account-recovery methods. Keep an American platform where a job, client or family group genuinely requires it. The point is not to create inconvenience as a patriotic ritual. It is to reduce the number of places where one foreign company can make a decision that instantly changes the tools Canadians rely on.

The Lake America lesson is bigger than the label

Google’s Lake Ontario decision is politically striking because the map is so visible. Yet the real sovereignty question is not the lake’s label. It is the architecture underneath daily life.

Canada cannot regulate itself into technological autarky, and it should not try. The United States is Canada’s neighbour, largest trading partner and home to many excellent products that Canadians will continue to use. But dependence becomes strategic when there are no credible alternatives, no domestic capacity and no practical exit.

That is the useful idea in Marx’s catalogue. Every workable alternative is a small exit door. A country with many exit doors bargains differently from one that has none.
