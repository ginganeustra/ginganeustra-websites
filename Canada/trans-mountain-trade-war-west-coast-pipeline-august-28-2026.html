The U.S. trade rupture is accelerating Canada’s new Pacific oil pipeline.

Trans Mountain says the confrontation with Washington has made a second west-coast export route more urgent as Canada moves to reduce its extraordinary dependence on the U.S. oil market.

Published Aug. 28, 2026

Canada’s confrontation with the United States is beginning to change the timetable of the country’s energy infrastructure.

Mark Maki, chief executive of the federally owned Trans Mountain Corporation, said Friday that the collapse of Canada-U.S. trade talks has increased the urgency of plans for a new oil pipeline to the Pacific coast — a project designed to give Canadian producers substantially greater access to Asian markets.

“Events around us have made (the west coast pipeline) much more urgent, and I think that’s actually good. It’s helpful,” Maki told Reuters.

The significance goes well beyond another pipeline proposal. Canada remains one of the world’s largest oil producers, but roughly 90 per cent of its oil exports still go to the United States. That concentration has become a strategic vulnerability as Washington escalates tariffs and political pressure against Canada.

The federal government and Alberta had already agreed in July to advance a new pipeline capable of carrying about one million barrels a day toward global markets. The proposed corridor would begin in the Bruderheim area of Alberta and largely follow the existing Trans Mountain route to southern British Columbia, avoiding any change to the federal north-coast tanker moratorium.

The strategic change: Canada is no longer discussing Pacific energy diversification simply as an economic opportunity. The deteriorating U.S. relationship is turning alternative export capacity into sovereignty infrastructure.

A pipeline already running close to full

The existing Trans Mountain system illustrates why the question is becoming immediate. The pipeline’s expansion entered service in 2024, tripling nominal capacity to about 890,000 barrels per day. Trans Mountain reported Friday that the system averaged 840,000 barrels per day during the second quarter — about 94 per cent of capacity — compared with 703,000 barrels per day a year earlier.

Much of that oil is already moving beyond the United States. The Canadian Press reported that roughly two-thirds of Trans Mountain volumes are destined for Asian markets by tanker, while other shipments serve British Columbia and the U.S. Pacific Northwest.

Trans Mountain is simultaneously trying to squeeze considerably more capacity out of the existing system. Its optimization program contemplates adding as much as 300,000 barrels per day.

The first stage uses drag-reducing agents to increase throughput by about 90,000 barrels per day. A larger mainline optimization project involving additional pumping capacity and other infrastructure could add approximately another 210,000 barrels per day by the end of 2028, subject to investment decisions and regulatory approvals.

That would take the existing system from roughly 890,000 barrels per day to as much as 1.19 million barrels per day before the proposed new west-coast pipeline is counted.

Trade diversification becomes national policy

Ottawa’s own description of the new pipeline is unusually explicit. In July, the federal government said the project was intended to “strengthen our sovereignty” and diversify Canadian energy exports toward new and growing Asian markets.

The proposed ownership structure would bring together Trans Mountain, the Alberta Petroleum Marketing Commission and Pembina Pipeline Corporation, with a meaningful opportunity for Indigenous equity participation. Trans Mountain and Alberta would initially divide most of the ownership between them, while Pembina would hold a private-sector interest.

The Major Projects Office is considering whether to list the pipeline as a national-interest project under the Building Canada Act, a designation intended to streamline federal review while maintaining environmental requirements and Indigenous consultation.

Maki told Reuters that Trans Mountain needs to move quickly on route planning and consultations so proponents can seek regulatory approval early next year.

The project remains a proposal. It has not received final regulatory approval, and the enormous investment required means its eventual economics will depend in part on Canadian production growth and long-term commitments from oil producers.

That uncertainty is real. Reuters reported earlier this month that several proposed Canadian pipeline expansions together could add far more capacity than producers currently appear prepared to fill. Large oil-sands producers have remained cautious about committing the capital required for major new production.

But the strategic calculation has changed.

The price of dependence

For decades, geography made the United States Canada’s overwhelmingly natural energy customer. Pipelines ran south, refineries were integrated across the border and the American market offered enormous demand immediately next door.

That system was economically efficient when the political relationship was predictable.

The current trade confrontation is forcing Canada to put a price on that dependence.

The United States has imposed 50-per-cent tariffs on a new range of Canadian goods following the breakdown of negotiations, while Canada has announced dollar-for-dollar retaliation. Washington has also threatened much more damaging action against the deeply integrated Canadian auto sector.

Oil itself is not the immediate target of those measures. But the dispute demonstrates how rapidly access to the U.S. market can become political leverage.

That is why Pacific export capacity now belongs in the same discussion as defence procurement, critical minerals, domestic manufacturing and expanded trade with Europe and Asia. Each is part of the same strategic problem: how much freedom of action does Canada possess when its largest economic relationship becomes coercive or unpredictable?

The answer will not be to abandon the United States. The two economies, particularly their energy systems, remain profoundly interconnected. Maki separately warned Friday that deliberately disrupting Canadian energy exports to punish the United States would be damaging to Canada as well.

Diversification is a different strategy. It seeks to give Canada choices.

A second Pacific pipeline capable of moving roughly one million additional barrels a day would represent one of the largest physical changes to Canada’s trading geography in decades. Combined with expansions to Trans Mountain, LNG terminals, ports, railways and new trade relationships, it would shift a greater share of Canadian energy toward markets that cannot be reached efficiently today.

Canada has talked about that objective for years.

The difference in August 2026 is that Washington is giving Ottawa a reason to move faster.
