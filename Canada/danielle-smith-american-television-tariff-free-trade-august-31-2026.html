Danielle Smith takes her case to American television.

The Alberta premier is urging a return to tariff-free trade and negotiations—while holding fast to her view that oil and gas should not become Canada’s bargaining weapon.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 7:02 p.m. ET

EDMONTON — Danielle Smith’s latest intervention in the Canada–U.S. trade fight is not aimed principally at Ottawa. It is aimed at Americans.

After appearing on Fox Business on Sunday, Alberta’s premier said she had used the interview to advocate for a tariff-free relationship and for the two federal governments to resume negotiations. Her public message was straightforward: tariffs and counter-tariffs raise costs, disrupt supply chains and put businesses under pressure on both sides of the border.

That is a familiar Smith argument, but the audience matters. The collapse of the Canada–U.S. talks has pushed provincial leaders into a kind of public diplomacy: trying to reach American voters, companies and elected officials directly, rather than leaving every message to federal negotiators. Ontario Premier Doug Ford has taken a more combative version of that campaign to U.S. television. Smith is choosing a different register—appeal, economic interdependence and a negotiated off-ramp.

The difference is not merely stylistic. Alberta’s economy is unusually exposed to the American market through energy, petrochemicals, agriculture and a web of cross-border industrial supply chains. Smith’s political instinct has been to preserve the commercial relationship while arguing that escalation threatens workers on both sides. Her pitch is that a tariff fight makes neither country stronger.

There is also a clear limit to her approach. Smith has repeatedly opposed using Alberta oil and gas exports as a retaliatory lever. Supporters see that as a defence of a sector that depends on stable access to U.S. buyers. Critics argue it narrows Canada’s bargaining room at precisely the moment Washington is demonstrating a willingness to use economic power for political aims.

That tension is now central to the Canadian debate. A country facing coercive tariffs wants negotiation; it also needs credible leverage. The federal government has chosen counter-tariffs scheduled to take effect Sept. 8. Smith’s position is that the pressure should not be allowed to rupture the underlying partnership—especially the energy relationship that links Alberta and the United States every day.

Her American television appearance followed President Donald Trump’s order directing U.S. federal use of “Lake America” for Lake Ontario. The symbolism has sharpened an already ugly dispute. Smith’s answer has been to emphasize the long alliance, shared security and integrated economy rather than trade insults with the White House.

Whether that message can move the administration is another question. Public appeals can keep attention on the costs of tariffs and give U.S. businesses a reason to press their own government. They do not substitute for a deal. But Smith is making a distinct Alberta case: Canada should be firm about sovereignty, avoid treating energy as expendable, and keep a route back to tariff-free commerce open.
