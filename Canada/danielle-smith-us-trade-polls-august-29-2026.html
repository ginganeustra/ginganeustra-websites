CANADA AT WAR
Alberta · United States · Trade · Public opinion

Danielle Smith heads to the U.S. as Alberta’s politics of resistance take shape.

The premier’s meetings in Tennessee arrive as Albertans broadly support walking away from an unacceptable U.S. deal—but remain wary of escalation, politically divided at home and firmly against leaving Canada.

Published Aug. 29, 2026 · Updated Aug. 29, 2026

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith will travel to the United States next week for meetings added to a trip that, according to her office, had been booked months ago. It is a modest itinerary on paper—meetings with Tennessee tourism commissioner Mark Ezell, Nashville Chamber leader Stephanie Coleman and Nashville Convention and Visitors Corp. chief executive Deana Ivey—but it carries a larger political purpose.

Smith’s office says the point is direct diplomacy: making the case to American officials and business leaders that tariffs damage Americans as well as Canadians. Alberta’s senior U.S. representative, Nathan Cooper, is travelling with her. The approach fits Smith’s repeated argument that Canadian legislators should work relationships in Congress and state capitals rather than rely only on Ottawa’s formal negotiating channel.

It also places her in a provincial mood that is more complicated than the usual picture of Alberta as reflexively hostile to the federal government. Two national polls released after the breakdown of Canada–U.S. trade talks found clear Alberta majorities backing Ottawa’s decision to walk away rather than accept a bad deal.

Alberta and the trade-talks breakdown
  • Angus Reid: 67% of Albertans said Canada was right to walk away from the U.S. negotiations.
  • Abacus Data: 61% of Albertans said suspending the talks was the right decision; 48% approved of the federal government’s handling of the negotiations and 24% disapproved.

Those figures are below the national results—76% support in Angus Reid and 71% in Abacus—but they are still decisive majorities. Alberta may be the softest region in the country for the Carney government’s trade posture, but it is not a province demanding capitulation.

Diplomacy without an energy weapon

Smith has drawn a line between making a forceful case for Alberta and turning Alberta’s exports into a blunt instrument. She has rejected calls to use oil shipments as a high-stakes bargaining chip. Her warning is that the United States could retaliate against refined products Canada imports, including aviation fuel. That is a practical supply-chain argument, but it is also a political one: Alberta wants leverage without giving Washington an excuse to intensify a fight it cannot control.

The U.S. trip therefore represents Smith’s preferred alternative—show up, make Alberta’s case, and seek allies in an American business and political system that has much to lose from a prolonged rupture. Whether meetings with state-level officials and business figures can affect a Washington-centred tariff dispute is uncertain. But the strategy recognizes that Canada’s exposure is not only diplomatic. It is commercial, regional and personal.

A close provincial race

Smith undertakes that mission while the provincial electoral picture is much tighter than the UCP’s 2023 victory suggests. The newest published provincial vote-intention surveys range from a near tie to a more comfortable UCP lead. Ipsos, fielded May 28 to June 1, put the UCP at 48% and the Alberta NDP at 45% among decided voters. Angus Reid, fielded May 22 to 24, found 46% for the UCP and 45% for the NDP. Léger’s late-May poll showed a wider 49% to 40% UCP advantage.

These are not interchangeable results; methods, samples and treatments of undecided voters differ. Together they say one thing clearly: the NDP remains competitive, while the UCP cannot regard its lead as automatic. Ipsos also found 56% disapproval of Smith’s overall performance as premier. A leader can retain party support while losing personal approval, but it leaves less room for political error.

Recent Alberta provincial vote intention
  • Ipsos, May 28–June 1: UCP 48%, NDP 45% among decided voters.
  • Angus Reid, May 22–24: UCP 46%, NDP 45%.
  • Léger, late May: UCP 49%, NDP 40%.

Separation is not the mandate

The strongest poll finding concerns the question Smith must handle most carefully. Alberta’s Oct. 19 referendum asks voters whether the province should remain in Canada or begin the legal process toward a future binding separation referendum. The latest Angus Reid survey, conducted Aug. 10 to 13, found 61% preferred Alberta remain in Canada and 33% favoured beginning that process.

When respondents were asked the plainer hypothetical question of whether Alberta should leave Canada, support fell to 30%. Angus Reid says 41% of those favouring the October process see it chiefly as a message to Ottawa, not necessarily a demand for actual independence. Other recent polling has put the stay side still higher: Ipsos found 72% wanting to remain and 19% wanting to proceed in late May and early June; Léger reported 73% remain in June; Janet Brown found 67% remain in April.

The exact wording matters, and no poll is a vote. But the trend is consistent: a large majority wants Alberta to remain Canadian. Smith’s U.S. diplomacy can speak to legitimate anger about tariffs and market access without treating separation as the province’s answer to them.

The political task is to hold those ideas together. Albertans can want tougher protection for their economic interests, accept a federal refusal to sign a bad deal, prefer active provincial diplomacy and still reject a break with Canada. Smith’s trip is an opportunity to make that case. It will be judged by whether it advances Alberta’s interests without deepening the instability the province says it wants to escape.

Reading the numbers: The national trade polls report regional Alberta samples, not standalone provincial surveys. Provincial vote-intention and referendum polls use different questions and methods; they are snapshots, not forecasts.
