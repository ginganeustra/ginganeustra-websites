A SASKATCHEWAN COPPER-ZINC MINE IS NOW PART OF CANADA’S NATIONAL-SECURITY STRATEGY

McIlvenna Bay is ramping toward commercial production after major federal backing and an Eldorado acquisition. Ottawa explicitly links its copper and zinc to national security, advanced manufacturing and supply-chain resilience.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 9:23 p.m. ET

Ottawa is describing a new Saskatchewan copper-zinc mine in terms that would once have sounded more at home in a defence briefing than a mining announcement. Natural Resources Canada said Wednesday that production is under way at the McIlvenna Bay mine in the Flin Flon Greenstone Belt and explicitly tied the project to national security, advanced manufacturing, clean-energy technologies and stronger supply chains.

The mine is not suddenly beginning from zero on Sept. 2. Eldorado Gold, which acquired Foran Mining earlier this year, announced first copper concentrate in June and zinc and pyrite concentrate followed in July. The federal announcement marks the project’s continuing ramp-up toward expected commercial production in the third quarter, with more than 400,000 tonnes of material already available for processing.

That distinction matters because the larger story is not a ribbon-cutting date. It is the way the federal government now talks about domestic mineral production. Copper and zinc are being treated as strategic inputs whose availability affects power grids, vehicles, defence systems, communications equipment and advanced manufacturing. In that framework, a mine in Saskatchewan is part of Canada’s ability to withstand disruptions abroad and supply allies without relying entirely on foreign sources.

McIlvenna Bay is expected to operate for more than 18 years. Eldorado has said 2026 production could include five million to 10 million pounds of copper, 3,000 to 6,000 tonnes of zinc, 5,000 to 10,000 ounces of gold and 100,000 to 200,000 ounces of silver as the operation ramps up. The company is also studying an expansion from roughly 4,900 tonnes of ore per day to about 7,000 tonnes, along with a possible silver-lead circuit.

The project has become a showcase for a more interventionist Canadian industrial policy. The federal government committed $20 million through the Critical Minerals Infrastructure Fund in December 2024, another $41 million through the Strategic Innovation Fund in January 2025 and, through the Canada Growth Fund, approximately $156 million in May 2025. The project was also referred to the Major Projects Office in September 2025.

Those numbers help explain why Ottawa is presenting the mine as more than a private commercial venture. Governments are increasingly deciding that access to certain minerals is too important to leave entirely to the short-term economics of commodity cycles. Canada has large reserves, but deposits do not create strategic independence unless mines, processing capacity, transport links and customers actually exist.

The labour dimension is substantial as well. Natural Resources Canada says McIlvenna Bay currently employs about 380 full-time workers and is expected to employ about 450 when commercial production is reached. Roughly 34 per cent of the workforce is Indigenous. The federal release describes the project as being developed with Indigenous partners, an important part of how Ottawa is trying to connect critical-minerals policy with regional employment and participation.

Eldorado’s acquisition of Foran in April also changes the ownership context. The Vancouver-headquartered gold producer bought the company that had developed McIlvenna Bay, adding the Saskatchewan copper-zinc asset to a larger international mining portfolio. That gives the project greater corporate scale, but it also reinforces the need to distinguish where a mine is physically located from who controls capital, processing decisions and downstream relationships.

For Canada, the sovereignty question is therefore broader than whether ore comes out of Canadian ground. The strategic value depends on whether Canada can process enough of it, move it through resilient infrastructure, retain skilled workers, build domestic manufacturing around it and diversify the customers who buy it. A mine can reduce dependence only if the rest of the chain does not recreate the same vulnerability somewhere else.

McIlvenna Bay is an early example of the direction Ottawa is taking. The language of critical minerals has moved decisively from economic development into economic security. When the federal government calls copper and zinc part of national security, it is acknowledging that the ability to produce ordinary industrial materials at home can become a form of strategic power.
