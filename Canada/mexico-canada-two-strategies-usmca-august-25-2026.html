CANADA AT WAR
Mexico watch · North America splits tactically

Mexico and Canada are now testing two different strategies against Trump.

Claudia Sheinbaum has chosen lower friction with Washington in hopes of protecting Mexican access to the U.S. market. Mark Carney has chosen retaliation and strategic autonomy. The result could reshape the entire North American trade system.

August 25, 2026 · Canada at War

The Canada-U.S. trade confrontation is no longer only a bilateral dispute. Mexico has become the crucial comparison case.

Reuters commentary published Tuesday described the contrast directly: while Prime Minister Mark Carney is accepting greater trade friction in order to defend Canadian strategic autonomy, Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum has taken the opposite course, seeking less confrontation with U.S. President Donald Trump in exchange for greater certainty and access for Mexican companies.

That does not mean Mexico has sided politically with Washington against Canada. It means the two U.S. neighbours are currently pursuing different methods against the same pressure.

Mexico moved earlier — and more quietly

The divergence predates the latest Canada-U.S. collapse. In July, Reuters reported that U.S.-Mexico talks were roughly six months further advanced than the Canadian track. Mexico had already moved to answer several American complaints, including tighter customs rules, new measures involving imports from countries without free-trade agreements, and action on intellectual-property concerns.

Those bilateral negotiations created an obvious tactical advantage for Mexico: it could try to settle specific U.S. demands before they became explosive political confrontations. Canadian negotiations, by contrast, became entangled with disputes over autos, steel, aluminum, dairy, alcohol restrictions and, increasingly, questions touching trade sovereignty, critical minerals and cultural policy.

The difference is now visible in outcomes. Canada has entered a cycle of tariffs and counter-tariffs after talks collapsed. Mexico is still trying to keep its negotiating channel with Washington functioning.

North America is becoming an experiment: does accommodation win better terms, or does resistance prevent progressively larger demands?

The danger for Canada

For Ottawa, Mexico’s relative success creates pressure. If Washington reaches bilateral understandings with Mexico first, those terms can become precedents. Canada may then face a choice between accepting conditions already normalized elsewhere in the continent or standing outside a new emerging arrangement.

Reuters identified that risk in July: separate U.S. negotiating tracks can produce terms that the third country must later accept, renegotiate or contest. That matters especially in tightly integrated sectors such as autos, where production crosses all three countries and where rules of origin only function cleanly if the continental system remains coherent.

Canada therefore has two jobs at once. It is resisting Washington directly, but it also has to prevent that resistance from becoming isolation inside North America.

The danger for Mexico

Mexico’s approach carries its own risk. Reducing friction can preserve access in the short run, but if Washington’s strategy is ultimately to weaken or replace the trilateral agreement with bilateral deals, Mexico could win tactical concessions while losing the larger system that has supported continental investment for decades.

That is why the future of the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement matters more than any single tariff line. Trump declined on July 1 to grant the pact a new 16-year extension, leaving it in force but subject to annual reviews. Reuters noted Tuesday that a failure to reach fresh revisions could gradually push the system toward bilateral arrangements, creating more uncertainty in investment, sourcing and transshipment.

Mexico is the United States’ largest trading partner. Canada is second. If the three-country framework fragments, neither Ottawa nor Mexico City can assume it will be replaced by something equally stable.

Canada and Mexico also carry old scars

The current split is complicated by a history of distrust. Reuters reported last year that relations between Ottawa and Mexico City deteriorated badly after Canadian political figures suggested Canada might be better off negotiating separately with the United States. Mexican officials remembered that as a potential betrayal of the trilateral system.

Carney later made a deliberate effort to repair that relationship with Sheinbaum. Canadian officials stressed that they wanted a resilient Canada-Mexico relationship, and both governments publicly signalled that preserving trilateral trade remained important.

That repair effort is more consequential now. Canada needs Mexico not necessarily to copy its confrontational strategy, but to resist a structural outcome in which Washington permanently divides the continent into separate bilateral relationships.

The strategic question

The key question is not whether Carney or Sheinbaum is “right” today. Their countries face different economies, domestic politics and exposures to U.S. pressure. The real test is what each strategy produces over time.

If Mexico’s lower-friction approach wins durable market access without major sovereignty costs, Ottawa will face pressure to explain why confrontation was necessary. If Canada’s resistance forces Washington to soften demands, Mexico and other trading partners may conclude that accommodation only encourages further pressure.

And if both strategies end with a weakened USMCA, then the apparent tactical winner may not matter. North America as a whole will have lost a predictable rules-based framework that supports roughly US$1.6 trillion in annual goods trade.

Why the world is watching

Reuters’ August 25 analysis makes the international significance explicit. Other governments are watching the Canadian and Mexican approaches as two competing responses to Trump’s trade tactics.

Canada is testing whether economic retaliation and refusal can defend policy autonomy even when the short-term cost is higher. Mexico is testing whether managed accommodation can preserve access and reduce uncertainty.

The outcome will matter far beyond Ottawa and Mexico City. It will help answer a broader question facing governments everywhere: when Washington uses access to the U.S. market as leverage, is it safer to compromise early or to resist before the demands expand?

For Canada, the Mexican angle is therefore not a side story. It is the continental context in which the entire confrontation will be judged.
