THE TARIFF WAR IS STARTING TO CHANGE WHAT CANADIAN COMPANIES BUY

Ottawa’s $14.1-million Nova Scotia package is more than a grant announcement. One manufacturer says it removed U.S. suppliers from consideration; others are using federal money to pursue Europe, defence work and a U.K. trade mission.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 3:28 p.m. ET

Photo: Cnccp5/Wikimedia Commons, CC0 1.0 public-domain dedication. Used as a representative image of CNC milling; it does not depict Ace Machining’s newly purchased machine.

The federal government’s tariff response is beginning to show up in machine shops, export plans and purchasing decisions. A new $14.1-million package for Nova Scotia businesses contains one especially revealing detail: a Dartmouth manufacturer says U.S. tariffs caused it to take American suppliers out of the running when it bought new equipment.

Atlantic Canada Opportunities Agency Minister Sean Fraser announced the package Wednesday at Ace Machining Ltd. in Dartmouth. Ace received a $442,000 non-repayable contribution to purchase and install an automated CNC milling station intended to improve productivity and help it compete for more work in Canada and abroad.

Company president and co-owner Ron Wallace said the first round of U.S. tariffs changed the procurement decision. Ace removed American suppliers from consideration and selected a Matsuura MX-420 distributed by Elliott Matsuura Canada. Matsuura is a Japanese machine-tool manufacturer. Wallace said one purchase would not determine the trade dispute, but that similar decisions by enough Canadian companies could send a message.

That makes the announcement more interesting than a conventional regional-development package. The tariff conflict is not only changing the price of goods crossing the border. It is beginning to influence which countries Canadian companies look to for machinery, where they seek customers and which supply chains governments are willing to subsidize.

The other recipients show the same pattern. Halifax-based QRA Consulting is receiving $400,000 in non-repayable support for business development and engineering work aimed at becoming ready for European markets, identifying prospective customers and expanding exports. MacKenzie Atlantic Tool & Die/Machining in Musquodoboit Harbour is receiving $956,820 for advanced CNC equipment that Ottawa says will strengthen its ability to supply Canadian and international defence and industrial programs.

SeaChange Biochemistry in Meteghan is receiving $163,700 for automated freeze-drying and packaging equipment, staff expansion, branding and participation in a United Kingdom trade mission. Other projects in the 27-recipient package include automation, cybersecurity, export certification and processing upgrades.

The money comes through the Regional Tariff Response Initiative, which Ottawa says now totals $3.45 billion nationally. The government added $1.5 billion to the program in August as part of a larger $7.5-billion package for workers and businesses affected by U.S. tariffs.

There are two different claims embedded in those numbers and they should not be confused. The government can document that money has been approved and equipment is being purchased. It cannot yet demonstrate that a European sales campaign will produce customers, that a U.K. mission will produce contracts or that new machinery will permanently replace U.S.-linked supply chains. Those are intended outcomes, not established results.

But the Ace decision is already real. A Canadian manufacturer says tariffs changed its purchasing criteria and eliminated American suppliers from consideration. At the same time, federal money is being directed toward European market entry, British trade development and defence-oriented production capacity.

That is what trade diversification looks like before the big export statistics move. It is granular: one machine, one supplier list, one engineering team, one trade mission and one potential defence contract at a time. If the Canada–U.S. dispute persists, these small procurement decisions will be worth watching because they can eventually harden into new commercial relationships that are much harder to reverse than a tariff schedule.
