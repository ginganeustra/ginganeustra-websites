Canadians back a hard line on U.S. trade—but the economic consent has limits.

A new Ipsos survey for Global News finds broad backing for Canada’s decision to walk away from an unacceptable deal and answer American tariffs. Its more revealing finding is that the willingness to absorb the price of that stance is unevenly distributed.

Published Aug. 29, 2026 · News synthesis

Canada’s trade confrontation with the United States has acquired something that governments facing economic coercion often need: public permission to resist. An Ipsos survey commissioned by Global News, conducted after the latest talks collapsed, found large majorities support Canada holding its ground and imposing counter-tariffs on U.S. goods.

The headline numbers are emphatic. Sixty-three per cent of respondents said Canada was right to stand firm, even if the choice brought higher costs and job losses. Seventy-three per cent supported Ottawa’s dollar-for-dollar response to the latest U.S. tariffs. Those are not narrow margins or a polite preference for a compromise. They suggest that many Canadians see the dispute as more than a technical fight over rates and product lists.

That matters because the federal government’s stated rationale is not merely commercial. In its announcement of countermeasures, Ottawa said it suspended negotiations rather than accept terms it believed would damage Canadian workers, strategic sectors and the national interest. Prime Minister Mark Carney has also connected the dispute to the country’s ability to maintain independent decisions on trade, culture and domestic policy.

Support for resistance is real

The survey was fielded online among 1,001 Canadian adults on Aug. 26 and 27, with quotas and weighting intended to reflect the population. Ipsos put its credibility interval at plus or minus 3.8 percentage points, 19 times out of 20, if all Canadian adults had been surveyed. That is a useful warning against treating any one poll as a referendum. It is also sufficient to establish the central result: this is not a divided country on the immediate question of whether Canada should simply concede.

Global News reported that support also extends beyond the tariff list already announced by Finance Canada. Respondents were broadly open to further measures involving energy and critical minerals, tighter border inspections for U.S. imports, a tougher tax approach to large American technology companies, and Canadian-preference rules in public procurement and infrastructure.

The figures do not tell Ottawa to deploy every one of those tools. A public appetite for leverage is not a substitute for assessing legality, supply chains, regional exposure, consumer costs or the effect on Canadian workers. Electricity restrictions, for example, would affect connected Canadian and American systems; counter-tariffs can protect some domestic producers while raising costs for importers and households. Public opinion measures consent, not a complete policy design.

Still, the result narrows the political space for a deal that appears to trade away core Canadian choices for a temporary easing of tariffs. The point of a negotiation is not simply to end it. It is to reach terms worth living with after the headlines fade.

The endurance-economy warning

The same survey supplies the caution that should govern the next phase. Younger Canadians were less likely than older respondents to support holding firm or retaliating. Global News reported 48 per cent support for standing firm among Generation Z adults and 57 per cent among millennials, compared with roughly four in five older boomers on the core measures.

The gap is not difficult to understand. Younger adults are more likely to be navigating high rents or mortgages, precarious work, debt and the everyday vulnerability of prices rising faster than incomes. A geopolitical posture that is survivable for an established household can be a much more immediate risk for someone already deciding between groceries, rent and an unexpected bill.

Fifty-two per cent of respondents said they were personally prepared to accept significant economic pain to help Canada weather the dispute. That is a majority, but it is not an unlimited blank cheque. The number falls to 41 per cent among Generation Z respondents and rises to 67 per cent among boomers. The government cannot responsibly read this as a national endorsement of hardship without a corresponding responsibility to cushion the people who will bear it first.

That is why the accompanying policy matters as much as the counter-tariffs. Finance Canada says its package includes $7.5 billion in new and enhanced supports, including regional assistance for small and medium-sized firms, liquidity programs and measures for workers and sectors harmed by U.S. duties. Whether those supports are fast enough, accessible enough and targeted enough will determine whether public solidarity remains durable.

A parliamentary test

There is another message in the poll that Ottawa should not dismiss. Sixty-two per cent of respondents told Global News they want Parliament recalled before its scheduled late-September return to debate and vote on the response to the United States.

That is not a rejection of the government’s trade stance. It is a request for democratic ownership of it. A trade crisis that may alter prices, jobs, investment and Canada’s long-term economic orientation cannot be managed only through ministerial announcements and news conferences. Parliament is where competing regional interests can be put on the record, where support programs can be tested and where opposition parties must state what they would do differently.

The poll therefore describes a country that is more resolved than resigned. Canadians appear willing to resist American pressure, but they also expect the costs to be shared fairly and the choices to be debated openly. The political advantage from standing firm will not last on symbolism alone. It will depend on competent protection for exposed workers, credible diversification beyond the U.S. market and an unmistakable refusal to turn sovereignty into a bargaining chip.

What this poll can and cannot show: It measures the views of surveyed Canadians during Aug. 26–27. It does not predict the economic consequences of tariffs, prove that every proposed countermeasure is workable, or settle the trade dispute.
