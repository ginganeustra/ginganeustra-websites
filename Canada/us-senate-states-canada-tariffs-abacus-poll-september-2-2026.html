THE CANADA TARIFF HAS BECOME A SENATE-RACE PROBLEM

A new five-state Abacus Data poll finds that supporting tariffs on Canada is a stated electoral liability for U.S. Senate candidates in every state tested.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 11:43 a.m. ET

There is a new measure of the domestic political cost of Washington's tariff campaign against Canada, and it is not national. Abacus Data surveyed registered voters in five states with consequential U.S. Senate races—Iowa, Michigan, Ohio, Maine and South Carolina—and found a net negative response in every state when voters were asked whether a candidate's support for tariffs on Canada would make them more or less likely to support that candidate.

The study was conducted online from Aug. 26 to 28 among 2,500 registered voters, with 500 respondents in each state. Abacus says each state sample was weighted independently for age, gender, region, education, race and ethnicity, and partisanship. The company says its objective was to measure how the trade dispute may shape the five Senate contests.

The strongest numbers are among independents. In accompanying analysis, Abacus chief executive David Coletto reported a net 40-point penalty in Michigan and a net 42-point penalty in Maine when independents were asked about a Senate candidate supporting the tariffs. Those are stated reactions to a survey question, not a prediction that tariffs will decide either election. But they are a direct warning against the proposition that pressure on Canada comes without a political price at home.

The economic geography is part of the point. Abacus chose Iowa for agriculture and potash, Michigan for auto manufacturing, Ohio for steel and parts, Maine for its deeply integrated border economy and South Carolina for export-oriented manufacturing. The poll was fielded after the collapse of Canada–U.S. trade talks and the new 50-per-cent tariffs on roughly $20 billion in Canadian goods, and after the announcement of higher tariffs on Canadian-made vehicles, trucks and auto parts beginning in 2027.

Across the five states, the pollster says voters were more negative about the administration's handling of trade with Canada than they were about Donald Trump overall. Most respondents expected tariffs to hurt the American economy, workers, farmers and their own states. Opposition to the proposed 50-per-cent tariff on Canadian-made vehicles exceeded support in every state, according to Coletto's analysis, from 51 per cent opposed in South Carolina to 67 per cent in Maine.

The result also undercuts the idea that the dispute has made Canada itself unpopular. Abacus found that positive views of Canada remained high in all five states and that between 55 and 72 per cent preferred the bilateral relationship that existed before Trump took office to the current one. Fewer than one in five preferred the current relationship, the pollster said.

There are limits to what this can establish. It is pollster-produced research, paid for by Abacus Data, based on online state samples of 500 registered voters each. The public release describes the weighting but does not make the full tables and every question wording freely visible without the pollster's report-download process. State-level estimates are necessarily more sensitive to wording, weighting and sampling choices than a large national survey.

It is therefore not proof that tariffs will determine November's Senate results, or that a voter who reports a penalty today will vote on trade alone. It is nevertheless a more precise political test than a national approval poll. In states connected to Canada through cars, commodities, border commerce and manufacturing supply chains, voters say a candidate's support for the tariff pressure campaign would cost that candidate support.

For Canada, the finding does not change the immediate commercial damage from tariffs. It does identify a potential pressure point in the American political system: the administration's strategy is being tested not only against Canadian endurance, but against voters in states whose economies know the cross-border relationship at close range.
