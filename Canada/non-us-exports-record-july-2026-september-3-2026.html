CANADA’S NON-U.S. EXPORTS HIT RECORD $25.6 BILLION AS U.S. SHARE FALLS TO 66.3%

Canada exported a record $25.6 billion in goods to countries outside the United States in July, pushing non-U.S. markets to 33.7% of merchandise exports. In 2024, before Donald Trump returned to the White House, the United States accounted for 75.9% of Canadian domestic exports.

Updated Sep. 4, 2026 · 1:14 a.m. ET

When Trump returned to office in January 2025, the latest full-year 2024 customs-basis figures showed $547.4 billion in Canadian domestic exports going to the United States, or 75.9% of the total, and $173.6 billion going elsewhere, or 24.1%.

In 2025, the U.S. share fell to 71.7%, its lowest annual level since the early 1980s. Exports to the United States declined 5.8% while exports to countries outside the United States rose 17.2% to an annual record.

The July 2026 figures are one month of seasonally adjusted merchandise trade on a balance-of-payments basis, so their dollar totals should not be compared directly with the full-year 2024 customs-basis totals. The change in shares — from 75.9% U.S. and 24.1% elsewhere in 2024 to 66.3% and 33.7% in July — provides the cleaner measure of the shift.

Canada exported a record $25.6 billion in merchandise to countries other than the United States in July, extending a shift in the destination of Canadian goods that was already visible in 2025 and has continued into 2026.

Statistics Canada said exports to non-U.S. countries rose 7.4% in July, the third consecutive monthly increase. Those markets took 33.7% of Canadian merchandise exports during the month, leaving the United States with a share of about 66.3%.

The comparison with the period before Donald Trump returned to office is substantial. Statistics Canada’s exporter-characteristics data show that 75.9% of Canada’s domestic exports went to the United States in 2024. Global Affairs Canada says the U.S. share then fell 4.2 percentage points to 71.7% in 2025, its lowest annual level since the early 1980s. Annual goods exports to the United States fell 5.8% in 2025 while exports to non-U.S. markets rose 17.2% to an all-time high.

The July 2026 figure is a monthly reading rather than a full-year result, and Statistics Canada’s monthly merchandise series is measured on a balance-of-payments basis rather than the customs-basis domestic-export measure used for the 2024 exporter-characteristics comparison. The dollar totals therefore should not be treated as a single directly comparable time series. The destination shares nevertheless show the same broad movement: from roughly three-quarters of Canadian exports going to the United States in 2024 to about two-thirds in July 2026.

The July increase outside the United States came during an otherwise weak month for exports. Total merchandise exports fell 2.3% to $76.1 billion. Exports to the United States dropped 6.6%, the strongest monthly percentage decline since April 2025, with lower shipments of crude oil and gold contributing most to the fall. Imports rose 2.2%, reducing Canada’s merchandise trade surplus with the world from $4.2 billion in June to $769 million in July.

Statistics Canada identified the Netherlands, China and Germany as the largest contributors to the July increase in non-U.S. exports. Shipments to the Netherlands included iron ores, nuclear fuel and crude oil; exports to China increased across several products; and copper ores drove higher exports to Germany.

The figures do not establish that the Canada-U.S. tariff conflict caused the change. Commodity prices, the timing of large shipments, exchange rates and demand for individual products can move monthly trade totals sharply. July’s data also end before the Aug. 21 collapse of the latest bilateral trade negotiations and before the new U.S. tariffs that followed.

The longer annual record, however, shows that diversification was not confined to a single July shipment pattern. Global Affairs Canada reported that non-U.S. goods exports grew 17.2% in 2025 while U.S.-bound goods exports contracted. The department placed those changes side by side but did not attribute the entire movement to tariffs.

Firm-level data also show that diversification is broader than it was a year earlier, although the United States remains deeply embedded in Canadian business. Statistics Canada reported in April that the number of Canadian enterprises exporting to non-U.S. destinations increased by 292 in 2025, the first increase since 2019. The share of Canadian exporting enterprises selling into non-U.S. markets rose from 34.1% in 2024 to 34.8% in 2025. At the same time, the number of enterprises exporting to the United States fell by 542, its second consecutive annual decline.

That change reverses one of the cautions that applied to the 2024 data. In 2024, the dollar value of exports outside the United States was rising even while the number of Canadian companies selling to those destinations had been falling. The 2025 exporter data show the number of firms selling beyond the U.S. market beginning to increase again.

The depth of U.S. dependence still varies enormously by industry. Statistics Canada’s 2024 customs-basis data show that 94.1% of domestic exports of motor vehicles and parts went to the United States, while 88% of energy exports did. Metal ores and non-metallic minerals were far less U.S.-dependent. A national shift in the export share therefore does not mean every industry has found a substitute for the American market.

July’s record non-U.S. total nevertheless marks a measurable change in where Canadian goods are being sold. Canada’s trade deficit with countries other than the United States narrowed from $6.1 billion in June to $5.1 billion in July, the smallest since January 2021.

The next trade releases will show whether the shift survives the newest round of disruption. August merchandise-trade figures are scheduled for Oct. 6 and will include the first days after the Aug. 22 U.S. tariff escalation. September figures, scheduled for Nov. 4, will be the first full monthly release capable of reflecting trade flows after Canada’s new counter-tariffs are scheduled to take effect Sept. 8.

What the July figures establish is narrower but important: before the newest tariff round began, Canadian exports outside the United States had already reached a record monthly value, and non-U.S. buyers were taking roughly one dollar in every three of Canada’s merchandise exports.
