Canada enters the tariff shock with an $8.8-billion external surplus

Canada’s current account swung from deficit to its largest surplus since 2005. Record energy exports did most of the work—but the numbers also reveal a country entering the latest U.S. trade escalation from a stronger external position than expected.

Published Aug. 28, 2026

Canada earned more from its transactions with the rest of the world than it spent in the second quarter of 2026, producing an $8.8-billion current-account surplus and reversing an $8.3-billion deficit only three months earlier.

Statistics Canada says it was the country’s first current-account surplus since the second quarter of 2022 and the largest since the final quarter of 2005. In the context of Canada’s renewed confrontation with the United States, the result matters because the current account is one of the broadest measures of a country’s economic relationship with the outside world.

The improvement was driven overwhelmingly by merchandise trade. Canada’s goods balance swung from a $6.4-billion deficit in the first quarter to a $12.2-billion surplus in the second—the largest goods surplus since 2008.

Energy did the heavy lifting

Goods exports jumped 13.1 per cent to a record $232.1 billion. Energy exports rose 27.4 per cent, with crude oil and bitumen exports reaching a record $44.8 billion and total energy-product exports reaching $60.6 billion.

That strength reflects both Canada’s enormous resource base and unusually favourable international conditions. Statistics Canada attributes part of the rise in prices to international production and supply uncertainty associated with the conflict in Iran.

It therefore would be a mistake to read the $8.8-billion surplus as evidence that Canada has already solved its structural trade vulnerabilities. A large part of the improvement came from a commodity-price windfall. But it is equally mistaken to dismiss what the figures say about Canadian economic capacity.

Canada was able to increase exports dramatically at the same time as imports reached their own record. Motor vehicles and parts exports increased 19.3 per cent as Canadian auto production rose, although they remained below the quarterly averages recorded in 2023 and 2024.

A stronger starting point

The timing is politically and economically important. These numbers largely describe Canada immediately before the latest escalation in the U.S. tariff confrontation. They are not a measurement of the damage—or resilience—that will follow the newest tariffs and counter-tariffs.

Instead, they establish the starting point. Canada entered that confrontation with a current-account surplus, a record level of goods exports and a substantial goods-trade surplus.

The figures also arrive alongside stronger-than-expected second-quarter GDP data, reinforcing the picture of an economy that had regained momentum before the newest tariff shock.

Foreign money was also moving into Canada

The financial account contains another striking number. Foreign investors increased their holdings of Canadian securities by $100.6 billion during the quarter. Foreign purchases of Canadian debt securities reached a record $110.2 billion, including a record $80.8 billion of government bonds.

Foreign direct investment in Canada totalled $25.9 billion, up from $18.8 billion in the first quarter. More than half of that investment was directed toward manufacturing and finance and insurance. The principal sources were the United States, United Kingdom and Netherlands.

At the same time, Canadian direct investment abroad slowed sharply, and nearly three-quarters of that outward investment went to countries other than the United States—another small but notable indication of the increasingly diversified geography of Canadian capital.

The sovereignty question

For Canada, an external surplus is not sovereignty by itself. The country remains deeply dependent on the United States as a customer, particularly for energy. The second-quarter figures demonstrate both sides of the Canadian problem: enormous capacity to sell resources and manufactured products abroad, but continued exposure to a concentrated continental trading relationship.

The strategic question is whether Canada can turn temporary external strength into durable diversification—more pipelines and LNG capacity to non-U.S. markets, more Pacific and Atlantic port capacity, deeper European and Asian trade, more domestic processing and manufacturing, and greater Canadian ownership of the intellectual property and industrial capacity behind its exports.

The second-quarter surplus does not answer that question. It does show that Canada is not beginning the latest stage of the trade confrontation from the position of external weakness that the first-quarter deficit suggested.
