Bessent and Champagne reopen the door.

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says he will meet Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne in Asheville — the first publicly announced cabinet-level bilateral contact since Canada suspended the trade talks on Aug. 22. The meeting does not restart negotiations by itself, but it breaks a week of public silence at the top of the relationship.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 2:00 p.m. ET

For the first time since the Canada–U.S. trade negotiations collapsed, a senior minister from each government is preparing to sit down face to face.

Scott Bessent said Monday that he would meet François-Philippe Champagne on the sidelines of the G20 finance ministers and central bank governors meeting in Asheville, North Carolina. The encounter is the first publicly announced cabinet-level bilateral contact between Ottawa and Washington since Prime Minister Mark Carney suspended the negotiations on Aug. 22 and called Canada’s negotiating team home.

That distinction matters. There may have been private calls, diplomatic messages or working-level contacts that were never made public. But since the breakdown, both governments have publicly behaved as though the negotiating channel was effectively frozen. A Wall Street Journal account four days ago described the two countries as bracing for a prolonged dispute and reported that no new talks were then planned.

The first opening is not a negotiating round. It is a conversation between the two finance ministers after a rupture that both governments had been treating as durable.

Bessent’s language makes the moment more complicated. In a CNBC interview carried by Global News, he said the United States was “not at war with Canada,” while accusing Carney of turning the dispute into a political shouting match and saying the prime minister was not doing what was best for Canada. He also argued that Canada should not engage in tit-for-tat retaliation against an economy 13 times its size.

So the meeting arrives with two messages from Washington at once. The first is conciliatory in form: a senior U.S. official is willing to meet a Canadian counterpart. The second is confrontational in substance: Bessent is rejecting Canada’s framing of the dispute and criticizing the strategy Ottawa adopted after the talks failed.

Why this is different

The Aug. 22 collapse was not an ordinary pause. Carney said the United States had introduced terms that were unfair, uneconomic and inconsistent with a reliable partnership. He said Canada would not compromise on sovereignty, French-language protections or culture, and that Washington had ultimately asked too much while offering too little.

Ottawa then moved from negotiation to retaliation. It announced dollar-for-dollar counter-tariffs scheduled to take effect Sept. 8 and began emphasizing trade diversification, domestic industrial resilience and new strategic relationships outside the United States.

Washington, meanwhile, publicly blamed Canada for rejecting what U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer called the best treatment available to any major exporter. The relationship then deteriorated further through new tariff threats and Donald Trump’s campaign to rename Lake Ontario “Lake America.”

Against that background, a Bessent–Champagne meeting is not routine G20 choreography. Both men have broader portfolios than the formal trade negotiators, but they are central economic voices in their governments. Champagne is responsible for Canada’s fiscal response to the tariffs and has been one of the ministers explaining Ottawa’s retaliation. Bessent is one of the most influential economic officials around Trump and is hosting the G20 finance meeting.

The Canadian government had already announced that Champagne would use Asheville to hold bilateral meetings aimed at trade diversification, investment and strategic partnerships. Bessent’s public confirmation now establishes that the United States will be one of those conversations.

What to watch

The immediate question is whether the meeting is simply a temperature check or the beginning of a channel that can carry actual negotiating work.

Neither side has announced the resumption of formal trade talks. Canada’s chief negotiator, Janice Charette, has said Ottawa’s “pens are down” but the door remains open to a deal that protects Canadian economic interests and sovereignty. That position leaves room for contact without pretending the central disagreements have been resolved.

There are at least four things to watch if either government issues a readout. First, does the language refer merely to the broader bilateral relationship, or specifically to restarting trade negotiations? Second, is there any movement on the Sept. 8 Canadian counter-tariffs? Third, does Washington soften the demands Carney identified as unacceptable? And fourth, do the two sides establish another meeting or a working channel after Asheville?

Until one of those things happens, the significance of Asheville is limited but real: the door is no longer completely shut.

The setting is also revealing. The contact is happening inside a multilateral forum, not through a specially convened Canada–U.S. negotiating session. That gives both governments political room. Champagne can talk without retreating from Canada’s decision to suspend the negotiations. Bessent can engage without saying Washington has reconsidered its terms.

For Canada, that may be the most useful form of first contact available right now.

Bessent’s contradiction

Bessent’s insistence that the two countries are not at war is worth taking seriously — and also testing against policy. Canada is facing 50 per cent U.S. tariffs on roughly $20 billion of goods, additional threats against major sectors, and demands Ottawa says reached into language, culture and Canada’s freedom to make trade policy.

The Canadian government has responded with tariffs of its own and increasingly uses the vocabulary of economic security and sovereignty. Whether that deserves the word “war” is a matter of political framing. The economic coercion itself is not rhetorical.

That is what makes Monday’s meeting important. It offers the first chance since the collapse for two senior officials to find out whether the public confrontation has left any space for practical bargaining.

If it has, Asheville may be remembered as the moment contact resumed. If it has not, the meeting will still clarify something Canada needs to know: whether Washington wants a negotiated relationship, or simply a Canadian acceptance of the terms already rejected.
