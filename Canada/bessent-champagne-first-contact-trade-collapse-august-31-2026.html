Bessent escalates — then sits down with Canada.

Hours before an expected face-to-face meeting with François-Philippe Champagne, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent mocked Canada’s ability to retaliate, blamed Mark Carney for a “political shouting match” and claimed Ottawa walked away from “the best trade deal of any country on the globe.” The G20 now becomes the first test of whether the rhetoric is theatre — or the beginning of a harder American line.

Updated Aug. 31, 2026 · 3:53 p.m. ET

ASHEVILLE, N.C. — Scott Bessent did not arrive at the G20 sounding like an official preparing to cool the Canada–U.S. trade dispute.

Speaking to CNBC on Monday, the U.S. Treasury secretary dismissed the idea that Canada and the United States were engaged in a conventional tit-for-tat fight because, he said, the American economy is roughly 13 times larger. He accused Prime Minister Mark Carney of transforming the dispute into a political shouting match and suggested Carney was acting in the interests of himself and the Liberal Party rather than Canadians.

Then came the taunt. Bessent mocked the idea of Canada confronting the United States by asking whether it would deploy “their two submarines from the Edmonton Mall.” The remark was delivered as a joke, but in the middle of a tariff confrontation involving Canadian sovereignty, economic retaliation and increasingly personal attacks from Washington, it landed as something more than comic relief.

Bessent also repeated Washington’s central account of the collapsed negotiations: that Canada had been offered what he called the best trade deal of any country in the world and chose to walk away at the last moment.

The striking thing about Monday is the contradiction: Bessent is publicly belittling Canada while simultaneously confirming that he will meet the Canadian finance minister.

Ottawa’s version of the collapse is different. Carney has said the United States introduced last-minute terms that Canada regarded as unfair and uneconomic and that raised questions about whether any agreement would be reliable. Canada suspended the negotiations on Aug. 22 and moved toward reciprocal tariffs scheduled to begin Sept. 8.

So the argument entering the G20 is no longer simply about tariff percentages. It is about who broke the negotiation, what kind of agreement Washington was demanding and whether the two governments still agree on the basic rules under which a negotiation can take place.

Champagne: “ready to discuss”

There has been no comparable rhetorical response from Champagne so far. Instead, the Canadian finance minister arrived in Asheville telling reporters that he came in a good frame of mind and was “ready to discuss.” Axios also reported that Champagne told international journalists Canada’s economy was doing fine despite the escalating trade confrontation.

That makes the expected Bessent–Champagne bilateral unusually important. It is the first publicly announced cabinet-level meeting between the two governments since the Aug. 22 breakdown. It is not, by itself, a resumption of trade negotiations. But it does reopen a senior political channel that had effectively gone quiet in public.

What matters later today is the language the two men use when they emerge. A bland statement that they discussed the bilateral economic relationship would mean very little. A reference to further talks, instructions to negotiating teams, the Sept. 8 Canadian counter-tariffs or another scheduled meeting would be much more significant.

There is also the possibility that one side simply repeats its existing position. If Bessent again says Canada must accept the deal Washington believes was already offered, or if Champagne says Canada will not return without changes to the U.S. terms, Asheville may confirm that contact has resumed without showing any narrowing of the dispute.

Europe has other fights with Washington

As of late Monday afternoon, Canada at War has found no substantive public European reaction specifically to Bessent’s remarks about Canada. But the atmosphere around the G20 is hardly one of uncomplicated allied solidarity.

European finance ministers are openly unhappy about Russia’s return to the G20 finance table. Reuters reported that German Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil warned against any normalization of relations with Moscow while Russia’s war against Ukraine continues. European officials also kept Russian Finance Minister Anton Siluanov out of the traditional G20 family photograph.

At the same time, the European Union has backed one of Bessent’s principal objectives in Asheville: additional economic pressure on Iran. The EU said Monday that it supports further measures, including the U.S.-led “Operation Economic Outcast,” while continuing to call for diplomacy and de-escalation. Bessent has been trying to build international support for sanctions strong enough to force Tehran back toward negotiations.

That mixture — agreement with Washington on Iran, disagreement over Russia and anxiety over U.S. tariff policy — captures the broader mood of this G20. French Finance Minister Roland Lescure compared the world economy to Asheville’s weather: unpredictable and foggy, with everyone hoping it clears.

What else is happening at the G20

The formal American agenda is built around economic growth, deregulation, global imbalances and sovereign debt. Bessent has argued that countries should “grow their way out” of rising debt burdens and has promoted the U.S. approach of lighter regulation and stronger private-sector investment. He has also been pressing the case for easing regulations on smaller banks.

The gathering is occurring against a much less orderly geopolitical backdrop. Iran and energy prices are dominating security-related economic discussions. European anger over Russia’s participation has spilled into public view. The U.S. Treasury Department is also facing criticism after restricting accreditation for some journalists from major American outlets, including Bloomberg and The New York Times.

That is the setting in which Canada’s dispute is being discussed: not an isolated bilateral quarrel, but one of several fractures running through a meeting that is supposed to coordinate the world’s largest economies.

What we are waiting for now

The next useful information should come from Bessent, Champagne or their staffs after the bilateral. Neither side has announced a precise public timetable for a readout, so comments could emerge in a hallway scrum, a formal statement or later interviews rather than at a scheduled joint news conference.

There are four questions worth watching. Did they actually discuss restarting formal negotiations? Did Washington offer any modification of the terms Canada rejected? Did Canada signal any change to the Sept. 8 retaliatory tariffs? And did they agree to another meeting or establish a working channel?

If the answer to all four is no, Bessent’s morning remarks may turn out to be the day’s real story: Washington using the G20 not to lower the temperature with Canada but to reinforce its argument that Ottawa is too small to win a prolonged confrontation.

If there is movement, however, the same day could end very differently from the way it began. The finance ministers’ meeting offers both governments a way to resume contact without either side admitting it has backed down.

For now, the door is open — but Bessent has made clear that Washington intends to walk through it from a position of overwhelming economic power.
