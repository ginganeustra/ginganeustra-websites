REPUBLICANS PRESSED GREER ON CANADA. IN PUBLIC, THEY BLAMED CANADA.

A closed-door Republican meeting exposed pressure inside Donald Trump’s party over the trade war. The public message afterward was much more disciplined: Ottawa, not Washington, is responsible for the impasse.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 9:23 p.m. ET

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer spent part of Wednesday answering questions from House Republicans about President Donald Trump’s trade war with Canada, a sign that the dispute is no longer confined to negotiations between Ottawa and the White House. According to Agri-Pulse and Axios, Greer faced questions at a closed-door meeting of the Republican Study Committee as lawmakers raised concerns about the administration’s broader trade agenda, including the Canada confrontation and agricultural issues.

The significance is not that House Republicans have suddenly broken with Trump. The public evidence does not support that conclusion. CBC reported that Republicans who spoke after the meeting generally defended the president’s approach, even as some members were privately uneasy about the political and economic consequences of a prolonged tariff fight. The more revealing development is the split between what Republicans were willing to press Greer about behind closed doors and the message senior members carried out to reporters.

House Ways and Means Committee chair Jason Smith supplied the clearest version of that message. “We want to negotiate. It’s the Canadians,” he told reporters after the meeting, according to Agri-Pulse. That formulation closely tracks the line senior administration officials have been using since the collapse of the talks: Washington says it was prepared to make a deal, Canada walked away and any decision to return belongs to Ottawa.

That matters because the same argument has now moved beyond the administration’s television appearances and negotiating team into congressional Republican messaging. Earlier Wednesday, senior Trump officials were already presenting Canada as the party that rejected a favourable agreement and would eventually have to return. Smith’s comment extends that frame into the House at the very moment Republican lawmakers are asking Greer harder questions about the costs and politics of the trade agenda.

There are reasons for Republicans to pay attention. Canada’s retaliatory tariffs are scheduled to take effect Sept. 8, and members representing agricultural, manufacturing and border-state constituencies have to account for the consequences at home. Axios described mounting dissatisfaction among House Republicans with parts of Trump’s trade policy. The Canada dispute was discussed alongside the administration’s decision to permit additional tariff-free beef imports, another issue with direct consequences for politically sensitive constituencies.

The meeting therefore presents two realities at once. The first is that Republican lawmakers are not simply ignoring the Canada conflict. They are questioning the administration’s trade representative about it in a private party forum. The second is that the public Republican position remains substantially aligned with the White House: Canada is being portrayed as the side that chose the rupture and must make the first move to end it.

For Canada, that distinction is important. Ottawa cannot assume congressional unease will automatically translate into political pressure for a softer American position. Concern about tariffs can coexist with partisan support for the president and with a public narrative that assigns responsibility to Canada. If the administration succeeds in making that narrative the default Republican account of the dispute, the political room for congressional Republicans to challenge Trump may remain narrow even when they dislike parts of the policy.

It also gives Canada at War a useful marker for following Washington’s daily message. The administration’s claim that Ottawa rejected a good deal is no longer being delivered only by cabinet officials and trade advisers. It is appearing in the language of congressional leadership. Whether that remains disciplined as retaliation begins and economic effects accumulate will be one of the clearest tests of how durable the White House’s Canada message really is.
