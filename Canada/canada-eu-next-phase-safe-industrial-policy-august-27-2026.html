Europe · Strategic integration

The next Canada-EU relationship is already being built.

SAFE, defence procurement, critical minerals and Carney’s call for a much deeper economic partnership show that the coming talks are starting from something more ambitious than CETA alone.

← Canada at War front page

Prime Minister Mark Carney says Canada will begin discussions with the European Union this fall on a much stronger and deeper security and economic partnership. But several components of that relationship are already operational.

Canada is the first non-European country admitted to the EU’s Security Action for Europe, or SAFE, defence-procurement instrument. The Council of the EU formally concluded the participation agreement in June, giving Canadian companies and Canadian-origin products a route into qualifying common European defence procurement.

The relationship has already produced a concrete procurement: Montréal-based Marconi Technologies is to build Canadian-made tactical radios for Poland’s Cyber Command, drawing on nearly 100 Canadian suppliers. Canada has also launched negotiations to purchase Italian M-346 advanced jet trainers, while France, Germany, Italy and South Korea have signalled critical-mineral stockpiling partnerships with Canada.

These are important precedents because they move Canada-Europe relations beyond conventional tariff policy. CETA opened markets. The next phase is increasingly about whether Canadian companies can participate in European industrial strategy — procurement, defence production, technology, critical minerals and strategic supply chains — and whether European companies receive comparable opportunities in Canada.

The sovereignty logic

Carney’s diversification strategy does not assume Europe can replace the United States. It aims to create enough alternative economic depth that Canada has choices. SAFE illustrates the principle: Canadian defence firms gain another major market and Europe gains a trusted non-European supplier base.

The same logic could extend to energy, AI, nuclear technology, research, strategic infrastructure and procurement. The more deeply Canada is connected to several large markets, the less any single market can dictate the terms of Canadian economic policy.

That is why the coming Canada-EU discussions deserve to be watched for language that goes beyond “free trade.” The significant question will be mutual access to industrial policy: whether Canadian firms can increasingly qualify for European subsidies, procurement and strategic programmes and whether European firms receive reciprocal treatment in Canada.

The Norway model shows how far European economic integration can theoretically go without EU membership. Canada is nowhere near that model today. But SAFE proves that bespoke forms of insider access can be negotiated. The architecture of a deeper relationship is no longer entirely hypothetical.

Sources: Council of the European Union SAFE decision, June 15, 2026; Prime Minister of Canada statements on G7 defence and critical-mineral partnerships and Aug. 22 trade-policy remarks.
