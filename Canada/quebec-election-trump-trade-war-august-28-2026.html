Trump moves to the centre of Quebec’s election

The U.S. trade war is reshaping Quebec’s campaign — and complicating the province’s oldest argument about sovereignty.

Published Aug. 28, 2026 · Canada at War

QUEBEC CITY — Quebec’s provincial election campaign has barely begun, but Donald Trump is already occupying a remarkable position in it: not as a distant foreign-policy issue, but as an adversary around whom competing visions of Quebec’s future are being organized.

Coalition Avenir Québec Leader Christine Fréchette opened the campaign for the Oct. 5 election with an unusually direct declaration: “My opponent is named Donald Trump.” She called the U.S. president the “real adversary” and asked Quebecers for a mandate to guide the province through the economic storm created by the Canada-U.S. trade war.

The message reflects immediate economic pressure. U.S. tariffs are hitting Quebec industries, while a National Bank analysis cited by The Canadian Press found Quebec faces the highest effective tariff rate on exports among Canadian provinces. The trade conflict is therefore not an abstraction for the campaign. It is becoming a test of which party can best protect jobs, investment and Quebec’s room to act.

Trump complicates the sovereignty question

The more consequential effect may be on the province’s sovereignty debate.

The Parti Québécois entered the election period leading in public opinion and committed in principle to another referendum on independence. But leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon said before the campaign that he would not hold that referendum while Trump remains president. On Thursday, he warned voters not to allow the Trump administration’s tariffs to “confiscate” Quebec’s election or determine the province’s political future.

The difficulty for the PQ is visible in the polling. Reuters reported Aug. 22 that support for Quebec independence is around 30 per cent, among the lowest levels in decades. Trump’s repeated threats against Canada — including his 51st-state rhetoric — have stirred Canadian patriotism even among some francophone Quebecers who might otherwise be sympathetic to sovereignty.

That creates an unusual political inversion. A U.S. president hostile to Canada might have been expected to deepen Quebecers’ doubts about the federation. Instead, the external threat is giving federalists a new argument: in a confrontation with the United States, Quebec may be safer and stronger as part of a country of roughly 41 million people.

Quebec Liberal Leader Charles Milliard made precisely that case, arguing that an international trade war demonstrates the advantage of remaining inside Canada. On Friday, he went further, promising to reduce the share of Quebec exports going to the United States by at least 15 per cent through market diversification.

A campaign about protection — but protection from whom?

Fréchette’s strategy also carries risks. The CAQ has governed for eight years and she inherited a party weakened in the polls after François Legault stepped down. Casting Trump as the central opponent gives her an opportunity to present herself as a crisis leader, but it does not erase voter concerns about health care, housing, affordability or the government’s economic record.

St-Pierre Plamondon is trying to prevent the campaign from becoming a referendum on who can best defend Canada. His counterargument is that Quebec sovereignty itself is a form of protection: if Canadians insist national sovereignty must be defended against Washington, sovereigntists can ask why Quebec should not make the same claim for itself.

That tension makes the 2026 election different from a conventional federalist-versus-sovereigntist campaign. Trump has inserted a second sovereignty question on top of Quebec’s traditional one. Voters are being asked not only whether Quebec should have more independence from Canada, but how much economic and political independence either Quebec or Canada can realistically maintain from the United States.

The trade war is already producing practical responses. Canadian Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc said Thursday that Washington had dropped objections to Canadian measures supporting French-language and cultural content after those policies had surfaced as trade irritants. In Quebec, where language and culture are core political questions, even trade negotiations can now be read through the lens of national identity.

The Oct. 5 vote will still turn on provincial issues. But the opening days have established something extraordinary: a Quebec election is being fought partly around the question of how to withstand pressure from an American president. Whether that strengthens Canadian federalism, revives Quebec nationalism in a new form, or simply pushes economic security ahead of both will be one of the campaign’s defining questions.
