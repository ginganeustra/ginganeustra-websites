The China risk is real. So is the American one.

An American energy-intelligence firm warns that Chinese rules could leave critical infrastructure dependent on Beijing. Apply the same test to American technology and Canada confronts a much larger question about sovereignty.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 5:45 p.m. ET

Editorial illustration: Canada at War. The image places the United States and China back to back and upside down to represent the same sovereignty test being applied to both powers.

A new warning from Enverus Intelligence Research says China’s latest overseas-investment rules could create a hidden vulnerability for North American power systems. The risk, Enverus argues, is not necessarily that Chinese-made batteries or solar inverters suddenly stop crossing the border. It is that equipment already installed may depend on Chinese engineering, software, diagnostics, training or remote technical support — and Beijing has strengthened its legal authority over some of those cross-border relationships.

That deserves attention. But there is an obvious question missing from the American discussion: what happens if the technology is American? The same sovereignty problem exists. The United States already controls the export of strategically important technology, software, technical data and expertise. It can restrict who receives them, require licences, prevent foreigners from gaining access to particular capabilities and impose new restrictions that alter services companies and governments expected to use. That does not make the Chinese risk imaginary. It means Canada should stop treating technological dependence on China as a unique category of sovereignty problem while assuming technological dependence on the United States is sovereignty-neutral.

China’s State Council Order No. 837 took effect July 1. The regulation establishes a more formal framework governing Chinese outbound investment and places national sovereignty, security and development interests among the objectives of the system. Enverus examined the framework from the perspective of American power developers and said Chinese-linked engineering, training and remote technical support could become subject to additional approvals, creating potential delays for projects that depend on continuing relationships with Chinese companies. Enverus considers battery storage and solar inverters particularly exposed.

There is nothing inherently implausible about that conclusion. Modern infrastructure is not simply a collection of physical machines. A battery system can depend on proprietary software, firmware, remote monitoring, replacement components and engineers who understand its control architecture. A solar inverter can require updates and manufacturer support years after installation. Ownership of the box does not necessarily give its purchaser complete control over the capability. That is the important insight in the Enverus report. Once that principle is accepted, however, it cannot logically stop at China.

A Canadian dependence on technology whose continued operation ultimately requires another government’s permission is a sovereignty dependency regardless of the flag on the supplier’s headquarters.

The United States operates one of the most extensive technology-control systems in the world. Under the Export Administration Regulations, American controls can follow U.S.-origin technology and software after it leaves the country, and in some circumstances can reach foreign-made products incorporating controlled American technology. American law also regulates the release of controlled technology or source code to foreign persons. Canada benefits from exemptions, licences and unusually favourable arrangements, but those are permissions created under American law. They are not the same thing as sovereign Canadian control.

Artificial intelligence has provided a particularly vivid demonstration of the principle. This year U.S. controls affected access to advanced Anthropic models and forced the company to distinguish between capabilities available internationally and more restricted systems available only to vetted American organizations. The exact restrictions and model availability have changed, but the sovereignty lesson is durable: access to a privately developed American technology can change because of decisions made within the American national-security system.

The United States has long asserted similar authority in other domains. The Department of Energy regulates transfers of certain unclassified nuclear technology and assistance abroad. American defence technology is subject to its own export-control system. Canada receives privileged treatment in both areas, but privileged access is still access governed by another country’s rules.

Canada’s F-35 program illustrates the distinction. Canada will own its aircraft and retains operational command. Claims that Washington can simply press a secret “kill switch” on Canadian F-35s should not be presented as established fact. At the same time, the aircraft exists inside an international sustainment, software, mission-data and upgrade architecture anchored by the United States. Canada has substantial operational sovereignty within that system. It does not have complete technological independence from the country that designed the aircraft and anchors its support network.

None of this requires pretending Washington and Beijing are politically identical. They are not. The narrower structural reality is enough: both governments regulate strategic technology in the name of national security, both possess legal tools to restrict technology transfers, and both treat technical expertise as an instrument of national power. For Canada, the policy question raised by Enverus is therefore not simply whether China poses a risk. It is how much critical infrastructure Canada can operate, maintain, repair and sustain if access to any foreign supplier’s technology is interrupted.

Chinese-made batteries and inverters clearly have a commercial presence in Canada, but presence does not itself demonstrate vulnerability. Exposure depends on procurement and support agreements, which technologies are embedded, where data resides, how updates are managed, whether proprietary components are required and whether Canadian operators can function independently if a relationship breaks down. The same audit should be applied to American equipment and services.

Framed that way, the Enverus warning is useful but incomplete. Enverus is a commercial North American energy-intelligence company whose customers pay it to identify risk. It operates inside an American energy-policy environment that increasingly understands China through the language of supply-chain security, reshoring and strategic competition. That perspective deserves scrutiny, but the public record does not justify dismissing the firm as an anti-China propaganda operation. Enverus has also identified costs and inflationary effects arising from American tariffs and has acknowledged continuing American dependence on Chinese manufacturing.

The more defensible conclusion is that Enverus looks at China through a North American commercial and strategic lens. Canada should neither discard that work nor import its assumptions wholesale. Instead, Ottawa should take the analytical tool Enverus is using on China and turn it on every major foreign supplier.

That means asking who owns source code, who controls software updates, where servers and data reside, who holds encryption keys, whether Canadians can repair a system without the manufacturer, whether technical documentation is available, whether a foreign government could legally prohibit the manufacturer from assisting Canada, whether replacement components have alternative sources and how long an essential system could continue operating if the political relationship with the supplier’s home country deteriorated.

The problem is not China. The problem is dependency without sovereign fallback.

China’s Order No. 837 may indeed increase risk for companies dependent on Chinese technical support. Enverus is reasonable to investigate it. But the regulation’s greatest lesson for Canada may be something the firm did not set out to demonstrate. China controls technology and overseas economic activity in the name of Chinese national interests. The United States controls technology, nuclear expertise, defence information and advanced computing in the name of American national interests. Strategic technology is not merely something companies sell. It is an instrument of national power.

For decades Canada could afford to regard American technological integration differently because the political relationship was unusually stable and the two countries operated within an extraordinarily integrated defence and economic system. The present Canada–U.S. confrontation raises the question of whether that assumption should still be embedded automatically in Canadian procurement decisions.

That does not mean Canada should replace American technology with Chinese technology. Changing which foreign country Canada depends upon does not produce technological sovereignty. Building the capacity to withstand interruption does. That may require diversified suppliers, contractual rights to technical information, Canadian data residency, independent maintenance capability, stockpiles of critical components, software escrow, interoperability requirements, domestic engineering expertise and procurement rules that measure foreign political dependency alongside price and performance.

The Enverus warning therefore deserves to be taken seriously — just not selectively. If dependence on Beijing is a security vulnerability because Beijing controls Chinese companies and technology, dependence on Washington must also be measured against Washington’s demonstrated ability to control American companies and technology. Canada does not need to decide that the two powers are equivalent to understand the structural issue. It needs to ask a simpler question: if somebody else can decide whether Canada is allowed to keep using something essential, how sovereign is Canada really?
