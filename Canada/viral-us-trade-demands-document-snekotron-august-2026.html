CANADA AT WAR
Lead investigation · The viral demands list

The document nobody can authenticate — and nobody can quite dismiss.

A pseudonymous X account published an unsourced list of alleged U.S. demands in the final Canada-U.S. trade talks. It has not been authenticated. Yet several central themes overlap with demands Mark Carney has described publicly or that major outlets have independently reported.

August 25, 2026 · Canada at War

The most consequential political document circulating in Canada this week may not be a government document at all.

An X account calling itself Dr.Snekotron, using the handle @snekotron, posted what it described as a detailed list of extraordinary demands inserted by the United States late in the Canada-U.S. negotiations. The account framed the alleged terms in emphatic political language, calling them a form of subjugation and arguing that Washington wanted power over Canadian infrastructure from ports to telecommunications and electricity grids.

The material was headed “Summary of Key U.S. Demands in 2026 Canada-U.S. Trade Negotiations.” It listed alleged demands touching foreign investment, third-country trade, critical minerals, digital legislation, cultural and French-language protections, Buy Canada procurement, supply management, autos, pharmaceutical patents, energy, defence and Arctic procurement, and customs rules.

The post spread rapidly. But there is a central problem: the document has not been authenticated. The original post did not disclose a source, identify a negotiator, provide a draft agreement, show metadata or explain how the information was obtained.

What is established: the list is circulating publicly and some of its subject areas overlap with disputes Canadian officials have acknowledged. What is not established: that it is an official U.S. document, a Canadian government summary, or a verbatim account of the final American negotiating position.

Why people are taking it seriously

The reason the list has not simply vanished as another social-media rumour is that it mixes claims that remain unsupported with themes that have subsequently appeared in public descriptions of the failed negotiations. Carney has described American pressure affecting Canada’s ability to make future trade agreements. Canadian officials have discussed a dispute over trucks and autos. Carney has also spoken about U.S. pressure involving Canadian cultural and French-language protections and a right of first refusal involving critical minerals.

Those overlaps matter. They do not prove that the list is genuine, but they make it harder to dismiss the entire thing merely because it appeared first through a pseudonymous account. The politically explosive architecture of the list — trade sovereignty, strategic resources, culture, industrial policy and continental alignment — resembles important parts of the dispute that Ottawa has described publicly.

International reporting has added to that effect. Major outlets have reported U.S. demands touching critical minerals, Canadian cultural protections and constraints affecting Canada’s future international trade relationships. Again, that is corroboration of themes, not authentication of this document.

The account behind the post

Dr.Snekotron is a pseudonymous X account, not a demonstrated government source. The distinction is important. The account can be identified as a distributor of the viral list; there is no public evidence establishing that it wrote the underlying material, received it directly from a negotiator or obtained an authentic leaked document.

The rhetoric of the post also matters. It did not present the list neutrally. It interpreted the alleged terms as “subjugation” and potentially “annexation.” That political interpretation does not by itself make the underlying claims false, but readers should separate the account’s conclusions from the evidence for the list.

That is why the most useful question is not whether the account sounds convincing. It is whether individual claims can be independently matched to official statements, credible reporting or an eventual negotiating text.

Reasons for skepticism inside the list

Some claims are much more specific than anything publicly confirmed. The document describes a functional U.S. veto over major foreign investments in Canadian ports, telecommunications, pipelines and power grids. No released negotiating text has established such a sweeping mechanism. It describes priority allocations and price guarantees involving Canadian energy and strategic resources in terms that go beyond the public record.

The list also combines the Digital Services Tax with the Online News Act. Canada had already moved on the Digital Services Tax before this final breakdown, which makes the chronology important. A later demand could have involved legislative completion or broader digital policy, but the image alone does not tell us that.

On defence, the surrounding political context makes American pressure plausible: Canada’s F-35 program, Arctic surveillance, NORAD modernization and defence spending are deeply connected to the United States. But the list’s claim of binding mandates requiring Canadian spending to be directed strictly toward U.S.-made military hardware has not been demonstrated by a published agreement.

The most accurate description is not “the leaked U.S. deal.” It is an unsourced list whose most important themes overlap strikingly with parts of the real dispute.

Why secrecy gave the document oxygen

The document appeared in an information vacuum. Canada and the United States negotiated behind closed doors. When the talks collapsed, neither government published the draft text that caused the rupture. Carney described categories of unacceptable demands. Ambassador Mark Wiseman said the written language diverged in several places from Canada’s understanding of what had been agreed verbally. U.S. officials offered their own account of why the talks failed. But the public still does not have the marked-up text.

Confidentiality is normal in trade negotiations. What is unusual is the scale of the consequences. The breakdown has produced severe tariffs, Canadian retaliation and an increasingly political confrontation between two countries whose economies, supply chains and militaries have been integrated for generations.

In that environment an unofficial list gains authority because it offers a coherent explanation for events that governments have only partly explained. Each time an official statement resembles one of its themes, supporters treat the resemblance as validation of the entire document. That is understandable, but it is not sound verification.

One confirmed subject does not validate every unconfirmed sentence. The list could be a reconstruction from genuine information. It could combine leaked material with speculation. It could be based on public reporting and then sharpened rhetorically. It could also contain claims that turn out to be wrong. Without provenance, those possibilities cannot be separated.

What would settle the question

The clearest answer would be publication of the negotiating text, a U.S. term sheet, a Canadian summary of rejected clauses or another document whose provenance can be established. That would allow the viral list to be compared line by line with what Washington actually asked Canada to accept.

Until then, the responsible position is neither blind belief nor casual dismissal. The list should be treated as an allegation and tested continuously against evidence as it emerges.

At present that test produces an unusual result. Its provenance is weak. Its distributor is pseudonymous and politically emphatic. Several claims are far more specific than anything governments have confirmed. Yet the central themes — U.S. pressure over Canadian trade autonomy, critical minerals, culture and language, strategic industries and continental economic alignment — increasingly resemble important parts of the dispute described publicly.

That is why the material continues to circulate. Not because it has been proven authentic, but because the real negotiations have turned out to be extraordinary enough that portions of an extraordinary-looking list no longer seem implausible.

There is also a lesson for Ottawa. If Canada rejected a deal because the final text crossed fundamental sovereignty lines, leaving the precise terms hidden indefinitely creates a vacuum. Unofficial documents will fill it. Transparency is therefore no longer merely a historical question about what happened in the negotiating room. It is part of the political struggle over what Canadians believe their government was asked to sign.

Bottom line

Canada at War cannot authenticate the viral document. But parts of its subject matter overlap with publicly acknowledged disputes. We are covering it because it is newsworthy and politically consequential, while clearly separating corroborated themes from claims that remain unverified.

Reporting note: This story distinguishes the existence of the viral post from the authenticity of the material attached to it. It will be updated if an official draft, term sheet or other verifiable negotiating document becomes public.
