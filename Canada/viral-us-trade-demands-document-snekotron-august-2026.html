🇨🇦 CANADA AT WAR
Lead investigation · The viral demands list

The document nobody can authenticate — and nobody can quite dismiss.

A pseudonymous X account published an unsourced list of alleged U.S. demands in the final Canada-U.S. trade talks. The Canadian Press says there is no evidence it is an official document. Yet several of its central themes overlap with demands Mark Carney has since described publicly or that major outlets have independently reported. That is why an unauthenticated document has become part of the political reality of the trade war.

Published August 25, 2026 · Canada at War

The most consequential political document in Canada this week may not be a government document at all.

On Sunday, an X account calling itself Dr.Snekotron, using the handle @snekotron, posted what it described as a more detailed list of extraordinary demands inserted by the United States late in the Canada-U.S. negotiations. The account framed the alleged terms in the strongest possible language, calling them a form of subjugation and arguing that Washington wanted power over Canadian infrastructure from ports to telecommunications and electricity grids.

The attached image looked formal enough to travel. Headed “Summary of Key U.S. Demands in 2026 Canada-U.S. Trade Negotiations,” it set out a dozen categories of alleged American demands: foreign-investment controls, trade restrictions against third countries, privileged access to critical minerals and energy, changes to digital and news legislation, weakening of French-language and cultural protections, removal of Buy Canada procurement preferences, concessions on supply management, altered auto rules, longer pharmaceutical protections, defence and Arctic procurement conditions, and lower customs thresholds.

Within hours, the post was everywhere. Search indexes captured it at roughly 1.2 million views, with thousands of reposts and likes. Copies of the list spread to LinkedIn, Threads, Substack and Bluesky. It began to function less like a rumour and more like a leaked negotiating paper.

There is a problem: nobody has authenticated it.

What we know: The Canadian Press investigated the viral list on August 25 and found no evidence that Canadian or American officials had released this comprehensive document. The original post supplied no source. A later graphic that falsely appeared to be associated with Global News could not be traced to a real Global News article. Canada at War is therefore treating the image as an alleged summary, not as an official U.S. negotiating document.

Why it has not simply disappeared as misinformation

The answer is that the list contains a strange mixture of things that are now demonstrably connected to the negotiations and things that remain wholly unproven. That mixture makes it more difficult to dismiss than a fabricated document whose contents bear no relation to known events.

The Canadian Press found that Carney himself had publicly identified four themes appearing on the list. He said Washington had sought restrictions affecting Canada’s ability to make future trade agreements with other countries. He described a dispute over Canadian-made trucks. He said U.S. negotiators pressed Canada over subsidies and protections connected to French language and culture. And he disclosed an American demand for a right of first refusal involving Canadian critical minerals.

Those are not minor coincidences. They go directly to some of the most politically explosive claims in the viral list: constraints on Canadian foreign economic policy, privileged access to strategic resources, and interference with language and cultural policy.

Major international reporting adds another layer. The Financial Times has reported that the failed U.S. package included demands touching French-language promotion rules, preferential access to Canadian critical minerals and restrictions on Canada’s international trade partnerships. Associated Press reporting has similarly described late U.S. demands that Canada believed intruded on its ability to conduct independent trade policy and protect its cultural identity.

That does not authenticate the list. It does something narrower but important: it establishes that some of the list’s most startling subject areas were real negotiating subjects.

Confirmed publiclyFuture-trade constraints, French-language/cultural disputes, critical-mineral first-refusal demands and truck/auto issues have all been described by Carney or Canadian officials.
Credibly reportedMajor outlets have reported additional U.S. pressure involving critical minerals, international trade partnerships and strategic industrial policy, even though no final draft has been published.
Still unverifiedThe exact infrastructure veto, capped mineral prices, sweeping energy-priority guarantees, pharmaceutical patent terms, de minimis changes and precise military mandates remain unsupported by a released negotiating text.

The account matters — but not in the way people think

Dr.Snekotron is not a government source, at least not one that has been demonstrated publicly. It is a pseudonymous political X account. The post does not identify an official, negotiator, document repository or news organization as the origin of the list. Nor does it provide metadata, a draft agreement, an annex number, a negotiating-room photograph or any other provenance that would allow the public to verify where the material came from.

That distinction is critical. The account can be identified as the distributor of the viral list; it cannot, on the evidence available, be identified as the author of the underlying claims or the recipient of a genuine leak.

The rhetoric of the post also tells readers something about its posture. It did not present the material neutrally. It interpreted the alleged terms as “subjugation” and potentially “annexation.” That does not make the list false, but it means the account was advocating a political conclusion at the same time that it was circulating purported factual material. A responsible reader should separate those two things.

The Canadian Press fact-check is especially useful here because it did not fall into either trap. It did not declare the whole list true because some items matched known disputes. It also did not declare every item false. Its conclusion was more exact: the comprehensive list is unsourced and unauthenticated, while some of the items have independently emerged as genuine sticking points.

There are also reasons for skepticism inside the document itself

One example is the Digital Services Tax. Canada had already announced in June 2025 that it would rescind the tax in order to advance broader negotiations with Washington. A 2026 document portraying repeal of that tax as a fresh last-minute U.S. demand is therefore at minimum imprecise unless it refers to legislation completing the repeal or to a broader package of digital-policy concessions.

The document combines the Digital Services Tax with the Online News Act, but there has been no comparable public confirmation that repeal of the Online News Act was a specific condition in the final August 2026 package. Likewise, the claim that Washington sought a functional veto over all major foreign investments in Canadian ports, telecommunications, pipelines and power grids has not been publicly substantiated in that sweeping form.

The same caution applies to the document’s energy language. There has been credible reporting that Washington wanted stronger guarantees involving Canadian oil, gas and strategic resources. But a guarantee of priority allocations and fixed preferential access to crude, natural gas, hydroelectricity and nuclear power is more specific than the evidence currently in the public domain.

On defence, pressure is plausible and some surrounding facts are known: Washington has wanted Canada to follow through on major U.S.-linked defence purchases, Canada’s F-35 program remains politically sensitive, and Arctic/NORAD modernization is deeply integrated with the United States. But the viral list’s description of binding spending mandates strictly tied to U.S.-made equipment still requires documentary proof.

The most accurate description is not “the leaked U.S. deal.” It is “an unsourced list whose most important themes overlap strikingly with parts of the real dispute.”

Why the secrecy created the perfect environment for the list

The viral document emerged into an information vacuum. Canada and the United States spent months negotiating behind closed doors. When the talks collapsed, neither side published the draft text that caused the rupture. Carney described categories of unacceptable demands, Ambassador Mark Wiseman said the written language diverged in several places from Canada’s understanding of what had been agreed verbally, and U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer blamed Canada for seeking more at the end. But the public still has no marked-up agreement showing what changed, when it changed, or who inserted the disputed clauses.

That secrecy is not unusual in trade negotiations. Drafts are routinely confidential. But the consequences here are unusual because the breakdown immediately produced 50-per-cent U.S. tariffs, Canadian retaliation and an open political confrontation between two countries whose economies and militaries have been integrated for generations.

In that environment, an unofficial list can gain authority simply because it offers a coherent explanation for events that governments have only partially explained. The more official fragments that appear to resemble the list, the more people treat the entire document as validated. That is an understandable psychological process — and a dangerous evidentiary shortcut.

One confirmed item does not validate eleven unconfirmed ones. Four overlapping themes do not establish that every sentence in the image came from an American draft. The document could be based on genuine reporting and then embellished. It could be a reconstruction from public information. It could combine leaked material with speculation. It could be entirely synthetic but unusually well informed. Without provenance, those possibilities cannot be separated.

What would settle it

The fastest way to resolve the question would be publication of the negotiating text. Opposition parties have already demanded greater disclosure of what Canada was asked to sign. A redacted draft, a U.S. term sheet, or even a detailed official list of rejected clauses could show which parts of the viral summary were accurate and which were invention.

Until then, the list deserves neither blind belief nor casual dismissal. It should be compared line by line with evidence as it emerges.

At present, that comparison produces an unusual result. The alleged document’s provenance is weak. Its presentation is not proof of authenticity. Its distributor is pseudonymous and politically emphatic. Several claims are much more specific than anything governments have confirmed. Yet the central architecture of the list — U.S. pressure over Canadian trade autonomy, critical minerals, culture and language, strategic industries and continental economic alignment — increasingly resembles the dispute that Canadian officials and major international outlets have described.

That is why the image continues to circulate. Not because it has been proven authentic, but because the real negotiations have turned out to be extraordinary enough that parts of an extraordinary-looking document no longer seem implausible.

For Canada, there is a larger lesson. If Ottawa believes it rejected a deal because the final text crossed fundamental sovereignty lines, then leaving the precise terms hidden indefinitely guarantees that unofficial documents will fill the void. Transparency is not merely a historical question now. It is part of the political struggle over what Canadians believe happened in the room.

Bottom line

Canada at War cannot authenticate this document. The Canadian Press found no evidence that either government released it, and the original X post disclosed no source. But four major themes in the viral list have been acknowledged by Carney or Canadian officials, while other elements have been independently reported by the Financial Times, AP and other credible outlets. The correct posture is therefore forensic rather than credulous: preserve the image, identify its provenance problem, track every claim, and update the record as negotiating details emerge.
