WASHINGTON BACKS OPENAI. CANADIAN CREATORS ARE GOING TO COURT.

The Trump administration is urging G20 governments to give AI companies room to train on copyrighted work. SOCAN has sued Suno in Canada, while Mark Carney has already said “laissez-faire” is not the answer to AI-era safety.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 3:28 p.m. ET

Two developments on opposite sides of the border have turned an abstract argument about artificial intelligence into a concrete Canadian sovereignty question: who writes the rules for the technology, and who gets paid when machines learn from human work?

In Washington, the Trump administration has formally entered one of the central American copyright fights over generative AI. In a brief filed in Manhattan federal court, the U.S. government backed OpenAI’s argument that using copyrighted material to train artificial-intelligence systems can qualify as fair use. Reuters and The Associated Press reported that the administration described AI training as highly transformative and warned that an overly restrictive interpretation of copyright could impede innovation, national security and U.S. economic competitiveness.

On the same day, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick carried that philosophy into an international forum. At the G20 Innovation Ministerial in North Carolina, the United States urged governments to develop rules that leave room for AI companies to train models on creators’ work while also protecting creators’ rights. Lutnick invoked the American doctrine of fair use. The proposal did not set out a detailed global copyright regime, and American fair use is not Canadian law.

That distinction matters because Canada has just acquired its own live test case. SOCAN, the Society of Composers, Authors and Music Publishers of Canada, announced Wednesday that it has sued Suno Inc. in Federal Court. SOCAN alleges that Suno’s generative-music platform produces and publicly streams outputs that replicate songs in SOCAN’s repertoire without consent or compensation and thereby infringes performing rights in the underlying musical works.

SOCAN says its claim identifies a sample of 150 publicly available Suno outputs. Its case is an allegation, not a finding by the court. The Canadian proceeding should also not be collapsed into the U.S. newspaper litigation: the legal claims, rights asserted and factual records differ. But the two disputes expose the same underlying policy collision. AI companies want access to enormous quantities of human-created material; creators and publishers are asking where permission, compensation and enforceable rights fit into that model.

The timing makes remarks Mark Carney made in Ireland before the June G7 summit look more consequential. Asked about artificial intelligence, child safety and the role of the United States in a changing international order, the prime minister said Canada would take child safety seriously and that “laissez-faire is not the answer to child safety.” He added that Canada would act even when not everyone around the G7 table agreed.

Carney was speaking specifically about AI-era safety and children, not deciding the copyright question now before the courts. It would be wrong to turn that quotation into a Canadian legal position on Suno or OpenAI. What it does reveal is a broader regulatory philosophy: Ottawa does not accept that the default response to fast-moving digital technology should be to leave the market alone.

That philosophy is also written into the government’s AI for All strategy. In launching it in June, Carney said that in the AI era, prosperity and sovereignty would belong to countries able to develop, adopt and govern the technology on their own terms. The strategy promises updated privacy and online-safety rules, protections for children, stronger AI-safety capacity and international partnerships designed to reduce technological dependence.

Europe provides the clearest contrast with Washington’s lighter-touch push. The European Union’s AI Act already requires providers of general-purpose AI models to maintain a policy for complying with EU copyright law and to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content. Providers of the most powerful models face additional safety and cybersecurity obligations. Canada has not simply copied the European model, but Ottawa and European governments increasingly occupy the same side of the argument that AI requires public rules as well as private innovation.

The G7 itself moved partway in that direction in June. Leaders, including the United States, backed a call for safer digital spaces for minors and acknowledged risks associated with conversational AI. That is important nuance: this is not a clean split in which Washington opposes every AI safeguard and Canada and Europe favour every regulation. The disagreement is over how far governments should go, how quickly, and where copyright, safety and sovereignty should constrain commercial deployment.

SOCAN’s lawsuit now gives Canada a domestic arena in which those questions will be tested. The U.S. government’s OpenAI brief and Lutnick’s G20 campaign show that Washington is simultaneously trying to shape the international answer. For Canada, the issue is larger than a single music platform or newspaper lawsuit. It is whether the rules governing a foundational technology will be imported from the United States, borrowed from Europe, or written in Canada around Canadian law and Canadian interests.
