WASHINGTON CAN’T STOP TALKING ABOUT CANADA. OTTAWA HAS STOPPED ANSWERING EVERY VOLLEY.

Trump, his economic officials and their media ecosystem have kept Canada in an almost daily public argument. The Canadian government is still meeting, preparing counter-tariffs and leaving the door open to a deal — but since Carney spoke Thursday, it has not tried to answer every new American provocation in public.

Published Sept. 5, 2026 · 8:25 a.m. ET

OTTAWA — The Canada–U.S. dispute has developed an increasingly visible imbalance. Washington keeps talking. Ottawa, at least in public, has become much quieter.

That does not mean the Canadian government has gone silent or stopped working. Ministers met Friday with the Advisory Committee on Canada-U.S. Economic Relations. Officials continue preparing counter-tariffs scheduled for Tuesday. Prime Minister Mark Carney has said Canada remains open to a stable and credible agreement.

But the public tempo is no longer symmetrical. Since formal talks stopped, Donald Trump has posted about Canada, threatened its economy, complained about trade and banks and now said the United States could simply stop trading with the country. Howard Lutnick, Scott Bessent and Jamieson Greer have all carried parts of the administration’s case into television interviews and other public appearances.

Canada has not responded to each intervention. By early Saturday morning, no new public statement from Carney or a senior federal minister had been located answering Trump’s latest and most explicit threat: that Washington could declare “no trading with Canada.”

Carney answered Thursday

The last substantial public answer from the prime minister located for this analysis came Thursday in Thunder Bay. Carney rejected claims from Lutnick and Bessent that his government abandoned a nearly completed trade agreement for domestic political advantage.

Carney said he did not regard appointed American cabinet secretaries as authorities on Canadian politics and repeated that Canada would accept a deal only if it was stable, credible and mutually beneficial. He also said some U.S. positions that had been unacceptable appeared to have softened, leaving open the possibility of renewed negotiations.

That followed a sharper line earlier in the week, when Carney said the Americans needed to stop doing memes, stop “throwing shade” and stop trying to look tough if they wanted serious discussions. It was a direct response to the tone surrounding the dispute, but it was not followed by a Canadian answer to every new statement from Washington.

Friday brought government activity, not a rhetorical counterattack

Dominic LeBlanc chaired Friday’s meeting of the federal advisory committee on the U.S. economic relationship. Chief negotiator Janice Charette, Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne and Industry Minister Mélanie Joly were among the senior officials involved.

The government readout said the group discussed the latest U.S. tariffs, Canada’s countermeasures, assistance for workers and businesses, resilience, diversification and Team Canada engagement. Its tone was institutional and procedural. It did not trade insults with Washington or answer the administration’s claims one by one.

There is an important timing point. The readout preceded Trump’s later Oval Office remarks explicitly proposing an end to trade with Canada. As of early Saturday, the federal government had not issued a public answer to that escalation that could be located in the official statements and major reporting reviewed.

Washington’s argument has become a rolling public event

Across the border, the dispute has rarely been allowed to sit still. Senior administration figures have repeatedly appeared on television to explain why they say the negotiations failed. The details vary, but a recurring proposition has been visible: Washington offered unusually favourable terms, Canada walked away, and Ottawa will eventually have to return.

Greer carried that argument into a Fox appearance this week and then into an extended Financial Times interview published Saturday. Lutnick and Bessent have given their own versions. Trump has supplied the broadest and most volatile interventions, ranging from economic warnings to Friday’s explicit trade-cutoff language.

Friday added a new category. Trump began the day by threatening to stop trade with countries running deficits with the United States unless the Federal Reserve cut interest rates. Later, in the Oval Office, he explicitly used Canada as an example of a country with which the United States could simply stop trading.

He said such a move would save the United States as much as US$90 billion and claimed Canada conducts 95 per cent of its business with the United States. The latest merchandise-trade data put the U.S. share of Canadian goods exports at 66.35 per cent in July, not 95 per cent.

Silence can be observed. Motive cannot.

Analysis: It would be easy to label Ottawa’s restraint a deliberate communications strategy: deny Trump the reaction he seeks, stop responding to every threat and let Tuesday’s counter-tariffs speak for themselves. There is not enough evidence to state that as fact.

The observable fact is simpler. The American administration is conducting a large share of the dispute in public, through presidential remarks, social-media posts and repeated interviews. The Canadian government is not matching that cadence. It continues to act and occasionally speak, but it has not tried to answer every American volley.

There are several possible explanations. Ottawa may be trying to preserve room for negotiations. Officials may have concluded that responding to each statement only lengthens the argument. They may be waiting for a concrete policy rather than reacting to threats. Or the quieter period may simply reflect the normal rhythm of a government heading into a weekend after several days of public appearances.

Without confirmation from ministers or officials, choosing among those explanations would turn inference into reporting.

The next Canadian statement will tell us more

The quiet period is therefore useful as an observation, not yet as a theory. It becomes more meaningful the longer it continues and the more the American rhetoric escalates without a Canadian answer.

Tuesday provides a fixed deadline. Canada’s announced retaliatory tariffs on C$27.6 billion in U.S. goods are scheduled to take effect at 12:01 a.m. Sept. 8. Unless the policy changes, Ottawa is about to move from words to another material economic measure.

That creates two very different public tracks. In Washington, senior officials and the president continue narrating the dispute in real time. In Ottawa, the government’s most consequential near-term response is already written into a tariff schedule.

Whether that contrast is intentional will require evidence. For now, the contrast itself is the story.
