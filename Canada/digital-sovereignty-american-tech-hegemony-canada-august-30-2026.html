THE EMPIRE IN YOUR POCKET

Google’s “Lake America” looks like a ridiculous fight over a name. It is actually a remarkably clean demonstration of something far more serious: countries can retain their borders, governments and flags while surrendering control of the digital infrastructure through which their citizens increasingly see and navigate the world.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · 11:11 p.m. ET

This is a clear and harsh example of why Canada — and much of the world — is struggling to maintain real sovereignty under the predatory reach of American power and a technology industry that can behave as though national borders are merely another product setting.

The argument is not that Google has literally annexed Lake Ontario. It has not. Nor did Donald Trump acquire the legal authority to rename a body of water for Canada merely by signing an American executive order.

The important facts are more interesting.

Google says the United States Geographic Names Information System formally changed the American federal name for Lake Ontario to “Lake America,” and Google followed its longstanding Maps policy of reflecting official government sources. American users therefore see “Lake America.” Canadians continue to see “Lake Ontario.” Users elsewhere see both names. Google has made its policy explicit in a public statement.

That distinction matters. Google did not announce that Trump gets to name Canadian geography.

And yet the episode showed what happens when Canadian institutions build themselves on top of somebody else’s digital machinery. Canadian websites and applications that draw mapping information from third-party systems can inherit a foreign change even when the Canadian government, province or institution has made no corresponding political decision. The label can arrive through the plumbing.

There, in miniature, is the problem.

Canada had not changed the name. Ontario had not changed the name. Canadian naming authorities had not changed the name. The people running a Canadian service might not have decided to change the name.

The infrastructure can change it for them.

MapQuest demonstrated that the outcome was not technologically inevitable. It publicly declared that it would not make the change. Apple continued to show Lake Ontario in Canada. Google made one choice. MapQuest made another. The federal order was the same; the private implementation was not.

That is what turns a silly cartographic argument into a sovereignty story.

A country can exist on paper and still lose control

For most of modern history sovereignty had a physical vocabulary: territory, borders, ports, railways, telegraph lines, radio frequencies, banks, armies, currency, postal systems and power stations. Control enough of those things and a state had some reasonable claim to control its own affairs.

The digital state has acquired another layer.

Search determines what can readily be found. Maps determine what places are called and how they are represented. Social networks determine much of what gets distributed. App stores determine what software can economically reach citizens. Cloud companies store government and corporate information. Advertising exchanges finance journalism. Payment systems intermediate commerce. Artificial-intelligence companies are beginning to intermediate knowledge itself.

An extraordinary proportion of that infrastructure belongs to corporations headquartered in one country: the United States.

Alphabet owns Google Search, Google Maps, YouTube, Android and much of the advertising infrastructure of the web. Apple controls one of the two dominant mobile operating systems and a powerful app-distribution channel. Meta owns Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Threads. Microsoft operates Windows, Office, Azure and LinkedIn. Amazon operates AWS. American companies also dominate major portions of cloud computing, enterprise software, digital advertising and frontier AI.

This does not make those corporations simple extensions of Washington. They have different interests, frequently fight American governments and are themselves subject to American regulation. It does, however, create a structural fact: a Canadian government can pass a law, a Canadian court can issue a judgment and Canadian voters can elect an entirely new Parliament. None of those things creates a Canadian Google, Android, AWS or Azure the following morning.

Formal sovereignty and practical autonomy are no longer the same thing.

Canada’s own government now says as much. Its digital-sovereignty work is based on the principle that Canada must be able to control its data, technology and essential online services rather than become dangerously dependent on foreign companies, systems or laws.

The central question is no longer simply: “Who made the rule?” It is: “Whose infrastructure makes the rule real?”

Canada has been here before

None of this is entirely new. The technology changes. The sovereignty argument repeats.

In the 1920s the disruptive technology was radio. Canada’s airwaves were increasingly filled with programming originating outside the country. The 1929 Aird Commission concluded that broadcasting had become too important to national life to be left entirely to commercial and foreign forces. Its work helped lay the intellectual foundation for a Canadian public broadcasting system and, eventually, the CBC.

Radio was the Google of its day: borderless, technologically dazzling, hugely popular and capable of making a political boundary seem almost irrelevant. Canada’s answer was not to ban American radio. It was to ensure that a Canadian broadcasting system existed beside it.

The next version of the argument came with computers and databases. Federal privacy work in the 1970s was already discussing the movement of personal information across borders. The question became sharper after 9/11 as governments expanded national-security powers.

In 2004 British Columbia became embroiled in a dispute over the outsourcing of health-information processing to a company with American links. The concern was that information about British Columbians could become reachable through American law, particularly powers associated with the USA PATRIOT Act. The federal Privacy Commissioner stressed the basic jurisdictional reality: once information moves abroad, the law of the foreign country can matter. British Columbia responded with restrictions governing how public-sector personal information could be stored or accessed outside Canada.

Then the United States enacted the CLOUD Act in 2018. The U.S. Justice Department’s own explanation is important: a provider subject to American jurisdiction can, after valid legal process, be required to produce data within its possession or control regardless of where the company has chosen to store it. American warrants remain subject to judicial and constitutional requirements; this is not an unlimited licence to rummage through foreign data. But the jurisdictional principle matters.

Putting a server in Toronto does not necessarily solve the sovereignty problem if the company controlling the server remains subject to another country’s law.

Europe learned the same lesson. In 2020, in the decision widely known as Schrems II, the Court of Justice of the European Union invalidated the EU-U.S. Privacy Shield governing some transatlantic data transfers because the protections against American government access were not adequate under European fundamental-rights standards.

These disputes are normally described as privacy cases. Underneath, they are arguments about power.

Digital sovereignty reduced to one sentence: Which country’s law follows the data — and which country’s infrastructure is essential enough that everyone else has to live with the answer?

Canada’s digital defences are real — and fragmented

Canada is no longer doing nothing. It is doing quite a lot. The difficulty is that the pieces have emerged separately — broadcasting regulation here, privacy reform there, cloud procurement somewhere else — without yet amounting to a comprehensive national doctrine of digital autonomy.

The federal government’s Digital Sovereignty Framework says Ottawa must have enough autonomy over its digital assets and services to manage and protect them regardless of where technologies are developed, hosted or supported. There is an important limitation: the framework is principally about the Government of Canada’s own digital operations. The broader industrial and national economic problem is larger.

Shared Services Canada’s 2026–27 plan moves from theory toward infrastructure. Ottawa is developing sovereign-cloud hosting, Canadian data-residency capabilities and a dedicated private-cloud environment inside Canadian jurisdiction, while expanding work with Canadian providers.

That is significant because it treats cloud infrastructure as more than an IT procurement line item. It recognizes it as strategic infrastructure.

Canada is also trying to reclaim authority over other portions of the digital stack.

The Online Streaming Act, passed in 2023, extended Canada’s broadcasting framework into the streaming era. Its implementation requires major online streaming groups to contribute to Canadian cultural production. The Online News Act attempted something similar with journalism. Google ultimately obtained an exemption from mandatory bargaining after agreeing to contribute roughly $100 million annually, indexed to inflation, to Canadian news organizations. Meta chose another route and withdrew news from its principal Canadian platforms.

That episode should sober anyone tempted to reduce digital sovereignty to the phrase “just regulate them.” Regulation has much more force when the state possesses enough market leverage, alternative infrastructure or allied support to make withdrawal more expensive to the corporation than compliance.

Otherwise the platform possesses a veto of its own.

Canada’s Competition Bureau is pursuing Google in a separate advertising-technology case, alleging that the company abused a dominant position by tying together its tools, favouring its own services and inhibiting rivals. The allegations remain before the Competition Tribunal and have not been finally adjudicated.

Meanwhile, Canada’s privacy architecture is again under reconstruction. Bill C-36, the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, was introduced in June 2026 and remains before Parliament. Ottawa says the old private-sector regime predates AI at scale, deepfakes and modern algorithmic decision-making. Bill C-34, the Safe Social Media Act, would create a Digital Safety Act and a new Digital Safety Commission for regulated social-media, chatbot and other services. The federal Privacy Act governing personal information held by federal institutions is also under review after decades without fundamental modernization.

These are not trivial measures.

But they expose Canada’s recurring problem: we are regulating foreign infrastructure faster than we are creating credible Canadian alternatives to it.

The tax Canada surrendered

Nothing illustrates the imbalance more cleanly than the Digital Services Tax.

Canada designed the DST around a simple proposition: companies earning enormous amounts from Canadian users, Canadian advertising and Canadian data should contribute tax even when the conventional international corporate-tax system allowed profits to be booked elsewhere.

The tax was three per cent on certain revenues from online marketplaces, targeted advertising, social media and some sales or licensing of user data. It was aimed at very large businesses: a company generally had to meet a €750-million global-revenue threshold and a C$20-million threshold for covered Canadian revenues.

Finance Canada projected $2.3 billion in revenue in the first fiscal year — reflecting multiple earlier tax years — and roughly $900 million annually in each of the following four.

Then Washington objected.

On June 29, 2025, the Carney government announced that Canada would rescind the tax to advance negotiations on a new economic and security relationship with the United States.

It did not merely postpone collection. Bill C-15 subsequently abolished the Digital Services Tax Act retroactively to June 20, 2024, providing refunds with interest to businesses that had already paid. The legislation received royal assent on March 26, 2026. Finance Canada’s briefing material described the legal result in blunt terms: in effect, it would be as though the DST had never existed.

There are legitimate arguments against digital-services taxes. Critics cite the possibility of double taxation, discrimination against foreign firms and conflict with international corporate-tax reform. Those arguments deserve a hearing.

But the political lesson is impossible to ignore.

Canada enacted a tax. The United States made it a bilateral issue. Canada removed it.

That is leverage. And leverage is one of the ways sovereignty becomes real rather than ceremonial.

Europe decided size could be a weapon

Europe faces many of the same technological dependencies Canada does. Its answer has been considerably more muscular.

The European Union does not possess a European Google, Meta, Microsoft or Amazon. But it possesses something Canada lacks: a market of roughly 450 million people acting through a single regulatory system. It has increasingly converted that market into geopolitical power.

The Digital Services Act applies broadly to social networks, marketplaces, app stores and other intermediaries, imposing transparency, safety and systemic-risk obligations. The European Commission directly supervises the largest platforms.

The Digital Markets Act goes directly after gatekeeper power. Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, Microsoft and Booking are among the designated gatekeepers. One entry in the EU’s official gatekeeper list is especially relevant to this story: Google Maps.

Europe does not treat a mapping platform merely as a useful website. It treats it as a core platform service wielded by a gatekeeper.

On July 23, 2026, the European Commission fined Google a combined €890 million under the DMA — €460 million over self-preferencing in Search and €430 million over restrictions on steering users away from Google Play. Google has the right to challenge enforcement decisions, but the scale of the sanction demonstrates the difference between Canadian and European regulatory weight.

Europe is now pushing deeper into infrastructure. In 2026 the Commission moved ahead with sovereign-cloud procurement for EU institutions and developed a Cloud Sovereignty Framework that scores providers on strategic sovereignty, jurisdiction, data and AI control, technological autonomy, supply-chain exposure, operations and security.

In June, the Commission proposed a broader European Technological Sovereignty Package covering semiconductors, AI, cloud infrastructure and open-source software. Its proposed Cloud and AI Development Act explicitly addresses Europe’s overreliance on non-European cloud providers.

That is a qualitatively different idea from regulating harmful content. Europe is beginning to ask who owns the stack.

Its tax policy is messier. There is still no single permanent EU-wide digital-services tax, but several member states have refused to wait for a global settlement. France, Italy and Spain operate three-per-cent digital-services taxes, while Austria has a different digital advertising levy. The OECD’s Pillar One project is intended eventually to shift some taxing rights toward the markets where users and customers are located and to remove national DSTs.

Canada’s problem is therefore not that taxing digital giants is an eccentric idea no other democracy contemplated. It is that Canada entered the fight largely alone against the country in which most of the targeted giants reside.

Europe enters similar fights as a bloc.

That difference is enormous.

Australia: make leaving expensive

Australia offers another model. Canberra has repeatedly tried to make access to the Australian market conditional on accepting Australian rules.

Its original news-media bargaining regime became famous in 2021 when Facebook temporarily shut news sharing during the dispute. The weakness of the system later became obvious when Meta decided not to renew commercial news deals.

Australia’s answer was not simply to abandon the policy. It redesigned it.

In August 2026 Parliament enacted the News Bargaining Incentive package. The mechanism imposes a charge calculated at 2.5 per cent of relevant Australian digital advertising revenue on large covered search and social-media businesses, while allowing eligible payments to Australian news organizations to offset that liability. In other words, a platform does not escape the policy simply by removing news from its service.

The Australian government says the purpose is not mainly to raise revenue but to create enough economic pressure that platforms prefer commercial deals with news publishers to paying the charge. American technology interests have described it as a disguised digital-services tax.

The border between tax policy, competition policy, media policy and sovereignty policy has largely disappeared.

Australia has gone further in regulating social platforms themselves. Since December 2025, major services have been required to take reasonable steps to prevent Australians under 16 from maintaining accounts, with substantial penalties for systemic non-compliance.

One can debate whether that age restriction is wise, proportional or technically workable. But nobody can mistake the underlying constitutional statement: Australian law, not Meta’s terms of service, determines the terms on which Meta may operate in Australia.

That is sovereignty in its most practical form.

And then there is China

China offers the extreme case — and the case democracies should study without romanticizing.

Beijing decided decades ago that the digital sphere was not separate from national sovereignty. It treated it as part of sovereignty.

China’s Cybersecurity Law explicitly invokes “cyberspace sovereignty.” Its Data Security Law says one of its purposes is safeguarding the sovereignty, security and development interests of the state, and it asserts legal reach over some data-processing activity outside China where Chinese national interests are harmed.

Its Personal Information Protection Law requires important classes of data to remain within China and subjects certain transfers abroad to security assessment. Critical information-infrastructure operators and large personal-information processors face localization requirements. China’s Data Security Law also limits the ability of organizations and individuals inside China to provide domestically stored data directly to foreign judicial or law-enforcement authorities without approval from Chinese authorities.

China has built or nurtured domestic substitutes across almost the entire platform layer: Baidu in search and maps; Alibaba in commerce and cloud; Tencent in social networking, payments and messaging; Huawei in communications and computing infrastructure; ByteDance in algorithmic media.

The advantage is unmistakable. Washington cannot make Google alter the principal map used by hundreds of millions of Chinese citizens because Google Maps is not the system on which China depends. An American court cannot simply treat a Chinese state-controlled cloud as another server region operated by a U.S. hyperscaler.

China possesses a degree of technological and jurisdictional autonomy that Canada does not.

But it comes at an enormous price.

The same machinery that prevents foreign corporations from determining China’s information environment gives the Chinese state extraordinary power to determine it instead. Human-rights organizations describe one of the world’s most extensive systems of online censorship and surveillance. Speech is filtered. Platforms police content to state requirements. Digital identity, data and network control can be used not merely to protect a country from external coercion but to discipline the country’s own citizens.

That is not the model Canada should copy.

A sovereign internet is worthless if sovereignty is merely transferred from foreign corporations to an unaccountable domestic state. The democratic goal is harder: digital sovereignty without digital authoritarianism.

Sovereignty does not mean building a Great Firewall around Canada

Canada does not need a Canadian Facebook, Canadian iPhone, Canadian Netflix, Canadian Google and Canadian ChatGPT produced by government decree. Nor should sovereignty be confused with technological autarky.

Canada’s economy depends on international investment, research, standards and trade. American technology has delivered extraordinary benefits to Canadians. AWS and Azure are formidable products. Google Search became dominant in part because users found it useful. Apple built an exceptionally integrated computing ecosystem. American capital and research helped build the modern internet.

The answer is not to pretend none of that happened.

The answer is to recognize that dependency has strategic consequences.

Canada understands that concept perfectly well in energy, defence, food, banking and telecommunications. Nobody argues that because imported electricity may be cheaper on a Tuesday, Canada therefore has no interest in maintaining its own power-generation capability. Nobody suggests that because a foreign bank offers an excellent app, Canada should abandon domestic banking regulation.

Digital infrastructure deserves the same analysis.

The goal should be the ability to say no.

That means government systems whose continuity does not depend upon the political tolerance of another state. Canadian control over the most sensitive public data. Multiple cloud providers and genuine exit routes. Open standards that let institutions move information when a supplier becomes unacceptable. Procurement rules that measure foreign-jurisdiction exposure rather than merely server location. Serious Canadian compute infrastructure. Competition rules capable of preventing gatekeepers from using one monopoly to construct another. Public institutions that do not unknowingly inherit geographic names from a foreign database.

It also means allies.

Canada on its own represents roughly 40 million consumers. Canada joined with Europe, Australia, Japan, South Korea and other democracies represents a market that technology companies cannot casually ignore. Coordinated procurement, interoperability, competition enforcement, privacy standards, cloud-sovereignty requirements and digital taxation would give middle powers leverage that none possesses alone.

The most important thing about “Lake America” is that it is silly

The name is ridiculous.

That is precisely why the episode is useful.

No military secret was exposed. No bank failed. No hospital was shut down. No election was cancelled.

A politician changed an entry in an American government geographic database. A private mapping giant ingested the change. Canadian institutions using foreign mapping infrastructure could then find the American terminology appearing inside their own digital products even though Canadian naming authorities had made no such decision.

The machinery revealed itself because the stakes were low enough for us to notice it.

Imagine exactly the same architecture operating invisibly in areas where the consequences are not funny: public records, artificial intelligence, advertising markets, emergency communications, government cloud storage, app distribution, payment infrastructure, electoral information or national-security data.

Google’s response is defensible as a mapping policy. A global map must reflect local official nomenclature, and the company can reasonably argue that relying on official sources is more consistent than having executives adjudicate every place-name dispute from scratch. Canadian Google users still see Lake Ontario.

That does not make the larger concern disappear.

Indeed, it makes it stronger.

The problem does not require a conspiracy.

It requires only dependency.

A foreign government makes a sovereign decision. A foreign corporation changes its infrastructure to reflect it. Canadian institutions inherit the result because they built themselves on top of that infrastructure.

Nobody needs to telephone Ottawa.

Nobody needs to threaten Ottawa.

Nobody even needs to think about Ottawa.

The dependency does the work.

That is why Lake Ontario matters. Not because Donald Trump can rename it for Canada. He cannot. Not because Google owns it. Google owns no lake.

It matters because the episode exposes the architecture of power underneath everyday digital life.

Canada still possesses sovereignty. But sovereignty that cannot survive a change in somebody else’s database, cloud contract, platform policy, app-store rule, algorithm or foreign law is sovereignty with an asterisk.

The task is not to disconnect Canada from the world. It is to ensure that connection does not become submission.

And the first requirement of that project is simply to recognize the battlefield.

Sometimes it looks like a data centre. Sometimes it looks like a tax statute. Sometimes it looks like an app-store agreement.

And sometimes it looks like a map.
