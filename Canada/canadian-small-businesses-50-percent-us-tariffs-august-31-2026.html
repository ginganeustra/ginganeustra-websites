The 5 per cent problem is a small-business crisis.

Washington’s newest 50 per cent tariffs reach only a minority of Canada’s exports to the United States. But the companies inside that minority are often too small to reroute production, bargain down margins or wait out a trade war.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 2:00 p.m. ET

The headline number can make the new U.S. tariffs sound contained: roughly five per cent of Canadian exports to the United States are directly caught by the latest measures.

For the businesses inside that five per cent, there is nothing small about it.

The Wall Street Journal reported Monday that smaller Canadian exporters of products including honey, artwork, wool, cosmetics and flowers are among the companies most exposed to the new 50 per cent U.S. duties. Nearly 80 per cent of affected exporters surveyed expect revenue losses, according to the Journal, and more than a third expect losses of at least 50 per cent.

That is the difference between a macroeconomic statistic and a company’s survival.

A multinational can move production, finance inventory and absorb a bad quarter. A family exporter selling one specialized product into one nearby market may have none of those options.

The trade war has been dominated by autos, steel, aluminum and national industrial strategy. Those sectors deserve the attention: they employ large workforces and anchor entire regions. But the new tariff list reaches far beyond factories with thousands of workers.

It catches companies whose entire U.S. strategy may consist of a warehouse, a distributor and a customer base built over years. Their advantage was geography and predictable access to the world’s largest consumer market. A 50 per cent border tax can erase that advantage overnight.

There is no easy place to put the tariff

Every tariff forces the same practical question: who pays?

A Canadian exporter can try to raise its U.S. price and pass the duty to the buyer. It can cut its own price and absorb part of the tariff in its margin. The U.S. importer can absorb some of the cost. Or the transaction can simply stop.

For a differentiated product with strong demand, the burden may be shared. For a small company selling into a competitive market, a 50 per cent increase can be fatal to the sale.

Dan Kelly, president of the Canadian Federation of Independent Business, told Buffalo Toronto Public Media that he is hearing from owners whose American customers will not accept a 50 per cent premium and whose Canadian companies cannot swallow the cost themselves. That leaves them directly in the middle of the confrontation.

This is why the tariff’s relatively narrow share of total exports can be misleading. Exposure is not evenly distributed across the Canadian economy.

Small firms have less room to manoeuvre

A large manufacturer can sometimes redesign a supply chain. It can shift a product between plants, negotiate long-term freight contracts, hedge currencies, finance a temporary inventory build or lobby for an exclusion.

Small firms are less likely to have those tools. Diversifying to Europe or Asia may require new distributors, certification, packaging, language support and years of relationship-building. Selling more inside Canada can help, but a domestic market of roughly 40 million people cannot automatically replace access to a U.S. market more than eight times larger.

Even finding a new buyer does not solve the logistics problem. A southern Ontario company can put goods on a truck and reach millions of U.S. consumers in hours. Shipping the same product across an ocean changes freight cost, delivery time, working-capital needs and risk.

That is why “diversify” can be a good national strategy and still be a brutal short-term instruction to an individual business.

The employment effect comes later

The first response to a tariff shock is often not a plant closure. Companies cancel hiring, postpone equipment purchases, cut shifts, reduce advertising and hold more cash. Those decisions are harder to see than a factory gate closing, but together they weaken the economy.

The Journal cited forecasts that unemployment could rise to seven per cent and that tens of thousands of jobs could be lost if the tariffs persist. Those estimates remain forecasts, not outcomes. What is already visible is the uncertainty that causes firms to delay decisions.

That uncertainty can do damage even if a deal eventually removes the tariffs. A company that was planning an expansion this autumn may wait. An American customer may qualify a supplier in another country rather than risk another tariff shock. A Canadian owner nearing retirement may decide not to invest in another five years of growth.

The policy problem

Ottawa has announced billions of dollars in support for affected workers and businesses. That is necessary, but support programs face a difficult design problem: a tariff can destroy revenue faster than government assistance can be approved and delivered.

Loans help a viable company bridge a temporary disruption. They are much less useful if the tariff permanently removes the market that made the company viable in the first place.

The best support therefore has to do more than replace lost cash. It has to help companies find customers, certify products for new markets, finance inventory and survive the transition long enough for diversification to become real.

There is also a political lesson. Canada’s response cannot be judged only by whether GDP remains positive or whether the largest exporters survive. A trade war can be nationally manageable and locally devastating at the same time.

That is what the five per cent figure hides.

The biggest Canadian companies will dominate the negotiations because they dominate trade. But for thousands of smaller exporters, the question is simpler and more immediate: can the company still make a sale after a 50 per cent tariff is added at the border?

For many of them, the answer is no.
