What China is saying about Canada’s trade war with the United States

China’s government is speaking cautiously. Its media, however, is constructing a much larger story: American pressure can drive even its closest allies to defend their independence — and eventually to look elsewhere.

Updated Aug. 28, 2026 · 1:35 a.m. ET

In the space of two days, Chinese coverage of the Canada–U.S. trade confrontation has moved through three distinct registers: an officially neutral call for equal dialogue; state-media reporting that dwells on Canadian resistance; and a louder layer of commentary arguing that Washington is damaging its own alliances. The distinction matters. Beijing has not declared Canada a partner, and nothing in the official record supports saying that it has. But China plainly sees political and strategic value in the story.

The official line: equal footing

The clearest government statement came at the Chinese Foreign Ministry’s Aug. 26 briefing. Asked by Bloomberg about Canada’s retaliatory tariffs, spokesperson Lin Jian gave a single-sentence answer: economic and trade concerns, he said, should be settled through “dialogue and consultation on an equal footing.” The ministry’s official English transcript uses precisely that language.

“China believes that economic and trade concerns should be addressed through dialogue and consultation on an equal footing.”

— Lin Jian, Chinese Foreign Ministry, Aug. 26, 2026

It is formulaic diplomacy, deliberately so. Lin did not praise Ottawa, condemn Washington by name, endorse counter-tariffs or suggest a new Canada–China arrangement. Yet China chose to answer a question about Canada rather than deflect it. And “equal footing” is a pointed principle in a dispute Canada describes as involving both tariffs and the freedom to make sovereign economic choices. Beijing can restate its long-running opposition to economic coercion without formally inserting itself into a fight between two other countries.

State media amplifies the Canadian answer

Chinese outlets have been much less restrained. Xinhua’s Aug. 25 dispatch collected the strongest Canadian political responses: former prime minister Jean Chrétien’s proposal that Canada consider export taxes on energy, oil, gas and potash, and Ontario Premier Doug Ford’s warning that electricity and critical-mineral exports could become leverage if the conflict worsens. These are Canadian politicians’ statements, not Chinese policy proposals. But their prominence in Xinhua matters because they reinforce a simple message to Chinese readers: even the United States’ most integrated ally is prepared to impose costs.

China News, a state-affiliated outlet, then turned the White House’s eagle-and-Canadian-goose social-media post into a story in its own right. Its Aug. 27 article documented the post’s claim that Trump was ending Canadian “free-riding,” then featured online mockery of its imagery. One counter-meme described a docile bird attacked by a more aggressive one, only for the goose to stand up and the hungry eagle to leave confused. Another reinterpreted the animals through U.S. midterm politics.

That is not an official Chinese statement, and the comments themselves were not necessarily Chinese. What China News did was select and amplify an international online response that matched the outlet’s framing. The effect is to turn a piece of White House theatre into evidence of arrogance meeting resistance. It is a familiar information strategy: formal diplomacy stays measured while media reporting gives a sharper emotional form to the same underlying argument.

The bigger narrative: Washington is pushing partners away

The most ambitious claim is emerging in commentary. A Sohu article, published on a platform that carries user-generated material and therefore should not be read as official Chinese policy, argued that U.S. terms had sought to restrict Canada’s ability to make independent agreements with other countries, especially China. It cast the collapse as an act of self-sabotage: Washington wants Canada to help reduce reliance on Chinese critical minerals, the argument goes, yet tariff pressure may make Canada more willing to diversify toward China.

That conclusion is a forecast, not a fact. Canada remains a NATO and Five Eyes member with substantial and unresolved disputes with Beijing on security, foreign interference, human rights and trade. Canada’s diversification strategy is not synonymous with replacing one dependency with another. Still, the Sohu argument exposes what Chinese commentators hope this moment will prove: American economic power does not automatically produce loyalty, and attempts to police a partner’s options can make alternatives more attractive.

Global Times made a related point from the opposite direction on Aug. 27, criticizing U.S. media for treating any Canada–U.S. dispute as a story about whether China is “winning.” That response is revealing. Beijing wants credit for identifying what it says is a pattern of unilateral U.S. pressure, but it also wants to resist the idea that every rupture in the Western alliance system is a Chinese plot. In its preferred telling, Washington creates the friction itself.

What Canadians should take from it

Chinese coverage is useful evidence of international perception, not an instruction manual for Canada. Its sympathy is self-interested: a Canadian refusal to yield strengthens Beijing’s broader case that U.S. tariff coercion has costs beyond China. Nor does media attention amount to a trade offer, a policy shift or a geopolitical realignment.

But the reaction does show that Canada’s fight is being watched far beyond North America. A government that sets out to punish a close ally may discover that every act of resistance becomes an example for other partners and competitors alike. China’s official statement is careful because it needs to preserve room to manoeuvre. Its media is less cautious: it sees Canada’s stand as proof that sovereignty, bargaining leverage and diversification are now part of the same argument.
