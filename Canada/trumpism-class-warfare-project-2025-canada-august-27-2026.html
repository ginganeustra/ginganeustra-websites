The real bedrock of Trumpism is class warfare — and Canada is already in its path

Project 2025 offers a governing theory of concentrated power with consequences for Canadian institutions, social trust and sovereignty.

Published Aug. 27, 2026 · Evening update

← Canada at War front page

Donald Trump’s politics are often described as populism, nationalism, authoritarianism or a culture war. Each label captures something real. But none gets to the centre of it as clearly as class warfare.

Not class warfare in the old, straightforward sense of workers against owners. Trumpism’s achievement is to turn a conflict that is fundamentally about wealth and power into a permanent cultural battle among working- and middle-class people themselves.

The promise is that ordinary Americans will be restored to dignity and power. The practical political method is to direct their anger away from concentrated corporate power, extreme wealth, deregulation, weakened labour rights and a shrinking public sphere — and toward migrants, racial minorities, public servants, universities, teachers, journalists, LGBTQ+ people, environmentalists and any institution that can be called “elite.”

That does not mean every Trump supporter is wealthy, cynical or motivated by hostility. Many are responding to real injuries: wages that failed to keep pace with costs, hollowed-out towns, declining faith in government, unaffordable housing and a sense that powerful institutions no longer listen. Those grievances are real. The question is what politics does with them.

Trumpism offers recognition and belonging, but its governing programme directs the resulting power upward. It seeks lower taxes and fewer constraints for the wealthy and large corporations, while making the state more punitive toward people with less security and fewer political resources. Its culture war is not a sideshow. It is the mechanism that makes this arrangement durable.

The document is Project 2025 — and it is 922 pages

The document is Project 2025, formally called Mandate for Leadership: The Conservative Promise. It is not 2,925 pages; the main volume is 922 pages.

Produced under the leadership of the Heritage Foundation and released in 2023, Project 2025 was prepared as a transition blueprint for the next conservative U.S. administration. Heritage described it as a plan to reshape federal agencies, and it was paired with a personnel database, training programme and a transition playbook.

Its significance is that it is unusually concrete. It does not merely argue for conservative values. It goes department by department through the U.S. government, setting out how power can be centralized in the White House, how civil servants can be replaced or controlled, how regulatory agencies can be redirected, and how public institutions can be forced into a more ideological role.

Donald Trump publicly distanced himself from Project 2025 during the 2024 campaign, and it was not formally his campaign platform. But the distinction should not obscure the overlap. Project 2025 and Trump’s own Agenda47 shared central themes: a more powerful presidency; the removal of officials regarded as disloyal; reduced regulatory power; a hard line on immigration; attacks on diversity, equity and inclusion programmes; a remaking of education; fossil-fuel expansion; and a more openly political use of government.

The important point is not whether every page becomes law. It is that the project provides a governing theory: democratic institutions should not be independent counterweights to political power. They should be made answerable to a leader and an ideological movement.

A politics that turns public institutions into enemies

In a healthy democracy, institutions are imperfect but necessary mediators. Courts, civil services, public broadcasters, universities, professional regulators, independent agencies and public schools can restrain arbitrary power. They can also protect citizens who lack money, connections or media influence.

Trumpism teaches people to regard many of those institutions as enemies.

Sometimes that critique begins from a legitimate observation. Institutions can be complacent, bureaucratic, exclusionary or unaccountable. But Trumpism does not generally seek to democratize them. It seeks to subordinate them.

The civil service becomes a “deep state.” Independent journalism becomes “the enemy of the people.” Universities become ideological factories. Public-health officials become conspirators. Judges who rule against the executive become corrupt. Election administrators become suspect if their count produces the wrong result.

Once that worldview takes hold, the public servant is no longer a fellow citizen performing a professional duty. The teacher, librarian, scientist, reporter or regulator becomes a political combatant. Public life loses the shared ground on which people can disagree without treating one another as enemies.

That is why the cultural element of Trumpism matters so much. A movement that wants to weaken independent institutions needs the public to distrust them first.

The class war hidden inside the culture war

The cultural targets change as needed. One week the focus is immigration; another, crime; another, transgender rights; another, a university protest; another, a school library. But the deeper pattern is remarkably consistent.

Social anxiety is transformed into anger at visible, less powerful targets. The person worried about rent is encouraged to blame a newcomer. The person angry about a hospital wait is encouraged to blame a “woke” bureaucracy. The worker who feels disrespected is invited to focus on a symbolic cultural dispute rather than on the employer, private-equity owner or tax structure that may be shaping their material life.

This is not to say that immigration, public safety, education or gender policy are imaginary issues. They are real matters of public debate. The question is whether they are being debated honestly, or being used to consolidate a political coalition whose economic programme serves the most powerful.

A politics that promises to fight “elites” while weakening organized labour, cutting regulation, reducing taxes for the wealthy and attacking public institutions is not an uprising against the ruling class. It is a reorganization of power within that class, with public anger used as fuel.

The genius of Trumpism is its emotional clarity. It tells people who is to blame. The danger is that it often points downward or sideways, not upward.

Why this matters to Canada

Canada is not the United States. Its constitutional structure, parliamentary system, political culture and public institutions differ. Canadians should not lazily assume that every American political development crosses the border unchanged.

But Canada is unusually exposed to American political weather. The countries share a continent, an economy, media platforms, cultural products, business networks and an increasingly integrated information environment. When American politics becomes more punitive, conspiratorial and hostile to independent institutions, Canada does not remain untouched.

The first impact is economic and institutional pressure.

A U.S. administration that treats trade as leverage rather than as a rules-based relationship can use tariffs, threats and sectoral exemptions to press Canada into policy concessions. That pressure is not only about automobiles, steel, aluminum, dairy or energy. It can reach into procurement, digital policy, environmental regulation, border administration, defence, critical minerals and the broad question of whether Canada retains room to make its own decisions.

A smaller country beside a far larger and more aggressive neighbour can be pushed toward a familiar response: accommodate, deregulate, lower standards, weaken public protections and call it competitiveness.

That is not inevitable. But it is the logic of dependence.

The second impact is political imitation.

Canada already has its own versions of the American culture war: disputes over education, gender, immigration, public broadcasting, climate policy, Indigenous rights, urban crime and trust in government. These debates are real and often difficult. The risk comes when they are imported in American language and American proportions, stripped of Canadian context and used to define fellow citizens as enemies.

Canada’s political traditions have long rested on a more modest premise than American revolutionary individualism: that a large, diverse northern country requires institutions capable of holding people together. Public health care, the CBC, public schools, universities, bilingualism, multicultural citizenship, the Charter, independent elections administration and a professional public service are all imperfect. But they are part of the civic infrastructure that allows a country as regionally divided as Canada to function.

If Canadians come to see all public institutions as corrupt by definition, reform becomes harder and demolition becomes easier.

The third impact is cultural.

American political media is designed for intensity. Its algorithms reward outrage, certainty, humiliation and conflict. Canada’s smaller media ecosystem is economically vulnerable and increasingly dependent on the same platforms. The result is pressure to turn every disagreement into an American-style identity war.

That changes more than politics. It changes how people treat neighbours, teachers, service workers, immigrants, public employees and even family members. It teaches that compromise is betrayal and that social trust is naïveté.

For Canada, social trust is not sentimental. It is strategic.

A country facing economic coercion, supply-chain shocks, defence pressures and a transforming global order needs citizens who can cooperate across region, language, class and background. It needs public institutions strong enough to act, but accountable enough to retain legitimacy. It needs a media culture that can expose failures without convincing the public that nothing is worth defending.

The Canadian test

The right response is not to pretend Canadian institutions have earned blind trust. They have not. Governments fail. Corporations capture regulators. Public systems can be slow and remote. Media organizations can be narrow, concentrated and timid. Universities can be insulated from ordinary life.

But the answer to institutional failure is democratic repair — better transparency, stronger labour power, genuine affordability, anti-monopoly enforcement, accountable public services and independent journalism — not a politics of permanent demolition.

Canada’s danger is not that it will become the United States overnight. It is that it will absorb the habits of Trumpism gradually: contempt for expertise, suspicion of every neutral institution, permanent cultural antagonism and the belief that power should belong to the leader who can hurt the right people.

Project 2025 matters because it gives those habits a governing architecture.

Its lesson is that class warfare does not always arrive carrying a banner for the rich. Sometimes it arrives wrapped in a flag, speaking the language of national renewal, promising to punish cultural enemies and asking ordinary people to surrender the very institutions that give them any protection from concentrated power.

For Canada, the task is not simply to resist American pressure at the border. It is to recognize the same political logic when it appears inside our own culture — and to defend a country where citizenship is stronger than resentment, public institutions serve the public, and national sovereignty means more than choosing which powerful interests get to rule.
