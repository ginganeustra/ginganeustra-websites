Hannah Arendt · Plain-language summary

Eichmann in Jerusalem

What Hannah Arendt meant by the banality of evil, why she held Adolf Eichmann fully responsible and why her account remains fiercely disputed.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · Approximately 2,000 words · Permanent reference article

The trial

In 1961, Hannah Arendt travelled to Jerusalem to observe the trial of Adolf Eichmann.

Eichmann had been an SS officer responsible for organizing the forced deportation of Jewish people across Europe. His office co-ordinated trains, schedules, property seizures, government agencies and police forces that delivered people to ghettos, concentration camps and extermination centres.

Israeli agents captured him in Argentina in 1960 and secretly brought him to Israel. He was tried on charges including crimes against the Jewish people, crimes against humanity and war crimes.

Arendt covered the trial for The New Yorker. Her reports became the 1963 book Eichmann in Jerusalem.

The book created immediate controversy. Arendt did not dispute Eichmann’s guilt. She believed he deserved conviction and execution. The controversy concerned how she described him, how she judged the trial and what she wrote about Jewish leaders forced to operate under Nazi rule.

The man in the glass booth

Eichmann sat inside a bulletproof glass booth.

Arendt expected to encounter a terrifying ideological monster. Instead, she saw a bureaucrat who spoke in clichés, repeated official language and described his career as though he had managed a difficult administrative assignment.

He did not appear demonic. He appeared shallow.

He defended himself by saying he had followed orders, obeyed Nazi law and performed duties assigned by superiors.

Arendt did not accept this defence. She considered his inability to judge his own actions central to his guilt.

Eichmann treated obedience as though it relieved him of moral responsibility. Arendt argued that adults remain responsible for deciding whether an order should be obeyed.

Eichmann’s work

Eichmann was not one of the principal creators of Nazi ideology. He was not Hitler, Heinrich Himmler or Reinhard Heydrich.

He was a specialist in what the regime called Jewish affairs.

Before systematic extermination was adopted, Eichmann worked on schemes designed to force Jews out of German-controlled territory. These policies involved intimidation, dispossession and coerced emigration.

He later became deeply involved in deportation.

His office communicated with ministries, occupied governments, railways, police and SS units. It determined how Jewish populations would be assembled and transported.

This bureaucratic role was essential to mass murder. The extermination system needed timetables, lists, trains, budgets, agreements and administrative co-ordination.

The distance between an office and a gas chamber did not reduce responsibility. Without administrators, the killing system could not have functioned across a continent.

Wannsee and respectable approval

In January 1942, senior Nazi officials met at Wannsee, near Berlin, to co-ordinate the Final Solution. Mass murder was already underway. The conference concerned organization, jurisdiction and co-operation among agencies.

Eichmann prepared material and recorded the official minutes.

Highly educated and respectable officials discussed the destruction of European Jewry as an administrative matter. Their participation reassured Eichmann that the policy had the approval of Germany’s leading authorities.

He could imagine himself as one official among many rather than a participant in an enormous crime.

For Arendt, this did not excuse him. It showed how social approval and bureaucratic procedure could silence moral judgment.

The prosecution’s larger story

The Israeli prosecution wanted the trial to do more than establish Eichmann’s individual guilt.

Prosecutor Gideon Hausner presented a broad history of persecution and the Holocaust. Survivors testified about experiences that did not always involve Eichmann directly.

The trial gave victims an internationally visible forum. This was historically important because the Holocaust had not yet assumed its later place in public memory.

Arendt nevertheless criticized the approach. She believed a criminal trial should concentrate on the defendant’s actions rather than use him to present an entire national history.

Her critics replied that she underestimated the importance of survivor testimony. Both points can be true: the trial established Eichmann’s guilt while becoming a major act of historical testimony.

Jurisdiction

Eichmann’s abduction from Argentina raised legal questions. Argentina protested that Israel had violated its sovereignty. Critics questioned whether an Israeli court could try crimes committed in Europe before Israel existed.

Arendt concluded the trial was legitimate, although she disagreed with parts of its reasoning.

The offences were not ordinary crimes confined to one territory. They were crimes against humanity: an attack on human plurality and the right of different peoples to exist in the world.

Arendt believed Eichmann should be judged because he participated in an attempt to remove an entire people from humanity.

The banality of evil

The phrase “banality of evil” appears mainly at the end of the book but has overshadowed everything else in it.

Arendt’s point was not that Eichmann’s crimes were banal. The crimes were enormous.

What seemed banal was his way of thinking—or his failure to think.

Eichmann relied on clichés and administrative language. He appeared unable to examine events from another person’s point of view. He discussed promotions, responsibilities and obedience while avoiding the human meaning of what he had done.

Arendt called this thoughtlessness.

She did not mean low intelligence. A person can be technically competent and still refuse moral reflection.

Eichmann could arrange complicated transportation operations. What he did not do was stop and ask what those operations meant for people placed on the trains.

The crimes were not banal. Arendt believed the frightening banality lay in Eichmann’s failure to think morally about what he was doing.

If great crimes required uniquely monstrous personalities, society could protect itself by identifying monsters.

But if a conventional careerist could participate in mass murder by conforming, seeking advancement and speaking organizational language, the danger was much wider.

Banality does not mean innocence

Arendt never claimed Eichmann was innocent because he followed orders.

She rejected the idea that obedience erased responsibility. Nor did she say everyone placed in his position would behave as he did.

The book includes people who resisted. One was Anton Schmid, a German army sergeant who helped Jews escape in Lithuania by supplying false papers and transportation. The Nazis executed him.

Arendt used such examples to show that choice remained possible even in terrible circumstances. Resistance might be dangerous and rare, but its existence proved that the system did not mechanically remove responsibility.

Obedience and law

Eichmann claimed he had respected the law. Under Nazi rule, Hitler’s will functioned as the highest legal authority.

Arendt reversed the argument. Blind loyalty to a criminal legal order did not make Eichmann law-abiding in a meaningful moral sense. It showed how completely he had replaced personal judgment with institutional obedience.

Eichmann sometimes described himself using the language of Immanuel Kant, whose ethics emphasize duty. Arendt considered this a grotesque distortion.

Kantian morality requires reasoning about principles that could apply universally. Eichmann converted moral duty into loyalty to the Führer. He kept the language of duty while removing independent judgment.

Different countries made different choices

A large portion of the book examines deportation across Europe.

In some places, national administrations and police co-operated extensively. In others, officials delayed, resisted or frustrated German demands.

Denmark became Arendt’s clearest example. Danish institutions and citizens helped move most of the country’s Jewish population to safety in Sweden.

The response mattered because it demonstrated that Nazi demands were not irresistible. Political resistance could change outcomes.

Arendt also discussed Bulgaria, Italy, Hungary, Romania, France, the Netherlands, Poland and other countries. Some national judgments were compressed and have been challenged by later historians.

Her central point was that local choices mattered. Nazi Germany directed the Holocaust, but implementation depended partly on whether governments, civil servants, police, railways and neighbours co-operated.

The Jewish councils controversy

The most explosive section concerns Jewish councils, commonly called Judenräte.

Nazi authorities forced Jewish community leaders to administer orders inside ghettos. Councils might register residents, distribute food, select workers, collect property and prepare deportation lists.

These leaders operated under terror, incomplete information and threats against their communities and families.

Arendt argued that Nazi use of Jewish administrative structures increased the efficiency of deportation.

She wrote in a tone many readers considered harsh and insufficiently compassionate. Critics accused her of blaming victims.

This remains one of the strongest criticisms of the book.

The Nazis’ exploitation of Jewish institutions is historically important. But council members did not possess freedom comparable to Nazi officials. They did not create extermination policy and could not withdraw without consequences.

Different leaders behaved differently. Some sought advantage. Some tried to delay deportations. Some believed compliance might save part of the community. Some supported resistance. Many lacked reliable knowledge about what deportation meant.

Arendt’s broad treatment could flatten those differences.

Her tone

Arendt could be ironic, impatient and cutting. She criticized the prosecution, witnesses, Jewish organizations and public interpretations of the trial.

Many felt this tone was inappropriate when discussing survivors and Holocaust victims.

Arendt believed emotional solemnity could interfere with judgment. She wanted to analyze rather than simply condemn.

But intellectual distance can distort understanding. Critics argued that her resistance to sentimentality sometimes produced a lack of empathy.

Was Eichmann really banal?

Later research raised serious questions about Arendt’s portrait.

Evidence from Eichmann’s years in Argentina—including the Sassen interviews—showed him speaking more openly among former Nazis. He appeared deeply antisemitic and proud of his role.

Some material was unavailable to Arendt in its later, fuller form. Other evidence of his ideological commitment was available during the trial.

Modern historians often describe Eichmann as both an ideological Nazi and a bureaucratic organizer. These qualities are not mutually exclusive.

A person can hate victims, seek advancement, obey authority and hide behind administrative language at the same time.

This weakens a simple portrait of Eichmann as merely thoughtless. It does not destroy Arendt’s warning about bureaucracy and judgment.

The danger may be that ideological hatred can operate through ordinary offices, procedures and professional ambitions.

The judgment

The court convicted Eichmann. Israel’s Supreme Court rejected his appeal, and he was executed in 1962.

Arendt agreed with the result but supplied her own reasoning.

Eichmann deserved death, she argued, because he supported a policy declaring that Jewish people had no right to share the world with others. By participating in that policy, he made coexistence impossible.

Her reasoning focused on human plurality: the condition that different people and groups inhabit a common world. Genocide attacks that condition itself.

The central lessons

  • Bureaucratic distance does not eliminate personal responsibility.
  • Following orders is not a complete moral defence.
  • Legal authority can become criminal.
  • Social approval can make atrocious policies appear normal.
  • People remain responsible for judging the systems in which they work.
  • Evil does not always announce itself through madness or theatrical hatred.
  • Institutions can convert persecution into routine administrative work.
  • Resistance remains possible, even when dangerous.

Why the book still matters

Eichmann in Jerusalem is not safe or comforting.

Its portrait of Eichmann is disputed. Its discussion of Jewish councils is often insensitive and overly general. Its tone caused real pain among survivors and Jewish intellectuals.

But it asks an essential question: What happens when people treat institutional rules as a substitute for moral judgment?

Arendt’s answer is that civilization cannot rely only on laws, job descriptions or professional obedience. Individuals must think.

Thinking does not mean possessing a high IQ. It means examining one’s actions and attempting to see the world from another person’s position.

The “banality of evil” is not a claim that everyone is secretly an Eichmann. It is a warning that enormous crimes can be administered through familiar weaknesses: conformity, ambition, self-deception, careerism and the desire to avoid responsibility.

Eichmann’s office was part of a killing system. The paperwork did not make the killing less real. The distance did not make him less responsible.
