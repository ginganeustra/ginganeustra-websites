AMERICANS TELL TRUMP: COMPROMISE WITH CANADA

The striking number in a new U.S. poll is not simply that Americans dislike the tariffs. It is that they reject the negotiating theory behind them.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 11:21 a.m. ET

68% want trade-offs. 25% want the hard line.

Donald Trump has presented the confrontation with Canada as an exercise in leverage: use the size of the American market, tariffs and the threat of still more economic pain to force Ottawa to accept Washington’s terms. A new Reuters/Ipsos poll suggests most Americans do not want their government to negotiate with Canada that way.

The national survey found 68 per cent of U.S. adults believe the United States should be willing to make trade-offs with Canada even if Washington does not get most of what it wants. Only 25 per cent said the United States should take a tough stance and insist on getting most of what it wants.

That may be the more consequential finding than the poll’s headline tariff number. On the tariffs themselves, 57 per cent oppose additional U.S. tariffs on Canada, while 20 per cent support them. Another 21 per cent said they did not know.

The poll was conducted by Ipsos from Aug. 28 to 30 using its probability-based KnowledgePanel and included 1,023 U.S. adults. Ipsos describes the sample as nationally representative.

The public also assigns responsibility for the dispute very differently from the Trump administration. Forty-six per cent say the United States bears more responsibility for the current trade dispute. Twelve per cent put more responsibility on Canada, while 18 per cent blame the two countries equally.

There is a pocketbook warning in the results as well. Forty per cent expect the tariffs between Canada and the United States to have a mostly negative effect on their own financial situation. Only four per cent expect a mostly positive effect; 27 per cent expect little effect and 28 per cent are unsure.

For Canada, those numbers matter because Washington’s leverage does not exist in a political vacuum. The administration can impose economic costs on Canada, but the domestic political sustainability of that pressure depends partly on whether Americans believe the confrontation is worth the cost.

The poll does not prove voters will punish Republicans over Canada in November. Nor does it establish that public opposition will change White House policy. Foreign policy and tariff powers can be exercised even when opinion is hostile. But it does show a large gap between the administration’s maximalist posture and the negotiating posture preferred by the public it represents.

The same gap appears in Trump’s symbolic pressure campaign. Sixty-three per cent oppose his executive order renaming Lake Ontario “Lake America”; 14 per cent support it. Opposition includes 84 per cent of Democrats and 65 per cent of independents. Even Republicans are more likely to oppose the renaming than support it, 43 per cent to 33 per cent.

That makes the poll more than another approval-rating story. Canada’s central strategic problem is how much economic and political pressure Washington can sustain while Ottawa refuses terms it considers unacceptable. Reuters and Ipsos have now measured one limit on that power: the American public is not asking Trump to squeeze Canada harder. By nearly three to one, it is asking him to compromise.
