Critical minerals · Industrial sovereignty

Sudbury reaches new nickel ore — and Canada gains another piece of strategic mineral security.

Glencore's nearly $2-billion Onaping Depth project has reached its high-grade nickel-copper ore body, putting the first new mine developed in the Sudbury Basin in more than a decade on the path toward production.

Canada's critical-minerals strategy moved from plans and permitting to something much more tangible Thursday: miners reached the ore.

Glencore Canada marked completion of the shaft and first access to the ore body at its Onaping Depth project at Craig Mine near Sudbury. The high-grade nickel and copper deposit lies almost 2,600 metres underground. Production is expected to begin later in 2026 and the project is designed to extend nickel production in the Sudbury Basin beyond 2040.

The scale matters. Glencore says nearly $2 billion has been invested since construction began in 2019. The federal government says the project generated roughly 7,000 jobs during development, while Sudbury Integrated Nickel Operations already employs more than 1,000 people.

Nickel is now sovereignty infrastructure

Nickel is used in stainless steel and batteries and is increasingly treated by Western governments as a strategic input for advanced manufacturing and defence supply chains. Canada's policy challenge is therefore no longer simply to possess mineral deposits. It is to get mines operating, retain processing capacity and become a dependable supplier to Canadian industry and allied economies.

That is why Ottawa described the milestone explicitly in the language of sovereign critical-minerals production. The project gives Canada a long-lived source of high-grade nickel in one of the country's oldest mining districts at a moment when governments are trying to reduce exposure to concentrated overseas supply chains.

There is also a technological dimension. Onaping Depth is being developed around a fully battery-electric underground fleet, from drilling and extraction equipment to service vehicles. Ottawa previously committed up to $11 million through its industrial decarbonization program.

The importance for Canada at War is straightforward: unlike a proposed mine or an exploration announcement, Onaping Depth has now physically reached the resource. Canada is adding productive capacity in a mineral that sits at the intersection of batteries, industrial manufacturing and national-security supply chains.
