Sovereignty Watch

The American officials exercising U.S. authority inside Canada

For decades, Canada and the United States deliberately built systems that put American border authority on Canadian soil. In a period of political confrontation, arrangements once treated as conveniences deserve a fresh sovereignty audit.

← Canada at War front page

American officials do not merely influence Canada from Washington. Under formal bilateral arrangements, U.S. Customs and Border Protection officers work physically inside Canada, screening travellers and goods bound for the United States before they leave Canadian territory.

Preclearance began in Canada in the 1950s and has expanded substantially. U.S. officers operate at major Canadian airports, and Canada opened new U.S. CBP preclearance facilities at Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport and Vancouver’s Pacific Central rail station in 2026. The system has obvious practical advantages: travellers arrive in the United States already cleared, connections become easier and border processing can be faster.

But the legal powers are real. Canadian legislation permits U.S. preclearance officers, within designated areas and subject to Canadian law, to conduct U.S. immigration and customs examinations. Federal Justice Department material describes powers involving examination, search and detention of documents stored on personal digital devices under prescribed thresholds. U.S. officers can detain a person in circumstances requiring Canadian police intervention, although they do not have Canadian peace-officer status and cannot themselves make a Canadian arrest.

The arrangement is reciprocal. Canadian officers can perform Canadian preclearance functions in the United States, and Canada launched its first land preclearance operation there in 2025. Canadian government documents repeatedly stress that U.S. officers in Canada remain subject to Canadian law, including the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, the Canadian Bill of Rights and the Canadian Human Rights Act.

The sovereignty question is therefore not whether these officials are secretly occupying Canadian territory. They are here because Canada agreed that they could be here. The question is whether agreements designed during an era of exceptional bilateral trust should be reviewed when the political assumptions underlying that trust have changed.

That distinction matters. A serious sovereignty review should not turn lawful co-operation into conspiracy. It should ask whether the authorities granted remain proportionate, whether Canadian oversight is sufficient, whether data collected in Canada is adequately protected, whether reciprocal Canadian powers in the United States genuinely balance the arrangement and whether Canada retains practical freedom to modify or terminate systems that no longer serve its interests.

The broader Canada-U.S. relationship contains many such layers: integrated border enforcement, policing co-operation, intelligence sharing and defence structures. Most were built because integration produced enormous benefits. But integration also creates dependence, and dependence becomes politically important when one partner begins openly using economic leverage against the other.

Sovereignty Watch will examine those arrangements one by one — distinguishing legitimate bilateral co-operation from genuine vulnerabilities, and asking a simple question: in each case, does Canada still possess the effective ability to make and enforce its own choices?

Sources: Public Safety Canada, Justice Canada, Transport Canada and Canada Border Services Agency material on the Preclearance Act and Canada-U.S. preclearance arrangements.
