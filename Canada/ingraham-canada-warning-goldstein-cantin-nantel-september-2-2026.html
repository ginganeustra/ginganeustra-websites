ON INGRAHAM, CANADA BECOMES A WARNING LABEL

Lorrie Goldstein's post moved through Laura Ingraham's social feed; Canadian commentator Élie Cantin-Nantel appeared on her program. The routes were different, but the story about Canada was strikingly similar.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 11:43 a.m. ET

Laura Ingraham did not interview Lorrie Goldstein in the material reviewed for this report. The connection was different: Ingraham's public X feed amplified Goldstein's Sept. 1 post promoting his column on Canadian living standards, presenting it as evidence of the kind of country American Democrats want to emulate. Goldstein's post and Ingraham's framing were public political commentary, not a news report or a finding by an independent American institution.

The following day, the same Fox program took a more direct route. The Ingraham Angle interviewed Canadian commentator Élie Cantin-Nantel under the on-screen premise that Canada had become an “unviable place” to build businesses. Cantin-Nantel said about 10,000 Canadians were relocating to the United States each month.

That number is the point at which the television argument outran the available evidence. Statistics Canada recorded 120,640 emigrants from Canada in 2025—about 10,053 a month. That is an all-destinations count. It does not show that 10,000 people a month moved to the United States, and it does not establish why each person left. The distinction is not semantic: a total-emigration figure cannot, by itself, demonstrate a U.S.-bound monthly flight.

Canada's economic problems are real. Low productivity, affordability pressures, interprovincial barriers and a large annual emigration figure are legitimate subjects for hard reporting and argument. Goldstein's underlying post cited a Fraser Institute report; the report's conclusions can be debated on their merits. What changes when the material crosses the border is its job. A Canadian argument about performance becomes an American partisan warning label: Canada is not a complicated neighbour under pressure, but a simple preview of Democratic failure.

That is a familiar frame on Ingraham's public feed and program. In a separate post, the account accused Prime Minister Mark Carney of sacrificing the Canadian economy to damage Donald Trump and Republican prospects in Maine and Michigan. In January, Ingraham posted that the two countries' economies should be compared again in six months. And in a March 2025 interview carried by the program, Donald Trump called Canada a “51st state” and one of the countries he found hardest to deal with.

Those examples do not prove that Goldstein, Cantin-Nantel, Fox News and Ingraham coordinated their messages. No such evidence is offered here. They do show a repeatable editorial pattern: Canada is cast as a failed progressive experiment, an adversarial trade actor, or a subordinate country whose economic weakness confirms American political claims.

The difference matters at a moment when Washington is using tariffs and political pressure against Canada. A domestic Canadian critique can be sharp, ideological and still be part of normal democratic life. Once it is repackaged for an American audience as proof that Canada deserves to be dismissed, punished or treated as a cautionary tale, it becomes part of the political atmosphere in which policy is made.

There is also a practical lesson for Canadian commentators. Strong criticism survives scrutiny when its numbers are exact about what they measure. The public does not need a falsely specific U.S.-migration claim to understand that Canada has serious economic work to do. And American viewers deserve to know when a figure about people leaving Canada has been turned into a claim about people fleeing to their country.

Canada at War is not alleging disloyalty or unlawful conduct by Goldstein or Cantin-Nantel. This is a documented account of a public chain of framing and amplification. It will publish substantive responses from either commentator, Fox News or Laura Ingraham in full or in relevant part.
