Hannah Arendt · Plain-language summary

The Origins of Totalitarianism

How antisemitism, imperialism, statelessness, propaganda, terror and loneliness combined to make a new form of total domination possible.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · Approximately 2,000 words · Permanent reference article

The question behind the book

Published in 1951, The Origins of Totalitarianism asks how modern societies produced governments capable of trying to control every part of human life.

Hannah Arendt was not writing a conventional history of Nazi Germany or the Soviet Union. She was identifying the political conditions that made those regimes possible.

Her argument is not that antisemitism automatically caused Nazism, or that imperialism inevitably produced concentration camps. She traces forces that developed separately—antisemitism, racism, imperial expansion, statelessness, mass politics, propaganda and social isolation—and explains how they eventually came together.

The book has three large sections: antisemitism, imperialism and totalitarianism.

Arendt’s central claim is that totalitarianism was something new. An ordinary dictator wants obedience and political control. A totalitarian movement seeks to transform society and human nature. It tries to eliminate independent thought, spontaneous action, private loyalties and even the possibility of objective truth.

Antisemitism becomes a political system

Arendt begins by distinguishing modern political antisemitism from older religious hostility toward Jewish people.

Anti-Jewish prejudice had existed in Europe for centuries. Modern antisemitism turned that prejudice into an organized political explanation for national problems.

Jewish emancipation complicated the position of European Jews. They were gradually granted legal rights, but acceptance remained incomplete. Some Jewish financiers had close relationships with monarchies because governments needed lenders who could operate across borders.

These “court Jews” were useful to rulers but remained socially exposed. As monarchies weakened and nation-states developed, Jewish families associated with the old political order could be blamed for the failures of that order.

This produced a dangerous contradiction. Jewish people were gaining formal citizenship while continuing to be treated as outsiders. They were legally included but socially separated.

Arendt argues that this made them vulnerable to conspiracy theories. Their international family and commercial connections were falsely presented as evidence of secret global power.

The reality was far different. Europe’s Jewish communities were politically divided and possessed no unified control over governments or finance. But conspiracy theories do not require accurate descriptions. They require emotionally satisfying explanations.

The Dreyfus affair

Arendt devotes considerable attention to the Dreyfus affair in France.

In 1894, Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish officer in the French army, was falsely convicted of passing military information to Germany. Evidence of his innocence emerged, but military and political authorities resisted admitting the error.

The case divided France. Defenders of Dreyfus demanded evidence and legal justice. His opponents turned the case into a campaign against Jews, intellectuals and the republic.

For Arendt, the affair showed how quickly a legal case could become mass political drama. Facts about one officer mattered less than the emotions attached to his Jewish identity.

Dreyfus became a symbol onto which national humiliation, distrust of republican government and fear of foreign enemies could be transferred.

Imperialism and endless expansion

The second part examines European imperial expansion during the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Arendt argues that imperialism introduced political habits that later returned to Europe in totalitarian form.

Traditional nation-states had defined boundaries. Imperialism demanded continual expansion. European powers sought territories, resources, labour and markets.

This created an alliance between wealth and power. Business interests wanted governments to protect investments abroad. Governments used military and administrative power to support economic expansion.

Arendt describes this as the political emancipation of the bourgeoisie. Business owners who had concentrated on private accumulation began demanding that the state turn expansion into national policy.

The operating principle became expansion for its own sake. A state following that principle could never reach a natural stopping point.

Race and bureaucracy

European empires ruled populations without granting them equal citizenship. They needed a justification for governing people who had never consented to European authority.

Racial ideology supplied it.

Colonized populations were portrayed as inferior or incapable of governing themselves. Europeans could then describe domination as a civilizing duty rather than exploitation.

Bureaucracy became the practical tool. Colonial administrators governed through classifications and decrees rather than democratic participation.

People were converted into categories. Their rights depended on racial identity, administrative status and decisions by distant authorities.

The combination of race and bureaucracy was especially dangerous. Racism supplied the justification for domination. Bureaucracy made domination impersonal and efficient.

The administrator did not need to display personal hatred. He merely processed people according to categories created by the system. This anticipates Arendt’s later analysis of Adolf Eichmann.

Tribal nationalism

Pan-German and Pan-Slavic movements claimed that people sharing a supposed racial or cultural identity belonged together regardless of national borders.

These movements weakened the idea of citizenship based on law. They promoted what Arendt calls tribal nationalism: the belief that political membership comes from blood and ancestry.

Under this theory, a person could be born in a country, live there for generations and still be considered an outsider.

If the nation belonged to an ethnic group rather than all its citizens, minorities could be treated as permanent guests or internal enemies.

Statelessness and the right to have rights

After the First World War, empires collapsed and borders changed. Millions found themselves living in states that did not recognize them as full members.

Declarations said human beings possessed universal rights. Stateless people discovered that rights were difficult to enforce when no government accepted responsibility for protecting them.

Arendt describes the fundamental right as the “right to have rights”—the right to belong to a political community where one’s words and actions matter.

Without citizenship, courts, institutions and political membership, abstract rights could become little more than declarations.

This created populations who existed under police supervision rather than legal protection. Refugees could be detained, confined or deported because they had lost the political status through which ordinary rights were enforced.

Rights described as universal proved dangerously fragile when no political community accepted responsibility for protecting them.

Classes become masses

In the final part, Arendt examines Nazi Germany and Stalin’s Soviet Union. Her comparison is controversial. She does not argue that their histories or ideologies were identical. She argues that they developed a similar form of total domination.

A dictatorship may prohibit opposition while leaving parts of private life intact. A totalitarian regime tries to destroy every independent institution and relationship that might compete with the movement.

Arendt distinguishes a class from a mass. Members of a class recognize common interests. Masses consist of people who no longer feel represented by established parties, classes or institutions.

They are disconnected. They may feel politically invisible, socially unnecessary and personally humiliated. What unites them is resentment toward the existing world and the belief that conventional politics is fraudulent.

Totalitarian movements offer belonging, purpose and a complete explanation for disappointment. The explanation need not be true. It must transform private frustration into membership in a historic mission.

Propaganda builds a fictional world

Totalitarian propaganda does more than circulate separate lies. It organizes lies into a complete system.

Every event is fitted into the movement’s explanation of history. Nazism claimed racial struggle governed human existence. Stalinism claimed scientific knowledge of historical and class development.

Once the ideology is accepted, contradictory evidence can be reinterpreted. If predictions fail, enemies interfered. If policies collapse, saboteurs are responsible. If innocent people confess, the confession supposedly proves the conspiracy was even larger.

The ideology protects itself against reality.

Arendt’s crucial insight is that the ideal subject is not necessarily someone who believes one particular lie. It is someone who has lost confidence in the difference between truth and falsehood.

When people conclude that every source lies and nothing can be known, they become easier to control. They may follow the movement because it supplies certainty, identity and direction.

Terror and the objective enemy

Propaganda attracts and organizes supporters. Terror governs the population after the movement controls the state.

Totalitarian terror is not limited to punishing identifiable opponents. If the government arrested only people who broke known rules, individuals could protect themselves by following those rules.

Under totalitarianism, innocence offers no protection.

The regime identifies “objective enemies”: categories supposedly condemned by racial nature or historical necessity. Nazi Germany treated Jews as enemies because of ancestry. Stalinist terror could designate entire social categories as enemies of historical progress.

The victim need not do anything. Identity becomes the offence.

The camps and total domination

For Arendt, concentration and extermination camps represented the most extreme institution of totalitarian rule.

They were not simply prisons or forced-labour sites. They were laboratories in which the regime attempted to destroy individuality.

Prisoners lost legal identity, social status, privacy and control over their bodies. Arbitrary punishment weakened the expectation that actions would produce understandable consequences.

The purpose was not only physical destruction. It was the destruction of spontaneity: the human capacity to begin something new, make a moral choice or act unpredictably.

Loneliness

Arendt ends by emphasizing loneliness.

Isolation can be political: a person is unable to act with others. Loneliness is deeper: the feeling of no longer sharing a common world with anyone.

A lonely person may distrust personal experience and become desperate for an explanation that makes life coherent.

Totalitarian ideology offers one. It says apparent chaos follows a hidden law and membership in the movement provides access to that law.

The regime creates isolation, but it also feeds on isolation already produced by social breakdown.

The argument in plain language

Arendt’s warnings are connected:

  • Rights are fragile when people lose political membership.
  • Racism permits governments to classify human beings as naturally unequal.
  • Bureaucracy allows participation in cruelty to feel impersonal.
  • Propaganda can replace individual lies with an entire fictional reality.
  • Terror becomes absolute when innocence offers no protection.
  • Social isolation makes people vulnerable to movements promising identity and certainty.
  • Totalitarianism seeks to destroy independent judgment, not merely control conduct.

Criticisms

Some historians argue that Arendt placed Nazi Germany and Stalin’s Soviet Union into one category while underestimating profound differences between them.

Other critics say her historical connections are sometimes suggestive rather than proven. Antisemitism, imperialism and totalitarianism are connected in her account, but the causal path is not always precise.

Her treatment of colonialism also contains language about Africa and African people that is deeply troubling today. Her information about Stalin’s Soviet Union was necessarily incomplete in 1951.

The book should be read as an ambitious work of political interpretation, not a comprehensive modern history.

Its warning remains powerful: free societies become vulnerable when disconnected people stop trusting a shared reality and surrender judgment to a movement claiming to explain everything.

Arendt’s ultimate question was not simply what happened. It was what had to break before totalitarianism became possible.
