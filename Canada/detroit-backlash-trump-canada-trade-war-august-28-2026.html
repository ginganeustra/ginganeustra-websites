Detroit turns on Trump’s Canada trade war

The border economy that helped build the North American auto industry is warning that Washington’s tariff escalation is punishing Michigan along with Canada.

Published Aug. 28, 2026 · Canada at War

DETROIT — The political backlash to Donald Trump’s trade war with Canada is becoming increasingly visible in the American city with perhaps the most to lose from it.

Detroit and Windsor do not function economically like two distant cities separated by an international frontier. Their factories, suppliers and workers are parts of a deeply integrated manufacturing system in which components can cross the border repeatedly before a finished vehicle reaches a showroom. The Guardian reported Friday that about US$1 billion in goods crosses the Detroit River each day. Against that backdrop, local political and economic voices are describing the latest escalation in unusually blunt terms.

The Detroit Regional Chamber warned after the breakdown in Canada-U.S. negotiations that escalating tariffs would inflict economic pain on both sides of the border, particularly Michigan and the Detroit-Windsor region. Chamber president and CEO Sandy K. Baruah said the two economies are “intertwined, interconnected, and stronger together,” and argued that global competitors stand to benefit from a weakened North American trade bloc.

Michigan is paying for the tariff strategy

The political problem for the White House is that the argument is no longer theoretical. The Guardian cited a June Epic-MRA survey finding Michigan residents opposed tariffs on Canadian goods by 63 per cent to 31 per cent, with opposition extending to 35 per cent of Republicans. Nearly three quarters of respondents said tariffs were contributing to higher prices.

The newspaper also reported estimates that Michigan residents are paying more than US$3,200 annually on average because of tariffs — far more than the national average — reflecting the state’s unusually high exposure to cross-border commerce.

Michigan Democrats have seized on that vulnerability. State Senate Majority Leader Winnie Brinks said this week that 50-per-cent tariffs on Canada would raise costs for families and put additional pressure on manufacturers and small businesses. Michigan Democratic Party chair Curtis Hertel warned that the measures could cost jobs and deepen disruption in manufacturing.

But the deeper issue is industrial rather than partisan. Reuters reported Wednesday that automakers had entered the latest negotiations expecting relief from existing U.S. auto tariffs. Instead, Trump announced a 50-per-cent tariff on Canadian vehicles, parts and trucks beginning Jan. 1. The industry had reportedly expected a prospective agreement to lower the top-line tariff on Canadian cars and light trucks from 25 per cent to 15 per cent.

That reversal matters because the Detroit Three are not simply American companies buying foreign Canadian products. General Motors, Ford and Stellantis operate production networks across the border. Toyota and Honda also build extensively in Canada. American-made parts are embedded in Canadian-built vehicles, just as Canadian inputs are embedded in vehicles assembled in the United States.

A political risk in a pivotal state

Michigan also matters because the tariff fight is arriving during a U.S. midterm election campaign in which control of Congress could turn on closely contested races in the state. A policy intended to demonstrate economic strength against Canada is therefore creating a conspicuous pocket of domestic opposition in one of America’s most important manufacturing regions.

The administration’s central argument is that tariffs can force production and jobs back into the United States. But the Detroit-Windsor experience exposes the difficulty of applying that logic to an industry built through decades of continental integration. A tariff on a Canadian component can become a cost imposed on an American factory that needs it.

For Canada, the Detroit reaction is strategically important. Ottawa’s retaliatory measures are designed in part to make the cost of the trade war visible inside the United States. Michigan is evidence that this pressure does not have to create anti-Canadian sentiment. In a region whose prosperity has long depended on Canada, it can instead intensify demands for Washington to restore predictable cross-border trade.

The most striking opposition to the Canada trade war may therefore not come from another foreign capital. It may come from across the river in Detroit.
