Historical snapshot · August 31, 2026

THE EMPIRE IN YOUR POCKET

Archive note: This text snapshot preserves a Canada at War page found in the publication's repository history after the original page was no longer present in the current edition.

This is a clear and harsh example of why Canada — and much of the world — is struggling to maintain real sovereignty under the predatory reach of American power and a technology industry that can behave as though national borders are merely another product setting.

The argument is not that Google has literally annexed Lake Ontario. It has not. Nor did Donald Trump acquire the legal authority to rename a body of water for Canada merely by signing an American executive order.

The important facts are more interesting.

Google says the United States Geographic Names Information System formally changed the American federal name for Lake Ontario to “Lake America,” and Google followed its longstanding Maps policy of reflecting official government sources. American users therefore see “Lake America.” Canadians continue to see “Lake Ontario.” Users elsewhere see both names. That distinction matters. Google did not announce that Trump gets to name Canadian geography.

And yet, within hours, something revealing happened. Canadian websites and applications using third-party mapping infrastructure began showing the American terminology even though no Canadian government had changed the name. Hydro One acknowledged that its outage map was among the affected services. The Canadian state had not moved. The infrastructure underneath Canadian institutions had.

There, in miniature, is the problem.

MapQuest demonstrated that the outcome was not technologically inevitable. It publicly said it would not make the change. Apple Maps was still displaying Lake Ontario on Sunday. Google made one choice. MapQuest made another. Apple, at least at that point, made another.

That is no longer merely a competition-policy question. It is a sovereignty question.

For most of modern history sovereignty had a physical vocabulary: territory, borders, ports, railways, telegraph lines, radio frequencies, banks, armies, currency, postal systems and power stations. Control those things and a state had some reasonable claim to control its own affairs.

The digital state has acquired another layer.

Search determines what can readily be found. Maps determine what places are called and how they are represented. Social networks determine much of what gets distributed. App stores determine what software can economically reach citizens. Cloud companies store government and corporate information. Advertising exchanges finance journalism. Payment systems intermediate commerce. Artificial-intelligence companies are beginning to intermediate knowledge itself.

An extraordinary proportion of that infrastructure belongs to corporations headquartered in one country: the United States.

Alphabet owns Google Search, Google Maps, YouTube, Android and much of the advertising infrastructure of the web. Apple controls one of the two dominant mobile operating systems and its app-distribution channel. Meta owns Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Threads. Microsoft operates Windows, Office, Azure and LinkedIn. Amazon operates AWS. American companies dominate major portions of cloud computing, enterprise software, digital advertising and frontier artificial intelligence.

This does not make every company an extension of Washington. They have different interests, fight American governments and are themselves regulated by American law. But it creates an extraordinary structural fact: a Canadian government can pass a law, a Canadian court can issue a judgment, and Canadian voters can replace Parliament — and none of those acts creates a Canadian Google, Android, AWS or Azure the next morning.

Formal sovereignty and practical autonomy are no longer the same thing.

Canada’s own government now says this openly. Shared Services Canada defines digital sovereignty as Canada staying in control of its data, technology and essential online services rather than relying on foreign companies, foreign systems or foreign laws. Its 2026–27 departmental plan describes digital infrastructure as essential infrastructure comparable in importance to roads, ports and energy networks.

None of this is actually new. The technology changes. The sovereignty argument repeats.

In the 1920s the disruptive technology was radio. Canadian airwaves were increasingly filled with programming originating outside the country. The 1929 Aird Commission concluded that broadcasting was too important to national life to be left entirely to commercial forces and foreign programming. Its work helped produce a national public-broadcasting structure and eventually the CBC.

Radio was the Google of its day: borderless, technically dazzling, popular and capable of making the political boundary seem irrelevant. Canada’s answer was not to ban American radio. It was to ensure that Canadian institutions and Canadian regulatory authority existed beside it.

The next version came with computers. By the 1970s Canadian policy-makers were already discussing transborder data flows and the danger that information about Canadians could move beyond Canadian jurisdiction.

After 9/11, the issue became concrete. British Columbia became embroiled in a dispute over outsourcing health-information processing to companies with American links. The concern was not that U.S. servers were inherently malicious; it was that foreign law can follow the company controlling the information. The federal Privacy Commissioner emphasized the general principle: when personal information moves abroad, it may become subject to the law of the foreign jurisdiction.

Then came the U.S. CLOUD Act in 2018. American law made explicit that, after valid legal process, a provider subject to U.S. jurisdiction can be required to produce data in its possession or control even when the provider stored that data outside the United States. The law contains safeguards and does not give Washington an unlimited licence to seize foreign data. But the jurisdictional principle is the point: storing bits in Toronto does not automatically end American legal exposure if the controlling company remains subject to American jurisdiction.

Europe learned the same lesson through litigation. In 2020 the Court of Justice of the European Union invalidated the EU–U.S. Privacy Shield in the case commonly called Schrems II , finding that European data transferred to the United States did not receive protection equivalent to that demanded by EU law under the then-existing framework.

These disputes are usually filed under privacy. Underneath they are about power: which country’s law follows the data?

Canada is not doing nothing. In 2026 it is doing more than at any time in recent memory. The problem is that the pieces have grown separately — privacy law here, broadcasting rules there, cloud procurement somewhere else — without yet becoming one complete national doctrine.

The federal Digital Sovereignty Framework says the Government of Canada must be capable of managing and protecting its digital systems, data and information regardless of where technology is developed, hosted or supported. But the framework itself acknowledges a limitation: broader Canadian economic, industrial and national-security interests are outside that document’s scope.

Shared Services Canada’s 2026–27 plan moves from theory toward infrastructure. Ottawa is developing sovereign cloud hosting, backup capabilities aligned with Canadian data-residency requirements and a dedicated sovereign private-cloud environment within Canadian jurisdiction. It says future work will expand Canadian cloud services and collaboration with Canadian-based providers. It is also building sovereign AI and compute capability, including a Protected B enterprise generative-AI environment and Canadian hosting options.

That is significant because it changes the procurement question. The question is no longer simply: which cloud gives government the best price and technical features? It becomes: which cloud can the Canadian state continue to operate, control and legally defend if the international political environment deteriorates?

Canada is also asserting authority higher up the stack. The Online Streaming Act , enacted in 2023, brought large online streaming services inside the Canadian broadcasting framework. The Online News Act created a bargaining regime under which Google ultimately agreed to contribute about $100 million annually, indexed to inflation, to Canadian journalism in exchange for an exemption from mandatory bargaining. Meta chose a different route and withdrew news from its principal Canadian platforms.

That episode is a warning against the slogan “just regulate them.” Regulation works best when the state has enough market leverage, alternative infrastructure or allied support that leaving is more expensive to the corporation than complying. Where the platform can credibly walk away, the platform has bargaining power of its own.

The Competition Bureau is meanwhile pursuing Google over alleged abuse of dominance in advertising technology. Those allegations remain before the Competition Tribunal and should not be described as adjudicated facts.

The privacy architecture is being rebuilt as well. Bill C-36 , introduced June 15, 2026 and still at second reading, would enact the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act and replace the private-sector privacy rules now contained in Part 1 of PIPEDA. The government says the existing framework is more than 25 years old and predates AI at scale, deepfakes and modern algorithmic decision-making.

Bill C-34 , the Safe Social Media Act, is also at second reading. It would enact a Digital Safety Act and establish a Digital Safety Commission with authority over regulated online services.

Those are substantial measures. But the weakness remains obvious: Canada is regulating foreign infrastructure faster than it is creating Canadian alternatives to it.

Nothing demonstrates the imbalance more clearly than the Digital Services Tax.

The logic was straightforward. Large corporations can earn enormous sums from Canadian users, Canadian advertising, Canadian marketplaces and Canadian data while the conventional international corporate-tax system does not always allocate taxable profit to Canada in proportion to that activity.

Canada’s DST imposed a three-per-cent tax on certain revenues from online marketplaces, targeted advertising, social media and specified sales or licensing of user data. It was aimed at very large businesses: generally groups meeting a €750-million global revenue threshold and more than C$20 million in covered Canadian revenue.

Finance Canada projected $2.3 billion in the first fiscal year, reflecting the tax’s retroactive reach to 2022, followed by about $900 million annually in each of the next four years.

Then Washington objected.

On June 29, 2025, the Carney government announced that Canada would rescind the tax “in support of negotiations on a new economic and security partnership with the United States.” The collection scheduled for the next day was halted.

The surrender was later written into law. Bill C-15 received royal assent March 26, 2026. It repealed the Digital Services Tax Act retroactively to June 20, 2024 and required refunds, with interest, of DST payments already received. Finance Canada’s own briefing language summarized the legal effect with unusual clarity: it would be as if the DST had never existed.

There are legitimate arguments against unilateral digital-services taxes. They can create double-taxation concerns, become trade irritants and complicate the OECD effort to produce a multilateral system for allocating taxing rights to market jurisdictions. The point here is not that the DST was economically perfect.

The point is sovereignty in practice. A state’s freedom to legislate depends partly on its ability to withstand retaliation from states with greater market power.

Europe has many of the same dependencies as Canada. It does not possess a European Google, Meta, Amazon or Microsoft of comparable global scale. Its answer has been to convert the size of its single market into regulatory power.

The Digital Services Act imposes transparency, safety and systemic-risk obligations on major online intermediaries. The Digital Markets Act goes directly after gatekeeper power by placing special obligations on the largest platform companies and services.

The identity of those gatekeepers tells the story: Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, Microsoft and Booking are among the designated firms. Google Maps itself is treated as a core platform service in the DMA system. Europe is therefore not pretending a mapping platform is merely a neutral website. It is treating it as infrastructure wielded by a gatekeeper.

On July 23, 2026, the European Commission fined Google a combined €890 million under the DMA: €460 million over self-preferencing on Google Search and €430 million over restrictions on steering users away from Google Play toward alternative purchase channels. Google can challenge enforcement decisions, but the scale of the penalty demonstrates the difference between European and Canadian regulatory weight.

Europe is now moving much deeper into infrastructure. In April the Commission awarded sovereign-cloud procurement contracts worth up to €180 million for EU institutions. Its Cloud Sovereignty Framework scores providers not just on cybersecurity but on legal and jurisdictional exposure, data and AI control, technological autonomy, supply chains and operational sovereignty.

Then in June the Commission proposed a broader European Technological Sovereignty Package covering semiconductors, AI, cloud and open-source software. The proposed Cloud and AI Development Act would create an EU-wide framework for assessing cloud and AI sovereignty and is explicitly designed to reduce dependence on suppliers outside Europe.

The distinction from Canada is not that Brussels has discovered some magic technology policy. It is scale. A bloc of roughly 450 million people can impose a cost on withdrawal that Canada alone cannot reproduce.

Australia offers another model. Canberra has not built a Chinese-style domestic digital ecosystem and it does not possess Europe’s huge supranational market. Instead it has repeatedly tried to make access to Australia conditional on accepting Australian rules.

Its original news bargaining regime became famous in 2021 when Facebook temporarily shut news sharing in the country during the dispute. The weakness in the original system became obvious when a platform could reduce or remove news and thereby weaken the bargaining mechanism.

Australia did not abandon the project. It redesigned it.

In August 2026 Parliament passed a package creating the News Bargaining Incentive . The News Media Bargaining Charge Act received assent Aug. 26. The package applies a charge to covered digital platforms based on Australian digital advertising revenue and allows qualifying payments to Australian news organizations to offset liability, giving platforms an incentive to strike deals rather than simply withdraw from news.

There is an important late legislative detail. The government introduced the charge at 2.5 per cent. During House consideration on Aug. 19 the rate was amended upward to 2.75 per cent , and that was the rate carried into the final legislation. The final mechanism is therefore tougher than the version initially announced earlier in August.

Australia has gone further with social media. Since Dec. 10, 2025, major platforms including Facebook, Instagram, Reddit, Snapchat, Threads, TikTok, Twitch, X and YouTube have been required to take reasonable steps to prevent Australians under 16 from holding accounts. Systemic non-compliance can expose platforms to penalties of up to A$54.6 million.

One can debate whether the age law is wise, proportionate or technically workable. The sovereignty statement is still unmistakable: Australian law, not a Silicon Valley terms-of-service document, determines the conditions on which a platform operates in Australia.

China is the extreme case — and the case democracies should study without romanticizing.

Beijing decided long ago that cyberspace was not separate from national sovereignty. It treated cyberspace as an element of sovereignty.

China’s Cybersecurity Law explicitly invokes cyberspace sovereignty. Its Data Security Law ties data governance to national sovereignty, security and development interests. Its Personal Information Protection Law and data-export system impose localization and security-assessment requirements on important categories of information and on specified organizations handling large quantities of personal information.

China also built or nurtured domestic substitutes across much of the stack: Baidu in search and mapping, Alibaba in commerce and cloud, Tencent in messaging and payments, Huawei in communications infrastructure and computing, and ByteDance in algorithmic media.

The strategic advantage is obvious. Washington cannot make Google alter the principal map used by hundreds of millions of Chinese citizens because Google Maps is not the system on which China depends. China possesses a degree of technological and jurisdictional autonomy that Canada does not.

But it comes at an enormous democratic price. The same machinery that prevents foreign corporations from determining China’s information environment gives the Chinese state extraordinary power to determine that environment itself. Censorship, surveillance and state control are not side effects of the Chinese model. They are central features of it.

That is not a model Canada should copy. A sovereign internet is not an achievement if sovereignty has merely been transferred from foreign corporations to an unaccountable domestic state.

The democratic objective is harder: digital sovereignty without digital authoritarianism.

Canada does not need a government-created Canadian Facebook, Canadian iPhone, Canadian Netflix, Canadian Google and Canadian AI model for every task. Nor should sovereignty be confused with technological autarky.

American technology has delivered extraordinary benefits to Canadians. AWS and Azure are formidable products. Google became dominant in part because people found it extraordinarily useful. Apple built an exceptionally integrated computing ecosystem. American capital and research helped build the modern internet.

The answer is not to pretend those gains never happened.

The answer is to recognize that dependency has strategic consequences.

Canada already understands this in energy, defence, banking, food, telecommunications and transport. Nobody argues that because imported electricity can be cheap, Canada therefore has no strategic interest in maintaining generation capability. Nobody argues that because a foreign bank offers a beautiful app, Canadian banking law should be optional.

Digital infrastructure deserves the same analysis.

The goal should be the ability to say no.

It also means allies. Canada alone represents about 40 million consumers. Canada acting with Europe, Australia, Japan, South Korea and other democracies represents a market that even the largest technology companies cannot casually ignore. Digital sovereignty may therefore depend less on retreating behind national borders than on democratic states creating interoperable rules and credible alternatives together.

The name is ridiculous. That is precisely why the episode is useful.

No military secret was exposed. No bank failed. No hospital was shut down. No election was cancelled.

An American president changed the entry in an American government geographic database. A private mapping giant ingested the change. Canadian institutions using that infrastructure began inheriting the result even though the Canadian state had made no comparable decision.

The machinery revealed itself because the stakes were low enough for us to notice it.

Imagine the same architecture operating invisibly in areas where the consequences are not funny: public records, emergency communications, artificial intelligence, government cloud storage, app distribution, payment infrastructure, electoral information or national-security data.

Google’s defence is coherent. A global map must resolve competing official names, disputed boundaries, languages and local usage. Using government geographic databases is a defensible product rule. Canadian Google users still see Lake Ontario.

That does not make the larger concern disappear. It makes it stronger.

The problem does not require a conspiracy. It requires dependency.

That is why Lake Ontario matters.

Not because Trump can rename it for Canada. He cannot.

Not because Google owns it. Google owns no lake.

It matters because the episode exposes the architecture of power underneath ordinary digital life.

Canada still possesses sovereignty. But sovereignty that cannot survive a change in somebody else’s database, cloud contract, platform policy, app-store rule, algorithm or foreign law is sovereignty with an asterisk.

The task is not to disconnect Canada from the world. It is to ensure that connection does not become submission.

And the first requirement of that project is simply to recognize the battlefield.

Sometimes it looks like a data centre.

Sometimes it looks like a tax statute.

Sometimes it looks like an app-store agreement.

And sometimes it looks like a map.
