Historical snapshot · August 31, 2026

The rules already existed. Canada and the United States wrote them in 1989.

Archive note: This text snapshot preserves a Canada at War page found in the publication's repository history after the original page was no longer present in the current edition.

Long before Donald Trump ordered the U.S. federal government to call Lake Ontario “Lake America,” Canada and the United States had already worked out a formal process for dealing with the names of geographic features that cross their common border.

That process was not improvised this week. It was developed over years by the two countries’ national geographic-names authorities and set down in a 1989 document called Treatment of Names of Geographical Features Shared by Canada and the United States .

The document matters because Lake Ontario is not an ordinary domestic feature. It is a transboundary lake. Its northern shore is Canadian, its southern and eastern shore is American, and its international status is governed by two sovereign countries. The 1989 arrangement was written for exactly that kind of geography.

The history is surprisingly practical. In 1984 the U.S. Board on Geographic Names created a Subcommittee on Boundary Names and began working with Canadian officials on a common problem: what should governments do when a lake, river, creek, mountain or other physical feature crosses the border but the two countries use different names or spellings?

Representatives from both countries met near Barrie, Ontario, in November 1984. Canadian participants then pushed back against a simple “one feature, one name” philosophy. They argued that standardization should not erase cultural history or legitimate local usage. If communities on opposite sides of the border had evolved different names for the same place, that difference could itself be part of the geography.

After several years of work, the chairs of the two national naming authorities signed the document of understanding in August 1989. It was later submitted through the United States–Canada division of the United Nations geographical-names process.

This is not a dead historical curiosity. The current policy manual of the U.S. Board on Geographic Names reproduces the Canada–U.S. arrangement. Congressional Research Service analysis of federal naming policy says that for features crossing the Canadian border, the Board’s domestic-name policy requires coordination with the appropriate foreign naming authority pursuant to the 1989 agreement.

That wording is important. It means the normal American system itself distinguishes a domestic naming dispute from a transboundary one.

The board’s ordinary domestic process is also deliberately slow. It researches proposals, seeks views from state, local, tribal and land-management authorities and normally takes at least six months. For a Canadian border feature, another layer is added: coordination with Canada.

Trump’s Aug. 27 executive order did something different. It directed the secretary of the interior, in coordination with the Board on Geographic Names, to change the U.S. federal name and update the Geographic Names Information System. The order does not say Canada agreed. It does not say the 1989 process was followed. And it does not purport to change Canada’s official name.

The agreement does not give Canada a veto over what the U.S. federal government prints on every federal map. Nor does it create a world court for place names. It is a co-operative framework between naming authorities, not a treaty that redraws sovereignty.

But it does establish the normal rules of the road. The striking fact in the Lake Ontario controversy is that those rules were designed around consultation, cultural history, local usage and bilateral coordination—the opposite of the impression created by a unilateral executive announcement.

That is why the legal and administrative question should be framed narrowly. The issue is not whether Trump can order his own executive branch to use a different label. He can exercise substantial control over federal nomenclature. The sharper question is whether the Board on Geographic Names and Interior followed, bypassed or modified the standing transboundary process that the United States itself has maintained for decades.

That is a factual question deserving a factual answer from the two naming authorities.

The 1989 process also reflects a principle that is easy to lose in a political argument: geographic names are not the same thing as sovereignty.

Canada can officially call a shared feature one thing and the United States another. The agreement expressly recognizes that possibility. What it tries to prevent is governments behaving as if one country’s administrative choice automatically determines the other country’s map.

The Canadian and American naming systems have co-operated successfully under that approach. A later United Nations report cited the approval of the name Salish Sea as an example of national, federal, provincial and state naming authorities working together “in the spirit” of the 1989 understanding.

Lake Ontario presents the reverse case. Canada has not changed the name. Ontario has not changed the name. New York State has rejected the federal rename. Yet the U.S. federal database now says Lake America, and Google has chosen to transmit that name to its American users.

The argument therefore is not really about whether a president can make a symbolic proclamation. It is about what happens when the established machinery for handling a shared geographic reality is overtaken by a unilateral federal decision—and then amplified by a private mapping platform used by billions of people.

The rules were already there. The next question is whether they were followed.
