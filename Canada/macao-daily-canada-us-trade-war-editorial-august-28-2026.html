Macao Daily sees a turning point in Canada’s trade war with the United States

The newspaper’s Aug. 28 editorial portrays Ottawa’s counter-tariffs as the moment Canada concluded that continued concessions would not buy respect — and that a much longer economic reorientation may now be under way.

Published Aug. 28, 2026 · 1:52 a.m. ET

“The U.S.–Canada trade war continues to heat up” is the blunt title of a Macao Daily editorial published Aug. 28. The piece is not a neutral dispatch. It is a political argument, written in the vivid, accusatory language common to Chinese-language commentary on the rupture. But it is also valuable evidence of how a newspaper in China’s wider media sphere is interpreting Canada’s confrontation with Washington: not as a routine tariff quarrel, but as a lesson in the limits of accommodation.

The editorial begins with the immediate measure: Canada’s announced retaliation against roughly US$20 billion in American goods, with tariffs reaching 50 per cent. It calls the move fast and forceful, then works backwards through the negotiations to explain why, in its view, Ottawa had reached that point.

Its central argument: yielding did not end the pressure

Macao Daily’s essential story is that Canada spent more than a year attempting to preserve a workable North American trade arrangement, only to find that every concession invited a further demand. The editorial describes a United States that bargained from strength, raised its asks during negotiations and treated tariff threats not as a last resort but as a tool for extracting economic and political advantages.

That account is the editorial’s interpretation, not a substitute for a published negotiating record. No complete authenticated term sheet from the failed talks has emerged publicly. The paper’s language should therefore be read as argument: it says Canada made repeated compromises in hopes of protecting the continental trade relationship, and then discovered that compromise had not produced a stable deal.

The editorial’s thesis: Canada did not turn confrontational because it rejected negotiation; it turned confrontational because it concluded that yielding could not secure a respectful or durable settlement.

It places the escalation on a timeline beginning in July, when it says Washington used tariff threats against a broad list of Canadian products while allowing short delays for talks to continue. The paper’s account then has the negotiations collapsing late on Aug. 21, Canada recalling its delegation and the 50-per-cent U.S. tariff taking effect the following day. It notes that Ottawa initially announced reciprocal action with a period before implementation, a detail the editorial treats as evidence that Canada had not immediately abandoned the possibility of a negotiated exit.

From tariffs to industrial pressure

The sharpest part of the editorial is its reading of the political purpose behind the dispute. It characterizes the U.S. position as using access to the American market to pressure Canadian firms into moving production south. In the newspaper’s terms, that is not ordinary trade competition: it is an attempt to weaken Canadian manufacturing capacity while demanding market access on American terms.

That framing aligns with a concern now widely present in Canadian debate. The danger in an asymmetric continental economy is not only the tariff paid at the border. It is the investment decision made years earlier, when a company concludes it must locate future production inside the United States to avoid political risk. The editorial does not offer independent evidence that this was the explicit aim of every U.S. demand. Its contribution is to articulate how the threat is being understood abroad: tariffs can operate as industrial-location policy, especially when paired with public invitations for Canadian factories to relocate.

Why the product list matters

Macao Daily pays unusual attention to the design of Canada’s retaliation. It says the package covers more than 700 American products in three rate bands and highlights items such as dairy products, appliances, agricultural equipment and pulp. The editorial argues that the list was selected with political geography in mind, concentrating pressure on agricultural and manufacturing states, including Ohio, Michigan and Pennsylvania.

The larger point is straightforward. Counter-tariffs are not merely a revenue measure. A government can choose products whose producers, workers and local politicians are positioned to demand a change in policy from Washington. The Macao editorial sees Canada trying to turn its economic leverage into U.S. domestic political pressure ahead of the midterm elections. Whether the package succeeds will depend on supply chains, substitution possibilities and the willingness of affected American industries to lobby — none of which the editorial can settle. But its reading correctly identifies the political logic that often shapes a targeted retaliation list.

Three futures, in the paper’s view

The editorial lays out three possible paths. The first is direct escalation: a tariff spiral in which both economies suffer. The second is a conditional compromise, in which a deal becomes possible but only if there is genuine room for a settlement. The third is a long-term restructuring, with Canada accelerating diversification and the United States losing some of its ability to set the terms of the North American economic relationship.

That third possibility is where the piece reaches beyond the next round of tariffs. It presents the crisis as a test of whether Canada can reduce its exposure without pretending that the U.S. market can be replaced overnight. Diversification in this sense means more export capacity, more trade arrangements and more domestic industrial resilience — not an abrupt departure from a deeply integrated continental economy.

The conclusion is both sympathetic to Canada and cautionary. Trade wars, it says, have no winners. Yet it argues that for Canada, continued concession would leave industries at a permanent disadvantage; for the wider world, the episode tests whether countries can maintain a rules-based trading system and a principled view of sovereignty when faced with pressure from a much larger economy.

What this tells us — and what it does not

Macao Daily is offering a clear political narrative, not a detached Canadian assessment. Its vocabulary is sharper than Ottawa’s, and some assertions about the negotiations cannot be independently verified without the missing deal text. That limitation should remain visible.

Still, the editorial is notable for the way it connects several elements that are often discussed separately in Canada: tariff retaliation, the location of manufacturing, U.S. electoral politics, export diversification and sovereignty. Its central warning is that a country can preserve commercial access in the short term yet lose bargaining power and industrial capacity in the long term. That is why it treats the present confrontation as something bigger than a disagreement over duties. The question, in the paper’s telling, is whether Canada can insist that partnership cannot mean permanent vulnerability.
