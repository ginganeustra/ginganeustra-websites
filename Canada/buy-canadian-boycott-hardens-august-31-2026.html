The boycott is becoming a habit.

Canadians are still checking labels, avoiding U.S. travel and keeping American alcohol off provincial shelves. What began as a burst of anger is showing signs of becoming ordinary consumer behaviour.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 2:00 p.m. ET

A consumer boycott matters differently from a tariff.

A tariff is written into law, applied at the border and measured by customs data. A boycott is decentralized. It exists in millions of small decisions: which pasta sauce goes into a shopping cart, whether a family books Florida or Halifax, whether a provincial liquor board puts American bourbon back on a shelf.

That makes it harder to measure — and potentially harder to reverse.

Reporting Monday from Buffalo Toronto Public Media says the renewed Buy Canadian push is continuing more than a week after the collapse of the trade talks. Shoppers are checking country-of-origin labels more carefully, some are returning U.S. goods bought by mistake, provincial leaders are maintaining bans on American alcohol, and Canadians are again cancelling or avoiding travel to the United States.

Business Insider separately reported on Canadians trying to build an almost “America-free” pattern of consumption. The experiment exposes both the reach of U.S. products into everyday Canadian life and the limits of substitution: technology, winter produce and some branded goods are difficult or expensive to replace.

The important shift is psychological. A boycott becomes more durable when the decision stops feeling like a protest and starts feeling like routine.

Canada has seen waves of Buy Canadian sentiment before. What is unusual now is the breadth of the trigger. The dispute is not confined to one commodity or one trade ruling. It has become connected to tariffs, sovereignty, language and culture, industrial policy, Trump’s attacks on Canadian leaders and the attempt to rename Lake Ontario.

That gives consumers more than one reason to keep participating.

The shelves are political

Grocery-store behaviour is the clearest everyday expression of the boycott. Canadian shoppers are looking more closely at labels and origin claims, and retailers have responded by making Canadian provenance more visible.

But origin is complicated. A familiar Canadian brand may be owned by an American company. A product assembled in Canada may rely on U.S. ingredients. A Canadian-owned company may manufacture abroad. Business Insider highlighted the problem of “maple washing,” where branding suggests a stronger Canadian connection than the product actually has.

That means the boycott is forcing consumers to decide what “Canadian” means: ownership, manufacturing location, ingredients, employment or simply where the economic benefit lands.

There is no single correct answer. The important point is that consumers are asking the question at all.

Alcohol is still symbolic leverage

American alcohol remains one of the most visible targets because provincial governments control major parts of the retail system. Removing U.S. wine and spirits from government-controlled shelves turned a consumer preference into an institutional boycott.

During the trade negotiations, Washington wanted those restrictions lifted. Ottawa indicated it would encourage provinces to restore U.S. alcohol in exchange for a satisfactory deal. The talks collapsed instead.

Now premiers are signalling that American products will not automatically return. That matters economically to producers on both sides of the border, but it matters even more politically: putting the bottles back would be read as a visible normalization of the relationship.

Travel may be the deeper wound

Travel decisions are harder to reverse than a grocery purchase. A consumer can buy a Canadian cereal this week and an American one next week. A family that replaces an annual U.S. vacation with a Canadian or European trip may discover a new habit, new loyalty points and new expectations.

Border communities, airlines, hotels and tourist destinations therefore have reason to worry about persistence rather than just the initial decline.

Some Canadians quoted in current reporting say they do not know when they will return to the United States. That is not the same as a permanent national shift, and anecdotal reports should not be mistaken for comprehensive travel data. But it shows how political resentment can become a long-term economic preference.

The boycott has limits

An America-free life is not realistic for most Canadians. The two economies are too integrated. Canadian hospitals, farms, factories, software systems, stores and households depend on U.S. goods and services that cannot be replaced quickly or cheaply.

There is also an affordability problem. Buying Canadian can cost more, especially when domestic production is smaller or seasonal. A boycott that becomes a test of household sacrifice will be harder for lower-income consumers to sustain.

That does not make the movement irrelevant. Boycotts do not have to eliminate every American purchase to change incentives. A modest but persistent shift in discretionary spending can affect brands, tourism and political lobbying.

American companies that lose Canadian sales have a reason to tell Washington that the trade confrontation is costing them. Canadian companies that gain customers have a reason to invest in capacity. Retailers have a reason to source differently.

From reaction to structure

The strategic question is whether governments and businesses treat the boycott as temporary anger or as evidence of a more durable reorientation.

If Canadian demand for domestic goods lasts, companies may respond with new manufacturing, packaging, distribution and marketing. If U.S. travel remains weak, Canadian destinations may capture spending that previously left the country. If provincial alcohol bans persist, importers and restaurants may build new supplier relationships.

Those adjustments make the original behaviour harder to reverse because they create alternatives.

That is why the boycott deserves to be tracked alongside tariffs and formal negotiations. Trade policy changes prices by law. Consumer nationalism changes markets by preference.

Canada’s confrontation with the United States is now doing both.
