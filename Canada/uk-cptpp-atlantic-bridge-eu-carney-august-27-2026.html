Britain may be the hinge in Carney’s Atlantic Bridge.

On September 1, the UK finally enters the CPTPP relationship with Canada. It looks like a technical trade milestone. In Carney’s strategy, it could be something much larger: a working connection between the Pacific trade bloc and a future deeper relationship with Europe.

Published Aug. 27, 2026

← Canada at War front page

Five days from now, a quiet change in the architecture of Canadian trade takes effect. Britain’s accession to the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, or CPTPP, enters into force with Canada on September 1.

The United Kingdom has already been operating under CPTPP with most members since late 2024 and with Mexico since June. Canada completed ratification in July. From September 1, Canadian and British businesses will be able to choose between the existing bilateral Canada-UK Trade Continuity Agreement and CPTPP rules, depending on which arrangement works better for a particular transaction.

That choice matters. The existing bilateral agreement already eliminates tariffs on 99 per cent of UK tariff lines, so this is not principally a story about another dramatic round of tariff cuts. CPTPP adds a second legal route into the British market and creates opportunities the bilateral agreement does not provide.

What Canadians actually gain

Global Affairs Canada says Britain’s CPTPP commitments add duty-free quota volumes for some Canadian meat products, immediate duty-free and quota-free access for sweetcorn, and preferential treatment for poultry and eggs. The rules-of-origin choice can also make it easier for Canadian businesses to construct supply chains across CPTPP countries and still qualify for preferential treatment.

Services and mobility may prove at least as important. Canadian business visitors can stay in Britain for up to 90 days in a 12-month period under relevant commitments; investors can receive stays of up to a year to establish or manage investments; spouses of intra-company transferees receive work access; and some highly skilled Canadian professionals can remain for 12 months rather than six. Financial-services firms gain additional cross-border rights in areas including portfolio-management advice and electronic payments. Canadian suppliers also gain guaranteed procurement access across levels of British government under CPTPP commitments layered on top of existing WTO and bilateral arrangements.

None of that by itself revolutionizes a Canada-UK relationship that was already unusually open. Its strategic importance comes from the network around it.

The first European member of a Pacific bloc

The CPTPP was conceived as a Pacific trade architecture. Its membership includes Canada, Japan, Australia, New Zealand, Mexico, Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, Chile and Peru, with Costa Rica moving through accession. Britain is geographically the anomaly — and strategically the bridge.

Prime Minister Mark Carney has explicitly said Canada is championing a connection between the CPTPP and the European Union. In his June “Atlantic Bridge” speech at Trinity College Dublin, Carney said such a link could create a trading bloc of 1.5 billion people. His February discussion with British Prime Minister Keir Starmer also specifically covered ongoing discussions between the EU and CPTPP.

This is the bigger idea: Canada is not simply trying to replace lost American commerce with a collection of bilateral deals. Carney is trying to connect existing blocs so middle powers collectively have an economic scale that none possesses alone.

Britain occupies a unique position in that project. It is now inside CPTPP but remains economically, geographically and institutionally tied to Europe. The UK is outside the EU customs union and Single Market, yet its largest trading relationships remain European and the Starmer government has sought closer practical co-operation with Brussels. Britain can therefore function as a demonstration that European economies can participate in the Pacific trade architecture without becoming Pacific countries.

What an EU-CPTPP bridge might mean

There is no signed EU-CPTPP mega-agreement, and Carney’s “bridge” should not be mistaken for one. The concept could develop incrementally: regulatory co-operation, compatible rules of origin, digital-trade standards, critical-mineral supply chains, energy security, investment, procurement and sectoral agreements that allow firms to operate across both networks with fewer barriers.

The strategic potential is enormous. CPTPP members include major advanced economies and important emerging Asian markets. The EU is one of the world’s largest economic blocs. Linking them would create a rules-based economic counterweight at a moment when Washington is increasingly willing to condition access to the American market on political and industrial concessions.

Canada is unusually well positioned to push the idea because it already has preferential trade agreements spanning both systems. CETA gives Canadian firms extensive access to the EU. CPTPP connects Canada across the Pacific. The UK now sits with Canada inside CPTPP while maintaining its own dense European connections. Rather than building an entirely new structure from nothing, Ottawa can try to make existing structures interoperate.

Defence is already showing how this can work

Trade is only one side of Carney’s strategy. Canada became the first non-European country formally admitted to participation in the EU’s SAFE defence-procurement instrument. Canadian companies and Canadian-origin products can participate in qualifying common procurement. Canada has already announced a Polish Cyber Command procurement of Canadian-built tactical radios through SAFE.

That precedent is important. Europe has demonstrated that it is willing to open a strategic industrial instrument beyond the continent when political trust and mutual benefit justify it. Canada is simultaneously working with Britain on critical minerals, digital trade, sovereign AI infrastructure and proposals for new defence-financing mechanisms.

The emerging model is therefore broader than “free trade.” It links markets, industrial policy, defence production, critical minerals, technology and security. That is precisely the terrain on which the Canada-U.S. confrontation is now being fought.

Why September 1 matters

The entry into force of UK-CPTPP relations with Canada is not a response invented after the latest breakdown with Washington. The accession process predates the current crisis. But timing changes strategic meaning. A trade architecture constructed over years is becoming operational just as Canada is urgently searching for ways to make economic diversification real.

Canada will remain deeply tied to the United States. Neither Britain, Europe nor the Pacific can duplicate the advantage of a continental neighbour with enormous purchasing power. Carney’s objective is different: create enough alternative routes for Canadian capital, exports, technology and strategic industries that dependence on the United States no longer gives Washington overwhelming leverage.

Britain could be central to that project precisely because it belongs completely to neither of the two worlds Carney wants to connect. It is European but outside the EU. It is Atlantic but now institutionally embedded in the Pacific trade pact. Canada is Atlantic, Pacific and Arctic at once.

If Carney’s proposed bridge ever becomes a serious economic architecture, September 1 may eventually look less like an obscure treaty implementation date and more like one of the pieces that made the bridge possible.
