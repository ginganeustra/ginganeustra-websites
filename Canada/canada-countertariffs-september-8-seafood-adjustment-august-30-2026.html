Canada–U.S. trade · Counter-tariffs

Canada’s Sept. 8 counter-tariffs are no longer just a headline.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · 9:35 a.m. ET

Canada’s response to the latest U.S. tariffs now has the specificity businesses had been waiting for. The Finance Department has published the complete list of American products covered by the new countermeasures scheduled to take effect at 12:01 a.m. on Sept. 8.

The government says the measures match, dollar for dollar, the U.S. Section 338 tariffs imposed on C$27.6 billion in Canadian goods on Aug. 22. The list matters because a political promise to retaliate does not tell an importer whether a particular shipment, component or consumer product will be caught. The schedule does.

The key change: Ottawa has removed fish and seafood products from the counter-tariff list after feedback that they could create economic harm for Canadian businesses.

That adjustment is not a retreat from the announced response. It is an acknowledgement that retaliation has to be designed around Canadian interests, not merely around the appearance of symmetry. Seafood moves through cross-border supply chains and Canadian processors, wholesalers and communities can be hurt when inputs become more expensive or less available.

What happens next

The list is due to take effect Sept. 8. Until then, importers have a short window to check tariff classifications, contracts and supply routes. The government’s published page is the operative reference for the covered products—not summaries, social-media lists or political talking points.

The larger conflict remains unresolved. Reuters reported that the U.S. measures followed the collapse of Canada–U.S. trade talks, and that Canada recalled its negotiators and announced dollar-for-dollar retaliation. Canada’s measure is therefore both a trade instrument and a negotiating signal: Washington’s use of a rarely invoked 1930 tariff authority will impose a cost inside the American export market too.

But the seafood carve-out is equally important. It shows that Ottawa is leaving room to adjust the schedule where countermeasures would chiefly punish Canadian firms rather than create leverage against the United States. For a country trying to reduce its vulnerability to economic coercion, that distinction is not a technicality. It is strategy.
