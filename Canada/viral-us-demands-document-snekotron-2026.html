🇨🇦 CANADA AT WAR
Investigation · Viral document · August 25, 2026

The document Canada is arguing about

An unsourced list of extraordinary alleged U.S. demands has raced across social media. It is not an authenticated negotiating document. But several of its most explosive themes overlap with disputes Prime Minister Mark Carney has now described publicly — which is why the list has become harder to dismiss, and more important to examine carefully.

The viral X post by @snekotron. Canada at War is reproducing it as evidence of what circulated online, not as proof that the attached list is an official U.S. or Canadian document.
STATUS: UNAUTHENTICATED. No Canadian or U.S. government has publicly released this comprehensive list. Canadian Press found no evidence that the document itself is an official negotiating paper.

For a document with no disclosed provenance, the list titled “Summary of Key U.S. Demands in 2026 Canada-U.S. Trade Negotiations” has achieved extraordinary reach. It appeared on X through an account calling itself Dr. Snekotron, @snekotron, accompanied by a blunt interpretation: the alleged demands amounted to “subjugation if not outright annexation.” The post focused particularly on a purported American veto over Canadian infrastructure partnerships, from ports to telecommunications and power grids. By Tuesday the post had been viewed on a vast scale, and copies of the list had spread beyond X to LinkedIn, Threads, Substack, Bluesky and other networks.

That virality creates an obvious danger. Repetition is not authentication. A professional-looking page can be produced by anyone. The document contains no letterhead establishing its origin, no negotiating classification, no names, no signatures, no document number and no chain of custody. Canadian Press examined the claim and concluded that there is no evidence Canadian or American officials released such a comprehensive list. A later graphic reproducing the claims purported to be associated with Global News, but Canadian Press could find no matching Global News article and reported that the cited web address led to a blank page.

Those facts matter. Canada at War therefore is not calling this a leaked U.S. negotiating paper. The proper description is an unauthenticated summary circulating online that purports to describe U.S. demands. But that is not the end of the story. What has made the list politically potent is that some of its central themes resemble issues Carney himself says were in the room when negotiations collapsed.

The four overlaps that changed the conversation

Carney has publicly identified several last-minute or unacceptable U.S. positions. He said Washington sought constraints on Canada’s ability to make future trade arrangements with other countries. He identified a dispute over Canadian-made trucks. He described American demands touching French-language and cultural policy. And he said the United States sought a right of first refusal involving Canadian critical minerals. Those are not peripheral details. They overlap with four of the broad categories appearing in the viral list: restrictions involving non-U.S. nations, auto-sector rules, cultural and language protections, and privileged access to critical minerals.

That overlap does not validate everything else on the page. It does, however, explain why the document has survived a fact-check that should otherwise have killed it instantly. If an anonymous list had contained thirteen spectacular claims and none resembled anything subsequently acknowledged by the prime minister, its credibility would have collapsed. Instead, readers discovered that some of the themes were real negotiating disputes. The result is a grey zone: an unauthenticated document carrying a mixture of claims, some of which appear broadly consistent with confirmed events and others for which no comparable public evidence has emerged.

The distinction is crucial. The list says Washington demanded a “functional veto” over major Canadian foreign investments in infrastructure. There is no public confirmation of that sweeping formulation. It alleges automatic Canadian alignment with U.S. sanctions, export controls and tariffs on third countries. Carney has confirmed a problem involving constraints on future Canadian trade agreements, but that is not the same thing as confirming every mechanism described in the viral summary. It alleges priority allocation and price guarantees for Canadian oil, gas, hydroelectricity and nuclear energy; binding defence spending tied strictly to U.S.-made hardware; pharmaceutical patent extensions; sweeping changes to customs thresholds; and extensive agricultural concessions. Those claims remain unverified unless and until documentary evidence or attributable reporting establishes them.

What the list actually alleges

Taken at face value, the page describes something far larger than an ordinary tariff bargain. Its twelve headings reach into strategic infrastructure, relations with third countries, critical minerals, digital taxation and online news, cultural and French-language policy, public procurement, supply management, autos, pharmaceutical patents, energy, defence and Arctic security, and customs rules. In other words, it portrays the negotiations as an attempt to rewrite significant portions of Canadian economic sovereignty in exchange for tariff relief.

That framing is why the document detonated online. Canada had entered the negotiations expecting a bargain over sectoral tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber and over Canadian countermeasures. The viral list instead presents a hypothetical settlement in which Washington would acquire influence over decisions traditionally made by Canadian governments and legislatures. If authentic, that would explain Carney’s unusually strong language after the talks failed. But “if authentic” is doing enormous work in that sentence.

There is independent evidence that the final stage of the negotiations moved beyond simple tariff percentages. Canadian reporting has established disputes involving Canadian-content rules for streaming and cultural policy. Carney has spoken about the United States treating Canada as though it were a subsidiary rather than a sovereign country. Ambassador Mark Wiseman has said the written American text diverged materially in multiple places from what Canadian negotiators believed had been agreed verbally. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer offers the opposite account, saying Canada introduced new demands and walked back commitments. That conflict over the negotiating record is now central to the rupture.

Who is Dr. Snekotron?

The account that propelled the list into the mainstream is not a government source, a named negotiating official or an established news organization. It uses the display name “Dr. Snekotron” and the handle @snekotron. Publicly indexed copies show the post spreading rapidly through reposts and quote posts, eventually reaching roughly the million-view scale. The account’s accompanying text did not present a sourcing trail for the document. That absence is the single biggest weakness in the claim.

There is also no reason to assume that the account created the underlying list. Posting a document and originating it are different things. Without a source trail, we cannot establish who drafted the summary, whether it was compiled from conversations with people familiar with negotiations, assembled from public reporting, generated as commentary, or deliberately fabricated. Canadian Press’s fact-check correctly focuses on precisely that gap.

Yet the account has become part of the political story because it succeeded in putting a purported negotiating inventory before a mass audience at exactly the moment Canadians were demanding to know what Carney had refused. Opposition politicians have called for greater disclosure of the U.S. offer. Ottawa has not published the draft agreement. In that information vacuum, an anonymous document that resembles some confirmed facts can travel farther than an official statement.

Why “partly corroborated” is not “authenticated”

There is a temptation on both sides to force the evidence into a binary answer. Supporters of the list point to the Carney overlaps and say: see, it was real. Critics point to the absence of provenance and say: fake. Neither conclusion is currently justified by the public evidence.

A list can contain accurate information without being an authentic document. Someone could compile genuine reported demands and add speculation. A source with partial knowledge could describe real negotiating themes while misunderstanding details. An activist could create a summary from public information. Conversely, a genuine internal summary could circulate without letterhead or attribution. Authentication requires evidence about origin, not simply agreement between some of its contents and later reporting.

That is why the four confirmed overlaps are evidence about the claims, not evidence about the document’s provenance. Carney’s comments increase the plausibility that the author knew or inferred something about the negotiations. They do not prove the page came from Ottawa, Washington or anyone at the negotiating table.

The secrecy is feeding the document

The larger political problem is that Canadians still do not have the proposed agreement. They have fragments: Carney’s description of unacceptable demands, Greer’s competing account, comments from diplomats, reporting from journalists and the known tariff concessions each side was discussing. The actual late-stage American text has not been released.

That secrecy may be normal in live trade negotiations, but once negotiations collapse over alleged sovereignty questions, the political incentives change. Canadians naturally want to know what was demanded in their name and what their government was prepared to concede. Every day the draft remains private increases the value of leaks, summaries, screenshots and rumours.

The viral list therefore matters even if it ultimately proves to be partly or wholly wrong. It is a symptom of an extraordinary breakdown in trust. Canadians are being told that a proposed agreement failed because the written terms crossed fundamental lines, while the United States says Canada changed its position. The primary document capable of resolving much of that dispute remains unavailable. Into that vacuum came a single-page list with explosive allegations and no provenance — and millions of people looked at it because there was nothing more authoritative to read.

What would establish the truth

There are several ways this could become clearer. Ottawa could release the U.S. draft or a redacted version. Parliament could obtain the negotiating text. A credible news organization could independently acquire and authenticate it. Named officials could confirm or deny individual items. Washington could publish its version of the proposed terms. Until then, each allegation needs to be judged separately.

For now, the strongest conclusion is narrower but still remarkable. The viral document cannot be authenticated. Some of its most dramatic assertions remain unsupported. But several of its core subject areas are no longer merely internet rumours: Carney has independently confirmed that restrictions involving third-country trade, Canadian trucks, French-language and cultural protections, and preferential access to critical minerals were genuine points of conflict.

That is enough to make the document worth investigating — and nowhere near enough to call the whole thing true.
