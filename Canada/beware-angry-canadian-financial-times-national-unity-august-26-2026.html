Canada at War · Media analysis · Aug. 26, 2026

Beware the Angry Canadian

The Financial Times sees something larger than a tariff fight: American pressure is producing an unusually broad Canadian coalition around sovereignty, resilience and the refusal to be pushed around.

The Financial Times opens its latest dispatch from Canada with a line that would once have sounded almost comic: “Beware the angry Canadian.” The humour quickly gives way to a serious argument. Its story is less about tariff schedules than about a change in Canadian political psychology after sustained pressure from Washington.

Donald Trump’s latest escalation includes a threatened 50 per cent tariff on Canadian cars and auto parts beginning Jan. 1. Canada has answered with dollar-for-dollar countermeasures. But the FT finds the argument moving beyond tariffs. Ontario Premier Doug Ford has discussed electricity and strategic commodities. Former Alberta premier Jason Kenney says export taxes on oil, gas, potash and hydroelectricity should not be ruled out. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly says Canada still has options.

The coalition is the story

The striking feature is who now occupies roughly the same side of the national argument. The FT moves from Ford and Joly to Kenney and former UN ambassador Bob Rae, then to Margaret Atwood, Mike Myers and Neil Young. Even Conrad Black, pardoned by Trump in 2019 and historically far more sympathetic to him than most prominent Canadians, warns that Washington may have mistaken Canada’s lack of assertiveness for a lack of resilience.

These figures disagree profoundly about Canadian politics. Their convergence is therefore evidence of how far the dispute has travelled from an ordinary trade negotiation. The common proposition is increasingly that there is a point at which accommodation ceases to be commercial pragmatism and becomes a question of national self-respect.

Carney’s political gamble

Prime Minister Mark Carney has described the confrontation in unusually stark terms, saying Canada was attacked and insisting that no other nation will determine Canada’s future. The FT reports an Angus Reid Institute poll finding 76 per cent support for his decision to abandon the negotiations, while roughly two-thirds of respondents said they believed Canada would ultimately emerge stronger despite the economic damage.

Joly’s description of Canadians as angry, anxious and proud captures the political equation. Anxiety can encourage compromise and anger can fade. Pride can make economic sacrifice feel like the cost of preserving independence — and that changes how coercive pressure works.

Canada has leverage — but using it would hurt

The FT also examines the other side of North American dependence. Canada sends roughly four million barrels of crude oil to the United States every day and is an essential supplier of potash, natural gas, hydroelectricity and other strategic resources. That does not mean Ottawa can weaponize those exports without severe Canadian costs. It means the relationship is more reciprocal than the simple claim that Canada needs America while America does not need Canada.

Critics correctly warn that emotion is not an economic strategy. The U.S. economy is vastly larger. Retaliatory tariffs raise costs at home as well as abroad, and export restrictions could injure Canadian producers. The durability of the current unity will be tested if factories close or unemployment rises.

Economic nationalism feeds on the confrontation

The FT notes that the dispute has encouraged domestic tourism and Canadian purchasing of Canadian products. That creates a paradox for Washington: pressure against Canadian economic nationalism may be generating more of it. The political mood is visible in petitions as well, including large campaigns directed at U.S. Ambassador Pete Hoekstra and Elon Musk.

The central point: economic coercion works best when the target concludes that resistance costs more than concession. If Canadians increasingly interpret the cost as the price of maintaining sovereignty, additional pressure can strengthen the political demand to resist rather than weaken it.

Why the FT story matters

The importance of the article is not that it proves Canada can win an economic confrontation with a country ten times its size. It is that one of Europe’s most influential financial newspapers is telling an international audience that Washington may have misread the country it is pressuring.

The emerging Canadian response is not simply anger at a tariff. It is a coalition spanning provinces, parties, cultural figures, business interests and former officials, organized around the idea that the United States cannot be allowed to dictate Canada’s political and economic choices.

That is why the FT’s opening line lands differently by the end. The angry Canadian is not frightening because Canada suddenly became the larger power. The angry Canadian matters because a population willing to absorb costs for reasons of sovereignty is much harder to coerce than one negotiating only over price.
