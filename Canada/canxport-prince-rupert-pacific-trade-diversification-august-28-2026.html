Canada opens a new Pacific export hub — infrastructure for the pivot away from dependence.

CANXPORT at Prince Rupert can transload at least 400,000 containers a year and eventually as many as 750,000, giving Canadian forestry, agricultural and industrial exporters more capacity to reach global markets.

Canada's effort to diversify trade away from overwhelming dependence on the United States has acquired another piece of physical infrastructure.

The CANXPORT logistics facility has officially opened at the Port of Prince Rupert in northern British Columbia. Formerly known as the Ridley Island Export Logistics Project, the terminal connects rail shipments with marine containers and is designed to handle at least 400,000 containers annually, with potential capacity of 750,000.

Ottawa contributed nearly C$50 million through the National Trade Corridors Fund. The facility includes three inbound and three outbound rail tracks dedicated to export transloading and is designed for products including forestry goods, agricultural commodities and plastic resins.

Trade diversification requires ports

The strategic significance lies in geography. Prince Rupert is North America's closest major port to Asia. In 2025 it handled 26.3 million tonnes of cargo, up 14 per cent from 2024, making it Canada's third-busiest container port and fourth-busiest port authority by tonnage.

Canada can sign trade agreements across Europe and the Indo-Pacific, but those agreements cannot materially reduce U.S. dependence unless Canadian producers can move substantially larger volumes efficiently to overseas customers. Railways, transload facilities, terminals and berths are therefore as much a part of trade policy as tariff schedules.

CANXPORT is especially useful because it converts bulk Canadian products arriving by rail into marine containers for export. That creates another route to customers that does not depend on the north-south continental supply chain.

The Port of Prince Rupert says about C$3 billion in expansion projects are underway and could double the volume of goods moving through the port by 2030. CANXPORT sits alongside other new logistics and energy-export infrastructure.

For Canada at War, this is what economic diversification looks like when it becomes concrete: not another promise to find new customers, but rails, cranes, containers and a Pacific port capable of moving Canadian production toward them.
