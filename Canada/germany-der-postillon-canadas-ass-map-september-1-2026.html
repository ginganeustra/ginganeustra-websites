GERMANY HAS A NAME FOR AMERICA’S NEW PLACE ON THE MAP. IT IS NOT DIPLOMATIC.

Der Postillon looked at North America, considered the current map-renaming mood and decided the United States needed a new label.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 4:52 p.m. ET

There are diplomatic communiqués. There are state visits. There are strongly worded statements. And then there is Germany’s Der Postillon, which has looked at the map of North America, considered the current mood, and decided that the United States needs a new name: “Ass of Canada.”

Before anyone rings Global Affairs, this is not a dispatch from Ottawa. It is satire—very deliberately and very efficiently deployed.

In a piece published Aug. 28, Der Postillon imagines Prime Minister Mark Carney using Donald Trump’s enthusiasm for renaming bodies of water as legal precedent. If a president can relabel the Gulf of Mexico the “Gulf of America,” the joke goes, why should a Canadian prime minister not look south and make a cartographic intervention of his own?

The publication’s imaginary Carney offers the map-based rationale: the country currently called the United States is, quite literally, south of Canada. From there the joke proceeds with the ruthless logic of a good newsroom parody: a new acronym, a parade of countries supposedly prepared to adopt it, and a proposed flag redesign too rude to be mistaken for a Department of Canadian Heritage initiative.

For Canadians arriving late to the joke, Der Postillon is Germany’s well-established satirical news outlet. Founded in 2008 by Stefan Sichermann, it publishes deadpan mock news reports in the formal voice of a newspaper and cheerfully claims a far older heritage—“honest news, independent, fast, since 1845.” That is part of the act.

Its Carney piece does not pretend to report a real Canadian law, a real renaming, or a real press conference. The comedy lies in treating a surreal escalation as if it were normal government business: a leader at a podium, a map, a neighbouring country suddenly recategorized with the tidy confidence of an administrative change.

There is also something useful in the fact that the line came from Germany. Canada’s relationship with the United States is usually narrated at home in the language of trade agreements, border rules, defence procurement and presidential threats. From a distance, the basic visual is more immediate: Canada, immense and north; the United States, directly below; and a global audience beginning to notice how strange the old assumptions have become.

The joke is coarse, but the craftsmanship is not. Der Postillon identifies the unnerving part of this period: once political leaders start treating names, maps and national symbols as disposable props, the rest of the world is free to answer in the same register.

Canada did not rename its neighbour. But Germany’s satirists have now put the thought on the map.

Satire note: The Canadian actions described in Der Postillon’s source article are fictional. This story reports on and analyzes the satire; it does not present the fictional renaming as a real government action.
