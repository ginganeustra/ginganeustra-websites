THE SECOND GATE OPENS FOR CHINESE EVS

Canada’s second import period starts today with 24,500 fresh slots plus unused first-period capacity. Now the question is which automakers turn quota access into Canadian showroom cars.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 4:25 a.m. ET

At 12:00 a.m. today, Canada opened the second six-month window of its new quota for electric vehicles built in China. The immediate number is 24,500 vehicles. The more interesting number is larger: Ottawa’s rules add any quota left unused from March through August.

Global Affairs Canada’s last published utilization update before the deadline showed 15,603 of the first period’s 24,500 slots used as of Aug. 28. If no large last-minute shipment changed that total, roughly 8,897 unused places would carry into the new period — potentially giving importers room for about 33,397 China-origin vehicles between Sept. 1 and Feb. 28.

That does not mean 33,397 new Chinese-brand cars will suddenly appear in Canadian dealerships. The quota is based on where a vehicle is substantially manufactured, not the nationality of the badge. Shanghai-built Teslas, Chinese-built vehicles from Western brands and demonstration vehicles can all count. It also takes time to certify vehicles, build dealer networks, stock parts, train technicians and convince buyers to trust a brand they may never have seen.

But the policy gate is now unmistakably open.

The 100 per cent tariff is no longer the rule inside the quota

Canada imposed a 100 per cent surtax on Chinese-made EVs in 2024. Under the Canada-China arrangement implemented March 1, 2026, eligible vehicles that receive a quota permit are instead assessed at the normal 6.1 per cent most-favoured-nation tariff. The annual first-year quota is 49,000 vehicles, split into two periods. Global Affairs Canada’s new Notice 1168 says the second period runs from Sept. 1 through Feb. 28, with permits issued to eligible importers on a first-come, first-served basis.

That is a major change in economics. A car that was effectively priced out of Canada by a 100 per cent surtax can now compete if the manufacturer secures quota space and satisfies Canadian vehicle rules.

The old story was whether Canada would allow Chinese-made EVs in meaningful numbers. The new story is which manufacturers will turn access into actual retail sales.

Three models worth watching

The models below are not a promise that all three will be sold in Canada. They are useful examples of the price-and-range pressure Chinese manufacturers could bring. Chery has confirmed a Canadian launch plan; the exact launch model or models were not yet settled when it announced its plans. BYD and Zeekr are more speculative model-by-model, so their figures should be read as reference points rather than Canadian MSRPs.

BYD Dolphin

Estimated Canadian price: about C$35,000

Range: roughly 211–427 km, depending on battery and trim

AutoTrader identifies the Dolphin as the kind of sub-$40,000 electric hatchback that could fill a conspicuous hole in the Canadian market. BYD has not announced a Canadian Dolphin MSRP, so the C$35,000 figure is an estimate, not a dealer price.

Chery Omoda E5

Estimated Canadian price: about C$35,000–45,000

Range: roughly 400–430 km

This one matters because Chery has publicly said it aims to sell vehicles in Canada by the end of 2026, likely beginning with one or two models from one of its brands. Omoda is among the brands it controls. The E5’s 61-kWh battery and compact-SUV shape put it directly in one of Canada’s most important vehicle segments. Chery has not confirmed that the E5 itself will be the launch model.

Zeekr 001

Reference price: about C$98,000

Range: up to about 620 km in long-range rear-drive form

Zeekr is part of China’s Geely group, which already has deep connections to brands Canadians know, including Volvo, Polestar and Lotus. AutoTrader has highlighted the 001 as a model it believes could fit Canada. It would not be the cheap-China story: it is a premium electric shooting brake aimed at high-end European rivals. There is no announced Canadian 001 price or confirmed Canadian launch.

Why Canadians have not noticed the quota yet

The first import period did not produce a wave of unfamiliar Chinese badges. Drive Tesla Canada, using Global Affairs Canada’s utilization data and known shipments, reported that much of the volume was likely Shanghai-built Tesla vehicles. Lotus has also brought China-built Eletre vehicles into the country. The federal data identifies customs categories, not a public model-by-model scoreboard.

That distinction is politically important. “Chinese EV imports” can mean a Chinese-owned Chinese brand, a U.S. brand building in Shanghai, or a European-linked brand whose vehicle comes off a Chinese assembly line. Ottawa’s rules focus on origin: a vehicle substantially manufactured in China falls under the quota, even if the logo on the hood is familiar.

Price could be the disruptive part

Canada’s new-vehicle market has an affordability problem. AutoTrader has noted how thin the selection of genuinely inexpensive battery-electric vehicles remains. That is why a Dolphin near C$35,000 or an Omoda E5 in the C$35,000–45,000 band would matter more to the mass market than another six-figure electric SUV.

There are still constraints. Quota access is limited. Federal incentive rules do not automatically make every Chinese import eligible for subsidies. Canadian crash, lighting and other regulatory requirements must be met. Dealers need parts and service. Concerns about connected-vehicle data and cybersecurity will follow Chinese brands into Canada. And Washington maintains a much harder policy toward Chinese vehicle technology, creating questions for Canadians who regularly drive across the U.S. border.

None of those issues erases the central change. Canada has chosen a different path from the United States: managed access rather than an effectively prohibitive tariff wall.

Today is the first real test of that choice

The second quota window begins with more room than the headline 24,500 suggests because unused first-period capacity rolls over. Importers can apply for shipment-specific permits up to 30 days before expected entry, and Global Affairs Canada says permits will continue to be issued first-come, first-served until further notice.

That means the infrastructure is in place for a faster second half than the first. Chery is preparing a Canadian entry. BYD has been openly discussed as a likely contender. Geely’s family of brands already has pathways into the market. The question over the next six months is whether those plans become showroom inventory — and whether the arrival of lower-priced, longer-range Chinese EVs forces every automaker selling in Canada to respond.

For consumers, the numbers to watch are no longer a 100 per cent surtax and a closed door. They are 6.1 per cent, a quota with unused capacity, and the price tags manufacturers decide to put in Canadian windows.
