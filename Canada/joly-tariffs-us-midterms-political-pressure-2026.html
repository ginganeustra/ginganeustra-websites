🇨🇦 CANADA AT WAR
Lead analysis · Canada’s political tariff strategy

Canada is targeting Trump’s political map.

Mélanie Joly has said out loud what governments usually leave implicit: Ottawa’s new tariff list is designed not only to protect Canadian industry, but to put political pressure on particular American states before the November midterms.

Canada at War · August 25, 2026

Canada’s latest retaliation against the United States is not simply an accounting exercise. Ottawa has promised to answer Washington dollar for dollar and rate for rate, but Industry Minister Mélanie Joly has now made clear that the government also looked at the American political map when deciding where the pain should fall.

Speaking as the federal government unveiled counter-tariffs on C$27.6 billion of U.S. imports, Joly said the first objective was to protect Canadian businesses facing American tariffs. Then she described a second objective: selecting products connected to particular U.S. states so that Canada could be, in her words, “wise and strategic” in applying political pressure. Reuters reported that she explicitly linked that pressure to the November 3 midterm elections. The Wall Street Journal separately reported that Canadian officials crafted the package to exert maximum pressure in important midterm races.

That is a significant evolution in the politics of the trade war. Retaliatory tariffs are often designed this way. Governments try to make foreign trade barriers politically expensive by concentrating costs in industries, regions and constituencies with influence over the government imposing the original tariffs. What is unusual is the clarity with which a Canadian cabinet minister has acknowledged the electoral logic while the campaign is underway.

September 8

Canada’s counter-tariffs take effect the day after Labour Day — traditionally the beginning of the final sprint of a U.S. election campaign — less than two months before Americans vote on November 3.

Maine shows how the strategy can work

The most obvious example is lobster. Canada’s published tariff schedule includes fresh and frozen lobster at 25 per cent. Maine’s lobster industry is not an abstract trade statistic: it is economically and culturally central to a state hosting one of the most closely watched Senate races in the country.

Republican Senator Susan Collins has already broken publicly with the Trump administration over the Canada confrontation. In a statement after negotiations collapsed, Collins warned that new tariffs would raise costs for Maine families and businesses and noted that farmers and lobstermen were worried about Canadian retaliation. She urged Washington and Ottawa back to the negotiating table. Associated Press reporting on the midterms identifies Maine, Michigan, Ohio and Alaska among states where the Canada dispute creates particular political risk.

That makes a tariff on Maine seafood strategically different from a tariff chosen solely because it produces an equivalent dollar value. It potentially converts Canadian retaliation into calls from American exporters, local business groups, elected officials and voters demanding that Washington find an exit.

Michigan may be even more consequential

Michigan is deeply exposed because the auto industry does not function as two neatly separated national industries. Vehicles, engines, metals and components cross the border repeatedly. The Washington Post reports that business groups representing Michigan automotive, tourism and agricultural interests are already warning about the consequences of the dispute. A third of Michigan’s exports go to Canada, according to AP’s reporting.

For Ottawa, the strategic objective is not necessarily to punish an individual senator or congressional candidate. It is to make the continuation of the trade war generate political friction inside the United States. A governor, senator, manufacturer or farm organization that would otherwise regard Canadian tariffs as a distant foreign-policy issue suddenly has a constituency problem.

Canada is leaving its biggest weapons holstered

The targeting also reveals restraint. Ottawa has not used this round to attack Canadian exports that give it some of its greatest structural leverage over the United States. Energy and potash remain conspicuously outside the new package. The United States relies heavily on Canadian petroleum and on Canadian potash for fertilizer. Using those weapons could create far greater economic disruption — but also much greater collateral damage and escalation.

Instead, the government has selected a broad range of politically visible and commercially meaningful products: steel and aluminum, furniture, apparel, cheese, seafood, appliances, prepared foods, perfumes and toiletries, plastics, lumber, pulp and paper, carpets, tools, machinery, electrical equipment, rail equipment, motorcycles and gaming products. The list is large enough to distribute pressure across many American constituencies while stopping short of an energy shock.

The American political environment is unusually receptive

Canada’s strategy is landing in an election year in which affordability is already a major vulnerability for the administration. The Washington Post reports that Republicans in trade-dependent states are worried about the impact of the Canada fight. Collins has called new tariffs a mistake. In Michigan, business interests that include many Trump voters oppose the tariffs because Canada is their largest trading partner. The United Auto Workers has also criticized further escalation against Canada.

AP describes the trade war as a potential complication for Republican efforts to retain control of the Senate. That does not mean Canadian tariffs will determine the election. American voters rank many issues above Canada. But Ottawa does not need Canada to become the central campaign issue. It needs enough American businesses and voters in enough strategically useful places to conclude that the cost of the dispute is no longer theoretical.

The strategy carries risks for Canada, too

Political targeting is not cost-free. A tariff is paid at the Canadian border, which means Canadian importers and ultimately Canadian businesses or consumers can bear part of the burden. The Canadian Automobile Dealers Association has already warned against retaliation that compounds the damage done by U.S. measures. And if Washington interprets explicit midterm targeting as interference in domestic politics rather than conventional trade retaliation, it could harden the administration’s position.

There is also a practical limit. The United States is vastly larger than Canada. Canadian retaliation covers only a fraction of total U.S. exports, just as the newest American tariffs cover a minority of Canadian exports. Ottawa therefore has to concentrate pressure rather than pretend it can win through raw economic scale.

That is precisely why Joly’s statement matters. It exposes the theory of the Canadian response. Ottawa is not trying to outspend or out-tariff the United States. It is trying to change the American political calculation by making the trade war costly in places where those costs can travel quickly from factory floors, fishing wharves and farm organizations to members of Congress.

A trade war aimed at Washington — through the states

For months, Canada’s strategy emphasized negotiation, exemptions and de-escalation. Ottawa rescinded its digital services tax, removed many retaliatory tariffs, explored compromises on alcohol and dairy and tried to obtain relief for autos, steel and aluminum. That approach ended when Carney suspended negotiations after concluding that the final U.S. terms were unacceptable.

The new approach is more openly political. The September 8 package still leaves room for negotiation and deliberately avoids some of Canada’s most dangerous economic weapons. But it also attempts to create an American constituency for ending the confrontation.

The calendar is impossible to miss. The tariffs begin immediately after Labour Day. The midterms follow on November 3. Maine has lobsters. Michigan has autos and integrated manufacturing. Other states have steel, agriculture, machinery, paper, consumer goods and exporters dependent on Canadian buyers. Ottawa has built a tariff list that reaches into that geography.

Whether it works depends on what happens next. If American politicians begin demanding exemptions or a return to negotiations because businesses at home are feeling Canadian retaliation, Ottawa will have evidence that targeted pressure can compensate, at least partly, for the enormous difference in economic size. If Washington simply escalates again, Canada will face a harder decision: keep playing a calibrated political game, or begin reaching for the much larger forms of leverage — energy, strategic resources and procurement — that it has so far kept off the battlefield.
