CANADA AT WAR
Auto industry · CAMI · Industrial sovereignty

96.5 per cent voted yes. Their factory still has nothing to build.

CAMI workers overwhelmingly accepted a new GM contract that guarantees no replacement vehicle for their idled Ontario assembly plant. What it gives them instead is income protection, time — and first consideration for Canadian military work if General Motors wins it.

Published Aug. 29, 2026 · Updated Aug. 30, 2026 · 11:13 p.m. ET

INGERSOLL, Ont. — There may be no number that better captures the precarious state of Canada’s automobile industry than 96.5 per cent.

That is the share of unionized workers at General Motors’ CAMI Assembly plant in Ingersoll who voted to ratify a new three-year collective agreement Sunday. It is an extraordinary mandate. It is also a vote for an agreement that gives those workers no replacement vehicle to build.

CAMI remains idled and, according to Unifor, the majority of its members are on indefinite layoff. The union says it will continue pressing for production to return. GM has committed to seek opportunities for the facility, but the ratified agreement does not announce a new vehicle or provide a timetable for restarting assembly.

Instead, it extends the Income Maintenance Plan for eligible laid-off workers until May 2028, creating what GM bargaining chair Trevor Longpre explicitly called a “bridge” until CAMI workers can get back on the job.

And it contains an unusual provision that points toward a very different possible future for the Ingersoll factory: GM has designated CAMI as its plant of first consideration for the allocation of Canadian Armed Forces defence work if that work is awarded to General Motors.

That qualification is important. There is no announced Canadian military contract for CAMI, no guarantee GM will win such work and no guarantee that defence production will ultimately return the idled workforce to the plant. But the provision itself is striking. A factory that once represented Canada’s ambitions in electric commercial vehicles is now being positioned for possible military production as Ottawa expands defence procurement and looks for ways to strengthen domestic industrial capacity.

A factory waiting for a future

The contrast with GM’s other Ontario operations could hardly be sharper.

Workers covered by the broader GMCC agreement at Oshawa, St. Catharines and Woodstock voted 80.5 per cent in favour. Those plants received concrete product and investment commitments. GM will put C$144 million into Oshawa Assembly to add the next-generation heavy-duty GMC Sierra and C$215 million into St. Catharines for a next-generation transmission, with that work anticipated to begin in late 2029.

The collective agreement also enshrines C$691 million previously committed for GM’s sixth-generation V8 engine in St. Catharines and C$63 million previously committed for stamping and customer-care-and-aftersales upgrades in Oshawa. Unifor describes the total commitments as more than C$1 billion, but a substantial portion of that headline figure consists of investments announced before this weekend’s ratification.

For Oshawa and St. Catharines, then, the agreement is partly about what those factories will build. For CAMI, the central question remains whether the plant can remain viable long enough to discover what it will build next.

From civilian vehicles to defence?

The defence provision places CAMI at the intersection of two strategic problems Canada increasingly has to confront at the same time.

The first is the vulnerability of an automobile manufacturing system built around deep integration with the United States. The second is Canada’s drive to increase military spending and develop more domestic defence-production capacity.

CAMI demonstrates how those issues can converge. An idled civilian vehicle plant, stripped of its previous product, is now explicitly being put forward as a potential home for Canadian Armed Forces work.

That does not mean CAMI is becoming a defence factory. The agreement says something narrower and more precise: if Canadian Armed Forces defence work is awarded to GM, CAMI is the plant of first consideration for its allocation. What matters politically is that this possibility has become part of a labour agreement over the future of a major Canadian assembly plant at all.

The American pressure behind the vote

The agreement was negotiated as Canada’s auto industry faces severe pressure from U.S. tariffs. Canadian vehicles currently face 25-per-cent U.S. duties, while President Donald Trump has threatened to raise tariffs on Canadian vehicles and parts to 50 per cent on Jan. 1, 2027. Reuters reports that the fate of Canadian auto plants has become a central issue in stalled Canada-U.S. trade negotiations.

Unifor president Lana Payne was blunt after ratification, saying Canada’s domestic auto industry is “under siege by the Trump Administration.”

The reason is structural. Ontario’s auto industry was built around a continental production system in which vehicles and components could cross the Canada-U.S. border with relatively little friction. Tariffs large enough to alter the economics of those movements can influence where automakers assign future products — and product allocation determines whether assembly plants live or die.

Against that background, GM’s investments in Oshawa and St. Catharines are significant Canadian production commitments. CAMI shows the other side of the story: what happens when a Canadian factory has no replacement product secured.

What does 96.5 per cent mean?

Ratification votes are usually reported as judgments on wages, pensions and benefits. CAMI’s result deserves to be read in a wider industrial context.

The three-year agreements provide three-per-cent annual wage increases, with full-rate production wages reaching $50.20 an hour and skilled-trades wages $62.71 during the contracts. But for CAMI’s laid-off workforce, the immediate issue is more fundamental than the hourly rate. It is getting the plant producing again.

CAMI workers voted 96.5 per cent for an agreement that gives eligible laid-off members income protection through May 2028 and places the factory first in line for potential GM Canadian military work — but gives them no confirmed replacement vehicle.

That tension is the story.

The vote buys workers protection and time. It does not answer the question hanging over Ingersoll: what will this two-million-square-foot Canadian assembly plant actually build?

Canada spent generations constructing an automobile industry around continental integration. If that model is being destabilized by U.S. tariffs and political pressure, Ottawa may increasingly have to decide whether government procurement, defence spending and industrial policy should be used deliberately to preserve Canadian productive capacity.

At CAMI, that debate is no longer abstract.

The 96.5-per-cent vote is real. The income bridge is real. The first-consideration clause for possible defence work is real.

The replacement vehicle is not.
