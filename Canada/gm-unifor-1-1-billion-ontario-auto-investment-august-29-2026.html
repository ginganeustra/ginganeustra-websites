CANADA AT WAR
Auto industry · Ontario · Canada–U.S. trade

GM’s $1.1-billion Ontario commitment puts pickups, engines and transmissions on Canada’s side of the border.

The new GMC Sierra line in Oshawa is only one part of a much larger tentative GM–Unifor agreement covering production, investment and job security across Ontario.

Published Aug. 29, 2026 · 7:30 p.m. ET · Industry report

The headline in the new General Motors–Unifor settlement is not simply a pickup truck line in Oshawa. It is a roughly $1.1-billion commitment to Canadian operations—an investment package built around heavy-duty trucks, V8 engines and a next-generation transmission at a moment when U.S. tariffs have made the future of cross-border auto production a central Canadian political issue.

Unifor’s bargaining report says the tentative agreement, covering more than 4,600 workers at GM facilities in Ontario, includes new investments and reconfirmed commitments over the life of the 2026 collective agreement. Members were scheduled to vote on ratification Aug. 29 and 30. Until that process is complete, the commitments remain part of a tentative deal rather than a finished contract.

The investment package
  • Oshawa: $207 million. GM plans $144 million to add the next-generation heavy-duty GMC Sierra and recommits $63 million for stamping and related upgrades.
  • St. Catharines: $906 million. This combines a new $215-million transmission program with a previously announced $691-million sixth-generation V8-engine investment.
  • CAMI, Ingersoll: GM pledged not to immediately sell or close the idled assembly plant; its longer-term production future remains unresolved.

Oshawa gets the GMC nameplate

The $144-million Sierra commitment is meaningful because it gives Oshawa a GMC-badged heavy-duty program alongside its Chevrolet heavy-duty truck work. Unifor says the plant is expected to remain on two shifts during the agreement, subject to demand and company volume forecasts. The union also notes that the current package builds on an earlier $280-million commitment for next-generation Chevrolet Silverado heavy-duty trucks.

That does not erase the pressure created by GM’s January decision to reduce Oshawa from three shifts to two. But it gives the plant a clearer production role at a time when the United States has imposed 25% tariffs on Canadian-built vehicles and threatened a further increase. Canada’s position in the stalled trade talks has been that an auto agreement needs genuine protection for Canadian production, not simply tariff relief on paper.

The larger sum is in St. Catharines

The bulk of the $1.1 billion is tied to St. Catharines Propulsion. GM recommits $691 million for sixth-generation V8-engine production and plans a further $215 million to build a next-generation transmission there. The transmission program is expected to begin in late 2029, with St. Catharines designated the single-source plant and approximately 250 new jobs anticipated at full volume, according to Unifor.

That combination matters strategically. Heavy-duty trucks and their powertrains are not marginal Canadian assembly work; they are core North American products. Retaining both vehicle and propulsion programs preserves a more complete industrial base—suppliers, skilled trades, logistics and engineering capacity—than a simple final-assembly commitment would.

CAMI remains the unresolved test

The deal does not create a new vehicle program for CAMI Assembly in Ingersoll, where BrightDrop electric-van production was halted. Its immediate value is defensive: a pledge not to sell or close the site right away. Reuters reported that defence contracts could be considered if awarded, but no confirmed replacement program has been announced. The distinction matters. A protected facility is not the same thing as a secured production future.

GM Canada said when bargaining opened that it had invested $3.3 billion in its Canadian manufacturing plants since 2020. The new tentative agreement makes a substantial addition to that record, but it also demonstrates why the trade fight is so consequential. Auto investment is being negotiated across a North American system whose rules are suddenly less dependable.

The central Canadian result is therefore larger than the Sierra headline: GM has put significant product and powertrain capacity in Ontario, while workers have secured commitments that will be tested by ratification, demand and the tariff confrontation still unfolding across the border.

What is confirmed and what is not: The investment figures and plant commitments come from Unifor’s bargaining report. The agreement was tentative at publication. Production schedules, job levels and CAMI’s future remain conditional on ratification, demand and GM decisions.
