Honda’s warning is the investment damage Trump said tariffs would prevent.

Honda needs another North American assembly plant. Now a senior executive says the company may change direction if USMCA is not extended. The threat is no longer theoretical: uncertainty created in Washington is entering corporate decisions about where the next generation of factories and jobs will go.

Updated August 26, 2026 · 14:00 ET

For months, the argument over Donald Trump’s tariffs on Canada could be treated as a fight over border taxes, negotiating leverage and the price of imported goods. Honda has now supplied a much more consequential measure of the damage: investment.

Honda Executive Vice-President Noriya Kaihara told reporters in Washington that the Japanese automaker is approaching full production capacity in North America and needs an eighth assembly plant. Honda wants that factory operating around 2030 and expects to decide its direction within a year or two. But Kaihara said that if there is no future USMCA agreement, Honda may have to change course.

That matters because a company that says it needs new capacity is now attaching that investment decision to the survival of the continental trade system Washington is destabilizing.

The location has not been announced, so it would be wrong to say a U.S. plant has already been cancelled. But the implications for the United States are direct. Trump’s tariff strategy is promoted as a way to force manufacturing investment into America. Honda’s warning shows the policy can produce the opposite incentive: when companies cannot predict the rules governing a deeply integrated continental supply chain, they can delay, redirect or reconsider investment altogether.

The entire auto system is flashing the same warning

Reuters reported Wednesday that automakers had expected the failed Canada-U.S. negotiations to lower the top-line U.S. tariff on Canadian cars and light trucks from 25% to 15%. Instead Trump threatened a 50% tariff on Canadian vehicles, trucks and auto parts beginning January 1.

The exposure is not marginal. Ford, General Motors, Stellantis, Toyota and Honda all operate across a border that the industry has spent decades treating as part of a single manufacturing platform. GM builds roughly 17% of Chevrolet Silverado production in Canada. Stellantis builds the Chrysler Pacifica there. Ford is preparing to import Super Duty trucks from Oakville. Toyota and Honda together accounted for more than three quarters of Canadian vehicle production in 2025, according to Global Automakers of Canada figures cited by Reuters.

Parts can cross the border repeatedly before a finished vehicle reaches a dealer. A 50% tariff therefore does not simply punish a foreign competitor. It can raise costs inside an American-branded vehicle assembled with Canadian and U.S. content, hit U.S. suppliers, and make North American production less attractive compared with imports from countries that have secured 15% tariff arrangements with Washington.

Honda is not the first investment warning

Honda’s statement fits a pattern. Reuters reports Hyundai previously told the administration that uncertainty surrounding USMCA was delaying investment decisions and said confirmation of an extension could unlock more than $20 billion in new American investment. In other words, trade-policy predictability itself has become an investment asset — and uncertainty has become a cost.

Honda has already suspended an approximately $11-billion Canadian EV investment plan and cancelled three planned EVs for the U.S. market amid a broader retreat from its earlier EV targets. Those decisions have multiple commercial causes and should not simply be attributed to tariffs. But the new assembly-plant warning is different because Honda itself explicitly connected its future direction to USMCA.

Trump escalates as industry asks for certainty

The warning arrived as Trump escalated his rhetoric again Wednesday, telling Glenn Beck that it was time to “teach Canada” a lesson and arguing the United States could obtain what it needs elsewhere. White House adviser Peter Navarro separately predicted Canada would ultimately receive a worse agreement than the one it rejected.

That political message is colliding with the industrial reality described by automakers: Canada is not simply a supplier from which the United States can painlessly detach. It is embedded in the same factories, supplier contracts, platforms and investment plans.

Industry officials told Reuters they remain hopeful Washington and Ottawa can reach an agreement before the January auto deadline. The delayed implementation itself leaves negotiating room. But every month of uncertainty matters when companies are making decisions about factories designed to operate for decades.

The test is now investment, not rhetoric

The most important question is no longer whether tariffs sound tough. It is whether they cause companies to put more capital into American factories. Honda’s warning provides an early and uncomfortable answer: the disruption of USMCA can make a company that needs additional North American capacity reconsider where and how it expands.

If that uncertainty eventually costs the United States a factory, supplier expansion or jobs, it would invert the central promise of the tariff policy. The instrument intended to pull investment into the United States would instead have helped make the United States — and North America as a whole — a less predictable place to invest.
