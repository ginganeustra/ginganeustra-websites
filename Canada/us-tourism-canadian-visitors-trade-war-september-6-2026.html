U.S. TOURISM WANTS CANADIANS BACK. THE TRADE FIGHT IS MAKING THAT HARDER.

U.S. destinations are offering discounts and new outreach to Canadians. But after a year of lost trips, a trade fight and political insults are making a quick return unlikely.

Published Sept. 6, 2026 · 10:39 a.m. ET

OTTAWA — American tourism groups are spending money to bring Canadians back. They are offering hotel and attraction discounts, meeting travel advisers north of the border and taking their pitch directly to a market that once supplied the United States with more overnight international visitors than any other country.

The problem is that the sales pitch is running into a political rupture that a lower room rate cannot easily solve. Canadian travel to the United States fell sharply in 2025 as Donald Trump returned to office, spoke repeatedly about absorbing Canada and launched tariffs. The breakdown in trade talks late last month has given many Canadians another reason to keep their spending elsewhere.

The Associated Press reported this weekend that New York State has promoted a Canada-focused package of hotel, restaurant and attraction offers; some downtown Las Vegas hotels are treating the Canadian dollar at par; and the Las Vegas tourism authority travelled to Vancouver to meet the travel trade. Brand USA will bring a renamed trade-event series to Canada in October.

Tourism money is part of the economic relationship

Canada’s pullback is not symbolic to the places that depend on it. Statistics Canada found that Canadian residents made 25.4 per cent fewer return border crossings from the United States in 2025 than in 2024. The agency said Canadians made 3.2 million fewer leisure visits to the United States while taking more leisure trips overseas.

The U.S. Travel Association had already warned that even a 10-per-cent reduction in Canadian travel could mean two million fewer visits, US$2.1 billion less spending and 14,000 fewer American jobs. Its estimate was a forecast, not a count of losses, but it shows why tourism offices are now working so openly to repair the market.

There have been some signs of movement this summer. Statistics Canada reported that Canadian return trips from the United States rose in May and June, and the World Cup brought extra cross-border traffic. But the recovery has been uneven: the agency said air travel to the United States was still below its year-earlier level through June, while the recent increase was driven mainly by road trips.

A discount cannot settle a political dispute

The latest outreach comes as Ottawa prepares counter-tariffs on C$27.6 billion in American imports for Sept. 8, in response to the U.S. duties imposed in August. The measures cover goods from steel and dairy to appliances, farm equipment, pulp and paper and electronics.

For Canadians who have changed their travel plans, the issue is broader than the price of a Florida hotel or a Las Vegas show. It is whether to direct vacation money to a country whose president has treated Canada as a target for tariffs and political taunts. The tourism industry cannot control that argument, but it has to live with the result.

Analysis: The tourism campaign is a practical reminder that Canada’s response is not confined to government policy. A family choosing Mexico, Europe or a Canadian holiday over a U.S. trip makes no customs declaration and pays no tariff. But repeated millions of times, that decision becomes a real cost for American communities — and another link in the old relationship that may not automatically return.

That is why this will be worth watching through snowbird season. Florida, Arizona and California depend heavily on winter visitors from Canada. If the political chill follows Canadians south through another winter, the consequences will be felt well beyond Washington and Ottawa.
