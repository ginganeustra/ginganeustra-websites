They already wrote the rules: the 1989 Canada–U.S. agreement behind the Lake Ontario fight

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · 10:27 p.m. ET

There is a rulebook for this.

Thirty-seven years before Donald Trump ordered the United States government to call Lake Ontario “Lake America,” Canada and the United States worked out a specific framework for what to do when a geographic feature lies on or crosses their common border. It was designed for lakes, rivers, mountains and other physical features shared by two sovereign countries, and it is still reproduced in the current policy manual of the U.S. Board on Geographic Names.

The document is called the Treatment of Names of Geographical Features Shared by the United States and Canada. The U.S. Board on Geographic Names approved it on Aug. 30, 1989. The Canadian Permanent Committee on Geographic Names approved it on Aug. 11, 1989, and the U.S. secretary of the interior approved it on Nov. 10. In 1992 it was submitted to the United Nations geographical-names conference as a formal Canada–U.S. document of understanding.

The central rule: the 1989 framework says it is “highly desirable to retain established official names.” When a change is contemplated, it says the appropriate naming authorities in both countries should consider it, with local usage and local agreement explicitly built into the process.

What the 1989 document actually says

The agreement is striking because it anticipates almost every issue now raised by Lake Ontario.

First, it begins from the premise that cross-border naming requires coordination. It says coordination over features on or across the Canada–U.S. boundary is mutually beneficial to the naming, mapping and surveying authorities of both countries.

Second, it does not pretend that every shared feature must have one universal name. It acknowledges that Canada and the United States have sometimes developed different names or spellings for the same features. Where those differences reflect different cultural histories or historical perspectives, the document says they should be respected.

Third, when a single common name is practical and “culturally acceptable,” the agreement encourages the Canadian and American naming authorities — federal, provincial or state as appropriate — to work toward one.

And then comes the most relevant passage for the Lake Ontario dispute. On changing an established official name, the document says retaining existing official names is highly desirable. A change can be considered to conform to strong local usage or citizen preference, on a special request supported by strong reasons, or for mutual standardization — but it places that consideration with the appropriate naming authorities in both countries and specifically refers to local agreement.

1989 principle:
Preserve established official names where possible; consult the appropriate authorities on both sides of the border; consider local usage; and recognize that the two countries may ultimately use different official names if agreement cannot be reached.

The framework even provides for failure. If different names are in use on opposite sides of the border and the naming authorities cannot agree on one, each country may make its own name official — or decide not to make either name official at that time.

In other words, the agreement does not give Canada a veto over what the U.S. federal government calls a feature, and it does not give Washington a veto over Canada. What it establishes is a cooperative process for shared geography, coupled with an acceptance that sovereign naming systems can diverge.

Congressional researchers described coordination as mandatory policy

This is not merely an old paper filed away at the United Nations.

In a 2025 examination of geographic naming policy, the non-partisan Congressional Research Service specifically discussed the Canada–U.S. agreement. It said that for features crossing the Canadian border, the Domestic Names Committee’s policy mandates that the U.S. Board on Geographic Names coordinate proposals with the appropriate Canadian naming authority under the 1989 agreement.

That is unusually direct language. It tells us what the ordinary American naming system is supposed to do when a name proposal involves Canada.

But Trump used a different lane

There is an important legal qualification, and it should not be blurred for political effect.

The U.S. Board on Geographic Names manual also says that names established specifically by an act of Congress or by executive order are legally official for U.S. federal purposes and are not bound by the board’s ordinary principles, policies and procedures. The same manual says such names are entered directly into the federal Geographic Names Information System.

Trump’s Aug. 27 order used exactly that route. It directed the secretary of the interior, working with the board, to rename Lake Ontario “Lake America,” update GNIS and remove Lake Ontario references from the federal database. It also ordered federal departments and agencies to use the new name.

That means the careful conclusion is not that the executive order has already been proven to violate the 1989 understanding. The better conclusion is that the order bypassed the ordinary consultative route that the two countries built for transboundary names. The American manual itself distinguishes presidentially established names from the normal board process.

That distinction matters. It prevents the argument from collapsing into slogans about whether Trump “can” rename the lake. For U.S. federal usage, his administration has done so. For Canada, it remains Lake Ontario. Internationally there is no authority forcing Canada or other countries to adopt Washington’s terminology.

The unanswered question

The more concrete question is procedural: what consultation, if any, took place with Canada before the U.S. database was changed?

Canada did not approve the new name and has publicly rejected it. As of Sunday night, Canada at War has found no public evidence that the Geographical Names Board of Canada, Ontario’s geographic-names authority or another Canadian naming body jointly considered or endorsed the change before GNIS was altered.

That does not resolve every legal question. The 1989 document is a bilateral policy framework, not a magic switch that prevents either sovereign government from using a different name. But it does show that the two countries once thought the naming of shared geography was important enough to build a process around restraint, local use, consultation and mutual recognition.

The Lake Ontario fight therefore has a simpler institutional story beneath all the rhetoric.

Canada and the United States already decided how this was supposed to work.

Their 1989 system did not require perfect agreement. It required something more basic: that shared geography be treated as shared geography.
