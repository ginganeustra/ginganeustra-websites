MAGAHEADS: CARNEY ANSWERS TRUMP’S ‘JUST WATCH’ WARNING

Mark Carney rejected the U.S. account of the stalled talks. At 11:14 a.m. ET, Donald Trump warned that making him ‘the enemy’ would end with Canada’s economy collapsing — adding the president’s own threat to a sequence of public pressure from Washington.

Updated Sept. 3, 2026 · 5:32 p.m. ET

Prime Minister Mark Carney answered the Trump administration’s account of the Canada–U.S. trade breakdown in Thunder Bay on Thursday. In response to claims by Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent that Canada abandoned a near-complete agreement for domestic political reasons, Carney said: ‘I don’t think, with all respect, appointed, unelected cabinet members in the United States are experts on Canadian politics.’

Carney said Canada remained ready to make an agreement that served workers, families and businesses on both sides of the border, but said any agreement required stability and credibility. He said U.S. negotiators had pressed Canadian language, culture and other issues until the final hour, and that Washington had since softened on some of them.

Carney also gave a more specific account of the stability issue. The Wall Street Journal reported that Canada had sought assurances that agreed tariff levels on steel, aluminum and automobiles would not be subject to later change. Carney said those assurances were not provided in the August negotiations and that, in some instances, Washington sought language that would have allowed the opposite. The account adds a concrete sector-by-sector explanation to his broader argument that any agreement must be stable and credible.

At 11:14 a.m. ET, Trump posted his own Canada message on Truth Social. The archived post records him writing that it was ‘very good’ for Canadian politicians such as Carney to make him ‘the enemy’ until Canada’s economy collapses. He said the political consequences would then be ‘Worse than anything that has ever happened to a Canadian Politician,’ and ended: ‘Just watch!’

The timestamp places the president’s intervention in the same day’s documented sequence rather than treating it as an undated social-media threat. The archive identifies the original Truth Social status as 117207758594429845 and records the post at 11:14 a.m. EDT; a preserved capture was made two minutes later. Contemporary live coverage also reproduced the message.

Among Trump’s Sept. 3 posts, this was the one that directly addressed Canada and the trade dispute. It moved beyond the administration’s earlier argument about who caused the negotiating breakdown: Trump explicitly tied political opposition to him to a warning that Canada’s economy would collapse.

The post was the clearest presidential warning in the verified public sequence examined in this briefing. It followed repeated appearances in which senior administration figures presented the impasse as Canada’s choice and forecast that Ottawa would have to return on less favourable terms.

On Wednesday, Peter Navarro, the White House trade adviser, used C-SPAN’s Washington Journal to blame ‘politics across our border’ for the failure of the talks and to attack Carney personally. Bessent told Fox News’s Fox & Friends that the dispute had ‘virtually no impact on American prices,’ said an agreement had been on the ‘1-yard line,’ and suggested a later offer would not be as favourable. Lutnick made the same basic case in CNBC and Bloomberg appearances, saying Canada had added late demands and walked away for political reasons.

In recounting one exchange, Lutnick said he asked a Canadian counterpart whether Canada was trying to blow up the deal and claimed the answer was: ‘I’m not allowed to say that.’ No direct Canadian confirmation of that anecdote has been located; it remains Lutnick’s account of the conversation.

Those appearances do not prove a private script or establish that the officials coordinated their language. Their claims, venues and emphasis varied. The documented common thread is a public account that assigns responsibility to Ottawa, treats U.S. economic pressure as sustainable, and predicts a Canadian return. Trump’s post added an overt warning about the consequences of resisting that account.

Trump’s closing words also carry a Canadian historical echo, although the reference should be stated accurately. ‘Just watch me’ was Pierre Trudeau’s answer in October 1970, during the FLQ Crisis, when a CBC reporter asked how far he would go to stop the Front de libération du Québec. It was not a statement by Justin Trudeau. The phrase became shorthand for the elder Trudeau’s resolve during a domestic security emergency; Trump used a different formulation in a trade dispute more than five decades later.

Carney’s response was not a declaration that talks were over. He said Canada was ready to sit down when the Americans were ready. The open question is whether the administration’s public pressure will precede a revised offer, further tariff action, or another round of negotiation.
