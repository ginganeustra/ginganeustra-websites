NATO’S HALIFAX LAB PUTS CANADIAN DEFENCE TECH NEAR THE FRONT OF THE QUEUE

GLOW is not an arms show. It is the integration stage where NATO tries to find out whether emerging systems can actually work together — while Ottawa is simultaneously opening a faster procurement route for Canadian drone and autonomous-system companies.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 11:21 a.m. ET · Updated 1:32 p.m. ET

NATO’s three-day Innovation Continuum GLOW event opened Tuesday at COVE in Dartmouth, Nova Scotia, turning Halifax harbour into part of the Alliance’s pipeline for moving experimental technology toward military use.

The event runs Sept. 1 to 3 and is led by NATO Allied Command Transformation. NATO describes GLOW as the integration phase of its 2026 Innovation Continuum: systems are brought together, physically integrated and put through dry runs before the live experimentation and demonstration phase, SHINE, returns to Halifax in October.

The 2026 technology priorities are unusually relevant to Canadian geography and industry: Arctic intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance; artificial intelligence; layered counter-drone systems; next-generation targeting; smart logistics and sustainment; electronic warfare; AI-enabled command-and-control and ISR; cognitive warfare; and cyber resilience.

The interesting Canadian story is not simply that NATO officials are meeting in Nova Scotia. It is that a domestic defence-tech ecosystem is growing around the same harbour where NATO is trying to shorten the distance between prototype and procurement.

Canada says 22 of the 150 companies selected for NATO DIANA’s 2026 cohort are Canadian — the largest national group. COVE itself is a DIANA accelerator site, and Canada hosts 16 of the Alliance’s more than 200 DIANA test centres. The Department of National Defence says DIANA’s Rapid Adoption Service can allow Allied governments to move into research, prototype or production contracts with innovators without the usual lengthy competitive process.

That matters for small Canadian firms because defence procurement has historically favoured large incumbents with the staff, capital and patience to survive long purchasing cycles.

Nova Scotia already has companies working in precisely the kinds of fields NATO is emphasizing. Federal defence-industrial funding announced this year backed Sensor Technology Ltd. for AI-enabled sensing; GALAXIA Mission Systems for space and mission technology; Marine Thinking for autonomous marine systems; AML Oceanographic for ocean sensing; and Leeway Marine as it develops a new unmanned surface vessel. Other Dartmouth investments support GSTS, which works in maritime intelligence and vessel-risk systems, and MilAero Electronics Atlantic, which builds high-performance electrical assemblies for aerospace, defence and marine customers.

Those firms should not be confused with a confirmed GLOW participant list. NATO’s public event material available Tuesday identifies the event, location and technology themes but does not publish a complete roster of the companies whose systems are being integrated this week. Canada at War is therefore not claiming those named Nova Scotia firms are participating in GLOW.

The distinction is important — and so is the opportunity. Canada is spending heavily to build sovereign capabilities in aerospace, robotics, autonomous systems and other defence technologies while simultaneously trying to reduce reliance on American suppliers. A NATO testing and adoption network located in Canada offers domestic companies a possible route into a market much larger than the Canadian Armed Forces alone.

It can also work in the other direction. Allied technology can be tested in Canadian conditions, integrated with Canadian systems and potentially adopted without every capability having to originate in the United States.

A second Canadian procurement pipeline is now taking shape alongside that NATO innovation network. On Tuesday, InDro Robotics announced that it had been selected as a qualified supplier for Canada’s new Defence Drone Initiative Marketplace. The government’s own CanadaBuys record confirms InDro Robotics Inc. as one of the suppliers admitted to the arrangement.

The marketplace is designed as a pre-qualified pool for uncrewed and autonomous systems, counter-drone capabilities, communications and data systems, engineering and integration, testing, training and experimentation. National Defence says the initiative is intended to strengthen domestic capacity, reduce critical supply-chain dependencies and give Canadian companies a faster route to military requirements.

CanadaBuys lists 142 related award notices under the Defence Drone Initiative supply arrangement. Those notices should not be read as 142 major drone purchases: many of the initial arrangements, including InDro’s, carry a nominal value of $1. The important point is access. Once qualified, companies can compete for subsequent research, prototype, testing, demonstration and potential production work.

That distinction is already becoming concrete. A separate federal solicitation for low-cost uncrewed aircraft systems was restricted to qualified suppliers in Stream 1 of the Defence Drone Initiative Marketplace, with bids due Sept. 1. In other words, admission to the marketplace can become the gate through which companies must pass before they can compete for actual military requirements.

InDro provides a useful example of the kind of Canadian technology Ottawa is trying to pull through that gate. The company says it has submitted proposals involving long-range remotely piloted aircraft and ground robotics, including work to robotize an extreme-range hybrid-electric all-wheel-drive vehicle for remote operation. It also describes a long-range ISR aircraft designed for Arctic temperatures. Separately, Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada lists InDro military technologies including Talus, an autonomous mixed-terrain electric platform designed for GPS-denied and degraded-communications environments, and the hydrogen-fuel-cell Obsidian Defense Tracker ground robot.

Qualification is not a purchase order, a production contract or a guarantee that any particular InDro system will be bought by the Canadian Armed Forces. But that is precisely why the supplier pool deserves scrutiny. The question is no longer only which Canadian prototypes appear at demonstrations. It is which Canadian companies get through the procurement gate, which receive funded orders, who owns their intellectual property and supply chains, and whether successful firms remain Canadian-controlled as they scale.

That makes Halifax worth watching after GLOW closes. The real measure will not be how many prototypes are demonstrated. It will be how many Canadian technologies survive integration, reach SHINE in October, win prototype or production contracts and remain Canadian-owned as they scale.
