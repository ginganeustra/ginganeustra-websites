THE CHINA RISK IS REAL. SO IS THE AMERICAN ONE.

An Enverus warning about Chinese technical-support dependencies points to a larger Canadian sovereignty problem: Washington also has legal power over technology Canada may depend on.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 5:44 p.m. ET

The image above is an AI-generated editorial illustration.

A new warning from Enverus Intelligence Research says China’s latest rules governing overseas investment could expose a weak point in North American power systems. The risk, Enverus argues, is not necessarily that Chinese-made batteries or solar inverters stop crossing the border. It is that equipment already installed may depend on engineering, software, diagnostics, training or remote technical support that remains tied to Chinese suppliers — and Beijing has strengthened the legal framework governing some of those relationships.

That is a legitimate concern. China’s State Council Order No. 837 took effect July 1 and explicitly places national sovereignty, security and development interests inside the framework for managing overseas investment. Enverus says the rules could add delay risk for U.S. projects that depend on Chinese-linked engineering, training and remote technical guidance, with battery storage and solar inverters among the areas with the greatest additional exposure. The company does not claim that China has already shut down North American projects, and its public summary acknowledges that existing American restrictions remain larger barriers to Chinese participation in the U.S. power market.

The important insight is broader than China. Modern infrastructure is not merely a collection of machines that arrive at a site and operate independently for decades. A battery system can depend on proprietary software, firmware, remote monitoring, replacement parts and engineers who understand its control architecture. An inverter can require updates and manufacturer support long after installation. Buying the box does not necessarily mean acquiring the independent capacity to keep the capability operating.

Once that principle is accepted, however, there is no logical reason to apply it only to Chinese technology. The United States also maintains extensive controls over strategic technology, software, technical data and expertise. Under the U.S. Export Administration Regulations, U.S.-origin items can remain subject to American export-control rules after they leave the country, and certain foreign-made products can also fall within those rules because of controlled U.S. content or technology. Washington therefore possesses legal mechanisms that can affect foreign access to American technology well beyond the original sale.

Canada is treated very differently from an American adversary. It benefits from a dense alliance relationship, regulatory exemptions and privileged access in many sectors. Those advantages are real and materially reduce risk. But an exemption granted under American law is still a permission created under American law. It is not the same thing as Canadian control over the underlying technology.

A striking example appeared this summer in artificial intelligence. Anthropic said the U.S. government imposed export controls on its Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models on June 12, requiring it to restrict access by foreign nationals whether they were inside or outside the United States. Anthropic said it did not have a reliable real-time mechanism to determine nationality and temporarily suspended the affected models. The controls were later lifted for Fable, which returned to global availability. Anthropic’s current Fable 5.1 is available internationally, so it would be wrong to say that the company’s latest general-purpose Claude model cannot be used outside the United States.

But Anthropic’s more restricted Mythos 5.1, released Sept. 1, provides the sharper example. The company says it is presently available only to vetted organizations and, for now, only to a set of U.S. organizations while Anthropic works to expand access. Whatever one thinks of the policy rationale, the episode demonstrates something important for Canadian procurement: access to a private American technology product can change rapidly because of a decision made by the U.S. government.

The same structure has existed for decades in other strategic sectors. The U.S. Department of Energy’s Part 810 system governs transfers of certain unclassified nuclear technology and assistance to foreign atomic-energy activities. The rules reach beyond physical equipment to knowledge and expertise. Canada benefits from favourable treatment and general authorizations in many circumstances, but the system reflects the same underlying principle Beijing invokes in its own way: technical knowledge can be considered a national strategic asset rather than an ordinary commercial service.

Defence makes the dependence even easier to see. Canada will own and operate its CF-35A fighters, and the Department of National Defence says Canada controls how its aircraft are used. DND also says partner countries participate in decisions on software updates and that Canada works with Australia and Britain in a reprogramming laboratory that develops mission data. Those facts matter, and public claims that Washington can simply press a secret “kill switch” on Canadian aircraft should not be treated as established fact.

At the same time, the F-35 operates inside an international sustainment system anchored in the United States. DND says the U.S. government owns and manages the global spare-parts pool until components are installed, while hardware and software upgrades are coordinated through the F-35 Joint Program Office in consultation with participating countries. American export law separately restricts defence articles, technical data and defence services. Canada receives important exemptions and special treatment under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, but those arrangements demonstrate that the underlying technology remains controlled.

This does not make China and the United States politically identical, nor does it mean that the probability of interruption is the same. Canada’s treaty relationships, defence integration, legal ties and decades of institutional cooperation with Washington create a very different risk environment from its relationship with Beijing. Those differences belong in any serious assessment. The narrower point is that the structural sovereignty question does not disappear because the supplier is an ally.

The provenance of the Enverus warning also deserves scrutiny. Enverus is not a Chinese-policy watchdog or a government agency. It is a large commercial energy-data and analytics company serving thousands of customers in dozens of countries. Blackstone announced an agreement to acquire it in 2025, describing a company used by more than 8,000 customers and deeply embedded in the U.S. energy industry. Its customers pay for intelligence about project, regulatory, supply-chain and market risk. That gives Enverus a commercial reason to identify vulnerabilities before they become expensive.

Operating inside the North American energy system inevitably shapes the questions Enverus asks. American policy increasingly describes Chinese batteries, inverters, communications equipment and other strategic technologies through the language of national security, reshoring and supply-chain resilience. An energy-risk firm working in that environment will naturally spend substantial effort examining Chinese exposure. That context should be understood rather than ignored.

But the available record does not support dismissing Enverus as an anti-China propaganda operation. Its own work has sometimes emphasized the costs of American restrictions. When the Biden administration raised tariffs on Chinese clean-energy products in 2024, Enverus noted that the measures would add inflationary pressure and underscored continuing U.S. dependence on Chinese manufacturing. Other Enverus analysis has credited Chinese oil buying with supporting global crude markets and has described Washington’s China hawkishness as a market factor rather than simply repeating it as an unquestioned truth. Its research should be read as commercially oriented analysis from within the North American energy-security environment — a lens with interests and assumptions, but not evidence by itself of fabrication.

For Canada, the question raised by the report is therefore not simply whether China can be trusted. It is how much essential Canadian infrastructure can continue operating, be repaired, updated and maintained if access to any foreign supplier is interrupted. Chinese battery and inverter manufacturers have a commercial presence in Canada, but market presence alone does not prove that a particular Canadian grid project is vulnerable to Order No. 837. That depends on contracts, supplier architecture, remote access, software, data, replacement parts and the ability of Canadian operators to work independently.

The same test should be applied to American systems. Who owns the source code? Who controls software updates? Where are the servers and data? Who holds encryption keys? Can Canadian engineers repair the system without foreign permission? Does a contract guarantee access to technical documentation and replacement parts? Could the supplier’s home government legally prevent it from providing support? Can the equipment operate safely if a foreign cloud connection disappears? How long could Canada keep an essential system running if relations with the supplier’s country deteriorated?

Those questions do not imply that Canada should replace American technology with Chinese technology. Simply changing the country on which Canada depends would not produce technological sovereignty. The practical objective is resilience: diversified suppliers where possible, independent maintenance capability, contractual access to documentation and software, domestic engineering depth, stockpiles of critical components and procurement rules that account for political dependency alongside price and performance.

Order No. 837 is therefore worth taking seriously. Enverus is reasonable to examine how Chinese controls over technology, services and technical personnel could affect projects that assume support will always be available. But the warning becomes much more useful to Canada when the same analytical standard is applied consistently.

China has legal tools to control strategic technology in the name of Chinese national interests. The United States has legal tools to control strategic technology in the name of American national interests. The alliance relationship makes those dependencies different in degree, context and probability, but it does not erase the underlying fact that another government may possess leverage over systems Canada considers essential.

The sovereign test is not whether the foreign flag over the supplier’s headquarters belongs to a friend or a rival. It is whether Canada has a workable fallback when somebody else controls something essential. If another government can decide whether Canada is allowed to keep using a critical capability, Canada has a dependency worth measuring.
