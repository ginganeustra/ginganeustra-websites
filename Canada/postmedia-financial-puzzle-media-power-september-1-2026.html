POSTMEDIA'S FINANCIAL PUZZLE: MONEY, OWNERSHIP AND MEDIA POWER

The public record shows years of losses, debt restructuring and a major U.S. hedge-fund role in one of Canada's most influential newspaper systems. It does not show that an owner ordered a particular headline. Both facts matter.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 3:04 p.m. ET · Rewritten and expanded for legal and factual precision

What this story is — and is not — saying: Canada at War is examining documented ownership, financing, political connections and editorial power. We are not alleging that Chatham Asset Management or any other owner dictates individual Postmedia stories, that every Postmedia journalist shares one political agenda, or that Postmedia is secretly acting on instructions from Donald Trump or the U.S. government. The public evidence reviewed here does not establish those claims.

Postmedia is one of the most powerful media institutions in Canada. Its network includes the National Post, major metropolitan dailies, Sun newspapers and a large collection of local and digital brands. That reach gives the company an unusual ability to influence which issues are elevated, which arguments receive repeated exposure and what political language becomes familiar across much of the country.

That would make its ownership and financial structure worth examining in ordinary times. It matters even more during an intense Canada–United States confrontation in which trade, sovereignty and the political relationship with Washington have become central national questions.

The starting point is not ideology. It is the balance sheet.

A newspaper empire that has repeatedly lost money

Canada at War reviewed Postmedia's annual reports through fiscal 2024. Since the company's creation in 2010, the record is dominated by losses rather than a conventional story of investors buying a newspaper chain, restoring profitability and collecting growing returns from the operating business.

The striking exception was fiscal 2021, when Postmedia reported a $33.7-million net profit. But that result included a $63.1-million non-cash pension-settlement gain. In fiscal 2022 the company reported a net loss of $74.7 million; in 2023, $72.6 million; and in 2024, $49.7 million. At Aug. 31, 2024, Postmedia's accumulated deficit stood at approximately $1.162 billion.

Those figures do not mean investors necessarily received no economic benefit. Creditors can collect interest. Debt can be bought below face value. Refinancings, exchanges, asset sales and restructurings can preserve or create value in ways that do not resemble an ordinary shareholder dividend. The complete private return of a creditor or hedge fund cannot be reconstructed from public filings alone.

What the filings do establish is narrower: Postmedia has not been a steadily profitable newspaper business.

The company was born from a collapse

Postmedia itself was created in 2010 from the newspaper assets of Canwest after Canwest's financial collapse. Many of the best-known newspaper properties in that group had entered Canwest a decade earlier, when it acquired substantially all of Hollinger's Canadian newspaper and internet assets in 2000.

That history matters because the institution Canadians now know as Postmedia did not emerge from a greenfield media startup. It inherited a national newspaper infrastructure — mastheads, newsrooms, printing operations, distribution systems and political influence — that had accumulated over generations.

The assets survived even as ownership structures and financing changed dramatically.

Chatham arrived through debt

One of the most important financial actors in Postmedia's later history is Chatham Asset Management, a New Jersey investment firm founded by Anthony Melchiorre.

In October 2016, Chatham disclosed that it had exchanged about US$178.9 million in Postmedia debt for 61.2 million variable-voting shares, representing roughly 65 per cent of that class at the time. The transaction was part of a recapitalization intended to reduce Postmedia's debt burden.

The distinction between economic ownership and legal voting control is important in Canada. Canadian ownership rules and Postmedia's share structure limit how voting rights can be exercised. It would therefore be inaccurate to describe the company simply as an American corporation or to collapse the share classes into one uncomplicated claim of foreign control.

It is accurate to say that Chatham became a central financial force in Postmedia through distressed debt and a debt-for-equity exchange.

Why the American political context is relevant

Ownership becomes politically interesting because Chatham's media and political history extends beyond Canada.

Public reporting cited by Canada at War shows that Melchiorre and his wife donated more than US$100,000 to Republican candidates and party committees over a period beginning in 2010. That fact should be stated carefully: donations to Republican candidates and organizations are not, by themselves, evidence that Melchiorre personally directed Postmedia coverage or that Postmedia publications received instructions from Republican politicians.

Chatham also controlled American Media Inc., the publisher of the National Enquirer, during the 2016 U.S. election. The U.S. Department of Justice later described American Media's admission that it made a US$150,000 payment in concert with a presidential campaign and principally to prevent damaging allegations from influencing the election. Reporting by the Los Angeles Times also placed Melchiorre at a White House dinner with Donald Trump and American Media chief executive David Pecker after Trump became president.

Those facts make the political network relevant. They still do not prove that a Postmedia headline in Canada was ordered by Chatham or that Canadian newsrooms are puppets of a U.S. owner.

The distinction is essential.

Influence does not require a secret memo

The strongest reason to examine a national newspaper chain is not the fantasy of a proprietor telephoning an editor every morning with a list of approved headlines.

Institutions shape political life more subtly than that.

They decide where resources go. They hire senior editors and executives. They determine how many reporters remain in a newsroom. They choose which commentary is syndicated. They establish priorities, cost structures, product strategies and the boundaries within which local publications operate. Over time, those decisions can affect the range and repetition of ideas reaching the public even when individual reporters work independently and conscientiously.

Postmedia newsrooms contain journalists who do serious reporting. Local mastheads have their own histories and professional cultures. A story about ownership should not erase those people or pretend every article has one source of political motivation.

But the opposite error would be to pretend ownership and institutional structure are irrelevant simply because no one can produce a memo saying, “write this headline.”

Media power is often structural.

The business of consolidation

Postmedia's survival has repeatedly involved cost-cutting, newsroom reductions, publication closures, reduced print schedules, centralized functions and consolidation. Its 2015 acquisition of Sun Media's English-language newspaper operations dramatically expanded the chain and left the same company operating titles that had once competed more independently in several major Canadian markets.

There is a defensible argument that consolidation kept some publications alive that might otherwise have disappeared. There is also a cost: fewer separately financed newsrooms mean fewer genuinely independent institutional centres making decisions about what to investigate, emphasize and challenge.

The public-interest question is therefore more complicated than “Postmedia good” or “Postmedia bad.”

It is this: what happens when a financially fragile but politically important newspaper system becomes concentrated under a small number of financial interests?

A platform can have value beyond annual profit

A national newspaper chain has assets that do not appear neatly as a line called “political influence” on an income statement.

It reaches voters, politicians, corporate executives, public servants and opinion leaders. It can help set agendas. It can make certain arguments seem mainstream through repetition. It can decide which disputes receive sustained attention and which are treated as marginal.

That does not mean Postmedia's owners necessarily maintain the company because of political influence. Canada at War has not found public evidence that proves such a motive, and we do not state it as fact.

It does mean the value of a national media platform cannot be understood solely from its annual net income.

Today's National Post headline is a useful example — not proof

On Aug. 31, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent went on CNBC and mocked Canada's ability to resist the United States, saying the American economy was 13 times larger and joking about Canada using “two submarines from the Edmonton Mall.”

The National Post promoted the story with the headline: “U.S. Treasury secretary taunts Canadian navy's lack of firepower: ‘Two submarines from the Edmonton mall.’”

Canada at War's separate Media Watch analysis argues that the framing deserves scrutiny because the humiliation itself became the promotional hook.

But that headline is not evidence that Chatham ordered anything. It does not prove coordination with Washington. It should not be used as a shortcut around the burden of evidence.

It is useful for a different reason: it demonstrates why editorial choices matter. Bessent supplied the insult. The newspaper chose which part of his remarks to elevate. Readers are entitled to examine that choice in the larger context of a company with enormous reach, a complicated financial history and significant U.S. ownership ties.

The question Canada should keep asking

For more than two decades, the newspaper infrastructure first assembled under Canwest and then carried through Postmedia has survived debt crises, collapsing print advertising, restructuring and repeated losses.

The mastheads remain influential.

That endurance deserves serious examination without conspiracy theories and without euphemism.

The careful conclusion is not that every Postmedia story is propaganda. The evidence does not support such a statement.

The careful conclusion is that ownership, financing, consolidation and political networks matter when evaluating any powerful media institution — especially one helping frame Canada's relationship with a much larger country that is currently applying economic and political pressure.

Canadians do not need to assume hidden orders to ask who owns their newspapers, how those newspapers survive, what interests surround their owners and how editorial choices shape the political atmosphere.

Those are ordinary democratic questions.

During a sovereignty crisis, they become necessary ones.
