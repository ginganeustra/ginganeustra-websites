CANADA AT WAR
Political endurance · August 26, 2026

Carney has support now. The economic clock is running.

Canada's confrontation with Washington has produced unusually strong political cohesion. The next test is whether that support survives a prolonged period of tariffs, factory pressure and higher costs.

Prime Minister Mark Carney's decision to walk away from the failed trade talks and retaliate against U.S. tariffs continues to command substantial domestic support. The immediate political effect has been to frame the dispute as a question of sovereignty and national leverage rather than a conventional tariff negotiation.

That advantage is real, but it is not permanent. Economists and industry analysts warn that a prolonged dispute could eventually produce concentrated damage in auto manufacturing, metals, forestry and other export-dependent sectors. If layoffs, plant slowdowns or consumer price increases become more visible, the government's political room for manoeuvre could narrow.

Diversification takes time

Carney's broader strategy is to reduce Canada's dependence on the U.S. market by deepening ties with Europe and other partners. That can improve Canada's long-term leverage, but new trade relationships, procurement arrangements and industrial investment do not replace integrated North American demand overnight.

The result is a race between two clocks: the time required to build credible alternatives and the time Canadian workers and firms can absorb tariff damage.

Bottom line: Carney enters that race from a position of political strength. The question is whether strategic diversification begins producing measurable economic gains before prolonged disruption starts eroding the unity that made the hard line possible.
