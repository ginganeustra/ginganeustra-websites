REPUBLICANS SHUT CBC OUT OF MIDTERM CONVENTION

RNC revokes accreditation for CBC/Radio-Canada as tensions with Canada spread from trade and diplomacy into the media.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 12:49 a.m. ET

The Republican Party has revoked the press credentials of CBC/Radio-Canada for its coming midterm convention in Dallas, pushing a dispute involving Canada’s public broadcaster into the centre of an American political event.

CBC head of public affairs Chuck Thompson confirmed Monday that the Republican National Committee would no longer provide accreditation for CBC/Radio-Canada journalists at the Sept. 9-10 convention. He called the decision “disappointing” and said the broadcaster hoped it would be reconsidered. CBC reported that convention organizers had not immediately responded to its request for an explanation.

The decision follows days of Republican criticism of CBC over the broadcaster’s language guidance concerning the Sept. 11, 2001 attacks. A leaked internal memo drew attention to longstanding CBC guidance that generally advised journalists not to describe acts as terrorism without attribution. Republican lawmakers and other U.S. officials attacked the policy after it became public.

CBC subsequently changed the guidance. Basem Boshra, the broadcaster’s senior director of journalistic standards and public trust, said the organization would make clear that journalists do not require direct attribution when describing the Sept. 11 attacks as terrorism. CBC said its previous approach was about consistent language standards, not a denial of the nature of the attacks.

National Post reported that the RNC specifically cited the controversy when it withdrew CBC and Radio-Canada’s previously approved credentials. The revocation therefore came even after CBC clarified its policy.

The convention itself is an unusually prominent midterm event. President Donald Trump and Vice-President JD Vance are scheduled to headline the two-day gathering, which Republicans are using to promote their candidates and national message heading into the November elections.

The dispute also raises a broader press-access question. Political parties control credentials for their own conventions, and accreditation is not the same thing as a legal right to enter a private political event. But denying access to a major foreign public broadcaster because party officials object to its journalism turns that administrative power into a political sanction.

CBC can still report on the Republican Party from outside the convention. What the RNC controls is direct access to speeches, delegates, officials and the activity inside the hall.

Against the backdrop of the wider Canada-U.S. confrontation, that distinction matters. The two countries are already fighting over tariffs, industrial policy and sovereignty. Now a Canadian national broadcaster has become part of the conflict.
