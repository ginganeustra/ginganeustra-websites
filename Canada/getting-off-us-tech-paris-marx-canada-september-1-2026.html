HOW TO GET OFF AMERICAN TECH — AND WHAT TO USE INSTEAD

Paris Marx’s guide to non-U.S. technology is surging after the Lake America map fight. Here is a practical Canadian version — from email and search to maps, office software, phones, hosting, streaming and AI.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 4:25 a.m. ET

Donald Trump’s decision to rename Lake Ontario “Lake America” has turned an argument about digital sovereignty into something Canadians can see on a phone screen. Google Maps adopted the new U.S. federal name for American users. That, in turn, sent readers back to a practical guide maintained by St. John’s writer and podcaster Paris Marx: a catalogue of ways to move everyday digital life away from American technology companies.

Marx, host of Tech Won’t Save Us and author of the Disconnect newsletter, began the project before the lake-name fight. His point is larger than one president or one map. Canada has allowed a remarkable amount of its communications, documents, searches, cloud storage, maps, phones and business infrastructure to sit inside systems controlled by a handful of U.S. corporations. A consumer cannot undo that architecture alone. But consumers and businesses can reduce how many points of dependency they have — and governments can make those choices easier by building alternatives.

This is a practical Canada at War guide to the strongest non-U.S. options in Marx’s catalogue, checked against the companies and projects themselves. It is not a claim that every service below is perfectly independent of American infrastructure. In fact, that caveat is central to the story: a European or Canadian app can still rent servers from Amazon, Microsoft or Google. Digital sovereignty is a supply-chain problem as much as an app-store problem.

Email, cloud storage and the easiest first move


PROTON MAIL · SWITZERLAND

For an ordinary household, email is probably the cleanest place to start. Marx’s own anchor is Proton, the Swiss privacy company whose suite now includes Proton Mail, Drive, Calendar, Pass, VPN, Docs and other tools. One account can therefore replace pieces of Gmail, Google Drive, Google Calendar, Google Docs and some security utilities without forcing a user to assemble six unrelated products.

There are other credible choices. Tuta and Posteo are German email providers. Infomaniak is Swiss and offers a broader cloud-and-productivity suite. Zoho is an Indian company with mail, office and business tools. Mailbox.org is German. Fastmail is Australian, though users who are trying to minimize U.S. infrastructure should still examine where any service hosts the particular data they care about.

The practical rule is not “find a perfect flag.” It is to ask three questions: Who owns the service? Under whose law does it operate? And whose infrastructure does it depend on underneath?

Web browsers: leaving Chrome without making the web painful


VIVALDI · NORWAY

Marx has settled on Vivaldi, developed by a Norwegian company founded by former Opera executives. It is deliberately aimed at people who want a full-featured browser rather than a stripped-down privacy experiment, and it synchronizes across major desktop and mobile platforms. For many users, that makes it a more realistic replacement for Chrome, Edge or Safari than a technically demanding browser that requires constant tinkering.

There is an important limitation: Vivaldi is built on Chromium, the open-source browser engine that also underpins Chrome and many competitors. Moving to Vivaldi changes the company that provides the browser and its services, but it does not completely escape an ecosystem whose technical direction is heavily influenced by Google. Firefox-based LibreWolf is a more radical alternative, but Marx notes that it is less friendly for ordinary users. The lesson repeats throughout this guide: independence comes in degrees.

Search: Qwant, Mojeek and Europe’s unfinished index


QWANT · FRANCE

Search is harder because Google’s advantage is not merely a website; it is the enormous index underneath it. Marx currently rates France’s Qwant as his best non-U.S. choice. Germany’s Ecosia and the Netherlands’ Startpage are alternatives, while Britain’s Mojeek is notable for operating its own search index.

Qwant and Ecosia began serving some results from a shared European search index in 2025. That project matters strategically because a search service that simply resells results from Microsoft Bing or Google has changed the storefront without eliminating the dependency. Marx says he now trusts Qwant with more searches than he did when he began his experiment, but still returns to Google for some difficult queries. That is useful honesty: migration does not have to be absolute to reduce dependence.

Maps: the category the Lake America fight made political


ORGANIC MAPS · ESTONIA

Maps are where this suddenly stopped feeling abstract. Marx’s strongest general-purpose non-U.S. option is HERE WeGo, operated by the Netherlands-based mapping company HERE Technologies. TomTom Go, also Dutch, is strong for driving. Canada’s Transit is built around public transportation. For walking, cycling and offline navigation, the OpenStreetMap ecosystem opens another route entirely.

Organic Maps, based in Estonia, packages OpenStreetMap data in a simple offline-friendly app. OsmAnd is another OpenStreetMap-based option associated with the Netherlands. Mapy is Czech. Magic Earth is Dutch. None necessarily reproduces every Google Maps feature, review database or business listing, but they prove that navigation itself does not require a Google or Apple account.

For Canadians irritated that a geopolitical decision in Washington can flow through an American platform and alter what millions of people see on a map, keeping a second mapping app is a particularly easy form of redundancy.

Documents and office work: the Microsoft problem is solvable


LIBREOFFICE · EUROPE

Microsoft Office and Google Workspace are deeply embedded in Canadian institutions, but basic document work has many alternatives. LibreOffice, backed by the Germany-based Document Foundation, remains the most obvious installed desktop suite. It handles word processing, spreadsheets and presentations without requiring a cloud account.

For collaboration, the picture is broader. Nextcloud, developed by a German company and open-source community, can provide file storage, calendars, contacts, calls and collaborative editing when paired with office tools. Collabora is based in Britain. Proton has been adding web-based documents and spreadsheets. Zoho provides a mature Indian alternative. CryptPad, developed in France, emphasizes encrypted collaboration.

Compatibility matters more here than ideology. A business that exchanges complex Excel models or heavily formatted Word files with American clients may not be able to leave Microsoft overnight. It can still move internal notes, routine documents, archives and some collaboration elsewhere. A staged migration reduces operational risk.

Passwords, authentication and personal security

Marx moved his password manager and two-factor authentication codes into Proton’s suite. Other non-U.S. options include KeePass, an open-source project with roots in Germany, and Aegis for Android, developed in the Netherlands. Canada’s 1Password deserves a special asterisk: it is Canadian, but Marx says the company told him it uses Amazon Web Services and did not plan to migrate away. That is exactly why nationality alone is not enough to answer the sovereignty question.

Security software is one category where a rushed political switch would be foolish. Export credentials carefully, confirm the new vault works on every device, save recovery material, then delete the old account only after the migration is proven.

Phones and hardware: the hardest dependency


FAIRPHONE · NETHERLANDS

Hardware is more difficult because a phone is an ecosystem, not just a slab of electronics. Marx points to Fairphone, the Dutch manufacturer known for repairable devices and longer support. Fairphone can also be paired with de-Googled Android options for users who want to reduce Google’s role further.

Other hardware makers outside the United States include HMD in Finland, Sony in Japan, Asus and Acer in Taiwan, Lenovo in China, and Samsung and LG in South Korea. Kobo, founded in Canada and now owned by Japan’s Rakuten, is an alternative to Amazon’s Kindle. Norway’s reMarkable competes in digital notebooks.

But even a non-American handset can contain U.S.-designed chips, U.S. intellectual property, American apps or Google services. Hardware shows why the serious policy conversation has to include semiconductor design, cloud computing, operating systems and procurement — not just consumer branding.

Messaging and social media: sometimes the network wins

This is where Marx’s experiment becomes deliberately pragmatic. He has acknowledged continuing to use some American services because the people he needs to reach are there. Signal, WhatsApp and Discord cannot be replaced merely by installing a technically superior app if everyone in a family, workplace or community remains on the old network.

Mastodon, whose decentralized network has strong European roots, offers a different model for social media. But the realistic strategy may be to add alternatives before deleting incumbents: build contacts elsewhere, move communities gradually, and make sure no single platform is the only way people can reach you.

Video calls, translation, design and the long tail

Norway’s Whereby offers browser-based video meetings, while Jitsi and Nextcloud provide open-source paths. For translation, Germany’s DeepL is the obvious heavyweight alternative to Google Translate; France’s Reverso and South Korea’s Papago cover other needs. For graphics, Britain’s Affinity competes with Adobe’s creative tools, Australia’s Canva has become a mass-market design platform, and GIMP remains an open-source image editor.

Money transfer offers another familiar non-U.S. name: Wise is British. For hosting, Marx moved his own sites to FullHost in Canada. For cloud storage, Canada’s Sync.com, Switzerland’s pCloud and Tresorit, and Proton Drive provide alternatives. Podcast publishers can look at Britain’s Captivate; independent publishers can examine Ghost, headquartered in Singapore. France’s Dailymotion and the decentralized PeerTube project provide non-U.S. video options.

Streaming and media: Canada already has part of the answer

American streaming services dominate television and music, but this is one area where Canadians already have domestic choices. CBC Gem, Crave and the National Film Board sit alongside non-U.S. services such as France’s Deezer, Britain’s Mubi and BritBox. No single service reproduces Netflix, Spotify or YouTube, but the practical question is whether every entertainment subscription needs to send money and viewing data through the same national ecosystem.

AI: there is no clean substitution yet

Artificial intelligence is the newest and perhaps most strategically sensitive layer. Europe has AI companies such as France’s Mistral, Canada has important research institutions and firms, and open-weight models can be run through non-U.S. infrastructure. But the leading consumer AI market is intertwined with American cloud providers, chip designers, model companies and app platforms. Anyone promising a simple national substitute is skipping several layers of the stack.

For governments and businesses, this is where procurement matters. Sensitive Canadian data can be required to remain under Canadian or allied jurisdiction; workloads can be diversified among providers; open standards can reduce lock-in; and contracts can include portability requirements so switching is technically possible before a crisis makes it urgent.

The point is not purity. It is leverage.

Marx’s guide is most useful when read as a map of dependencies rather than a shopping list. A Canadian can switch Gmail to Proton, Chrome to Vivaldi, Google Search to Qwant, Google Maps to HERE or Organic Maps, Microsoft Office to LibreOffice for some work, and a conventional Android phone to Fairphone. That can meaningfully reduce exposure to a handful of U.S. corporations. It cannot create sovereign infrastructure by itself.

The deeper task belongs to Ottawa, provinces, universities and Canadian business. Public procurement can support domestic hosting and open standards. Governments can insist that critical records remain portable. Canadian cloud and cybersecurity capacity can be treated as infrastructure rather than a boutique sector. Europe can be a partner rather than merely another market. And consumers should be able to choose a non-U.S. service without discovering that it secretly sits on the same American hyperscale cloud.

The Lake America episode provided a vivid demonstration of why this matters. A political decision in Washington did not remain inside a federal register. It travelled through a private American technology platform and became part of the digital reality presented to users. Digital sovereignty is the ability to decide that Canada should have another route.
