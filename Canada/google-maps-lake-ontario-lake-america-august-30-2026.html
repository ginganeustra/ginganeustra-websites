Google Maps adopts ‘Lake America’ for U.S. users. In Canada, it is still Lake Ontario.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · 2:15 p.m. ET

Google Maps has begun displaying “Lake America” to users in the United States after Donald Trump directed U.S. federal agencies to use that name for Lake Ontario. The company says the change follows an update to the U.S. Geographic Names Information System, the federal database it uses for U.S. place names.

For people using Google Maps in Canada, the lake remains labelled Lake Ontario. Google says users outside the United States and Canada will see both names. The differing labels are a technical expression of a political dispute, not a change to the lake itself, Canada’s border, or Canada’s authority over its own geographic names.

The practical answer: Google has changed what its U.S. audience sees. It has not renamed the lake in Canada. A U.S. executive order cannot compel Canada, international bodies or private mapmakers to accept the American label.

What Google changed—and what it did not

Google’s explanation is unusually direct. It says Maps reflects name changes in official government sources and that the U.S. database now uses “Lake America.” That approach is comparable to the one it used after the Trump administration changed the U.S. federal name for the Gulf of Mexico.

Google has not applied a single global replacement. Its stated system is location-based: U.S. users see Lake America; Canadian users see Lake Ontario; users elsewhere see Lake Ontario with the U.S. name alongside it. Apple Maps continued to show Lake Ontario in checks reported Sunday, while MapQuest said it would not change its label.

That distinction matters. Google’s decision is not evidence that an international geographic authority has accepted a new name. It is a private company’s decision to mirror the federal naming preference in the jurisdiction of a particular user.

Trump’s order has a narrow legal reach

Trump signed the order on Aug. 27, directing federal agencies to adopt “Lake America” in U.S. maps, documents and databases. A president can direct the executive branch’s terminology. The order does not give Washington power over the Canadian portion of the lake, Canadian maps, Canadian law, or the way international organizations describe a shared body of water.

Lake Ontario is bordered by Ontario on the north and New York State on the south. Its name has deep Canadian and Indigenous roots: “Ontario” comes from a Wendat term commonly rendered as “lake of shining waters.” Ontario Premier Doug Ford answered the U.S. action with a large bilingual sign declaring: “Lake Ontario. Now and Always.”

Prime Minister Mark Carney has rejected the American attempt to impose a different name. The federal government’s position is straightforward: Canada has not changed the name, and the United States cannot make that decision for Canada.

Why a map label has become a sovereignty story

It would be easy to dismiss this as a childish symbolic stunt. It is also a clear demonstration of how the current U.S. administration is using federal power to turn a trade and diplomatic conflict into a contest over symbols, language and public reality.

The name order arrived during a worsening Canada–U.S. dispute over tariffs. It does not alter trade rules or shipping routes. But it does ask Americans to see a shared Great Lake through a unilateral national frame—and it invites Canadian officials, businesses and citizens to react. That is why it belongs in the broader sovereignty story: not because Google Maps can redraw a border, but because symbols are being deliberately used as pressure.

Canada’s response should remain proportionate and precise. There is no need to pretend that a U.S. map label has erased a Canadian place. It has not. But there is value in recording the difference between a federal U.S. database, a corporate map interface and the enduring Canadian name of the lake. The latter is Lake Ontario.
