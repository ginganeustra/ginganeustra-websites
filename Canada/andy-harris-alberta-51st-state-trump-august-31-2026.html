Meet Andy Harris, the congressman who wants Alberta as the 51st state

He is not a random television provocateur. He chairs the House Freedom Caucus, sits on Appropriations and says Donald Trump is “taking advantage” of western Canadian alienation. His record makes the Alberta remark worth taking seriously — without pretending it is U.S. government policy.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 5:05 p.m. ET · Reporting & analysis

There are congressional backbenchers, and then there is Andy Harris. On Monday morning, the Maryland Republican went on Newsmax and suggested that if Alberta leaves Canada, the United States should make it the 51st state. “Tremendous oil reserves there,” he said. He then offered a diagnosis of western Canada: “The socialism doesn’t work in Western Canada. A lot of those provinces don’t want to be associated with Ottawa. And the president’s taking advantage of it.”

The first obligation is not to inflate the remark. Harris did not announce an American annexation policy. He was speaking on partisan television, and neither the White House nor Congress has announced a plan to absorb Alberta. But neither is Harris simply a man who wandered in from the parking lot. He is an eight-term member of Congress, chairman of the House Freedom Caucus and a member of the powerful House Appropriations Committee. When someone in that position describes a neighbouring country’s internal political division as something the American president is “taking advantage of,” Canadians are entitled to pay attention.

Harris, 69, was born in Brooklyn to parents who fled communist Eastern Europe after the Second World War. He trained as a physician at Johns Hopkins, practised anesthesiology for nearly three decades and served 17 years in the U.S. Naval Reserve, including during Operation Desert Shield and Desert Storm. He spent 12 years in the Maryland Senate before winning a seat in Congress in 2010. In 2024, his colleagues elected him chairman of the Freedom Caucus, the hard-right Republican bloc that has repeatedly exerted leverage over House leadership.

A record Canadians may find familiar

Harris has spent years near the sharp edge of American conservative politics. After the 2020 presidential election, he voted to object to electoral votes from Arizona and Pennsylvania even after the Capitol had been attacked on Jan. 6, 2021. During the overnight House debate, the Washington Post reported that Harris became involved in a heated confrontation on the floor that nearly turned physical. He later opposed creating the bipartisan Jan. 6 commission and was among 21 House Republicans who voted against awarding Congressional Gold Medals to police officers who defended the Capitol.

Two weeks after the attack, Harris set off a newly installed House metal detector while carrying a concealed handgun near the chamber. Capitol Police investigated. Harris’s office said he had a Maryland handgun permit, carried for self-defence because of threats, complied with screening and had never carried a firearm onto the House floor. Officers did not allow him into the chamber with the weapon. That distinction matters: the reported episode is striking enough without embellishment.

Then there was COVID-19. Harris is a physician, which made his intervention unusually conspicuous. In 2021 he said he had prescribed ivermectin as a COVID treatment and complained that pharmacists would not fill such prescriptions. At the time, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration had not approved ivermectin to treat or prevent COVID-19, and major American medical and pharmacy organizations opposed its use for that purpose outside clinical trials. Harris had earlier administered COVID vaccines himself, but later opposed vaccine and mask mandates and questioned their necessity.

His social-policy record is equally conservative. Harris has been a prominent opponent of abortion and was an original co-sponsor of the 2021 Life at Conception Act, legislation that sought legal protection beginning at fertilization. He later backed a federal 15-week abortion proposal containing exceptions. He also became a particular antagonist of Washington, D.C.’s marijuana legalization movement after inserting federal budget language preventing the District from spending its own funds to establish a regulated recreational cannabis market approved by voters.

There has also been a smaller ethics controversy. In 2018, Maryland Democrats filed a complaint alleging Harris failed to disclose income earned by the consulting firm of the woman who became his wife. Campaign finance records showed his campaign and other Republican committees had paid her firm. Harris’s campaign said the omission was an error and that he amended the disclosure after learning of it. That is the documented fact; an allegation of deliberate concealment would go beyond the evidence available to Canada at War.

The Orbán connection

For Canadians trying to place Harris politically, his enthusiasm for Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán may be more revealing than any isolated scandal. Harris has repeatedly cultivated ties with Hungary’s conservative political movement and publicly praised Orbán’s example. The relationship places him inside an international current of national-conservative politics that treats immigration, liberal institutions and progressive social policy as connected fronts in a broader political struggle.

That makes Monday’s language about Canada especially interesting. Harris did not merely say Albertans have grievances. He described western Canada through an American ideological template — “socialism” in Ottawa, alienated western provinces, enormous oil reserves — and then proposed changing an international border. The oil reference is difficult to miss. His hypothetical 51st state was introduced not with a meditation on Albertans’ democratic aspirations but with the observation that there are “tremendous oil reserves there.”

Canada should resist the temptation to turn Harris into more than he is. One congressman talking on Newsmax cannot annex a province. Alberta’s constitutional future belongs to Albertans and Canadians, and any separation process would involve Canadian constitutional law, democratic legitimacy and negotiations far more complicated than a television sound bite.

But dismissing him as a crank would be equally lazy. Harris helps lead an influential faction of the governing Republican Party. Donald Trump has repeatedly spoken of Canada as a possible 51st state. The administration is applying extraordinary economic pressure to Canada. U.S. officials are publicly mocking Canadian military and economic leverage. Alberta is simultaneously confronting a real separatist debate. Against that background, a senior congressional ally of Trump saying the United States should take Alberta — and that the president is exploiting western alienation — is news.

The question is not whether Andy Harris has discovered a workable plan for continental rearrangement. He has not presented one. The question is why annexation of Canadian territory has become casual political conversation among people with actual power in Washington.

That is a change Canadians would be foolish not to notice.
