Reason against the mob

Condorcet’s mathematics of collective judgment contains both an Enlightenment hope and a warning: crowds become wiser only under demanding conditions.

Condorcet · Democracy · Collective judgment · Permanent reference essay

The Marquis de Condorcet believed human beings could improve their institutions through reason, education and the spread of knowledge. He also tried to describe mathematically when collective decisions become more reliable.

The result now associated with his name—the Condorcet jury theorem—is often reduced to the claim that large groups are wise. That is not what it says.

Its assumptions are the important part.

When more people can help

In a simplified version of Condorcet’s argument, imagine a group choosing between two alternatives, one of which is correct. If each voter has a probability greater than one-half of choosing correctly and the judgments are sufficiently independent, adding more voters can increase the probability that the majority reaches the correct answer.

That is a powerful defence of collective judgment. No ruler needs to be infallible. Many imperfect people can outperform one decision-maker.

But change the assumptions and the result changes.

A crowd is not made wise by size alone. Information, competence and independence matter.

Manipulation attacks the assumptions

Propaganda can be understood as an assault on the conditions that make collective judgment useful.

If millions of people receive the same false information, their errors are no longer independent. If a campaign systematically lowers the quality of information, the average voter may not be more likely than chance to identify the correct answer. If social pressure makes people copy their group, adding more people may simply amplify the same error.

That is why a false story repeated across a network can be more dangerous than one isolated lie. It creates correlated error.

Education is political infrastructure

Condorcet argued strongly for public education. The connection to democracy is direct. Citizens cannot exercise judgment if knowledge is monopolized by elites or if large parts of the population are denied the tools needed to evaluate claims.

Education does not guarantee agreement. It improves the capacity to ask what evidence supports a statement, how a number was produced and whether an argument follows from its premises.

A democratic information system therefore needs more than freedom to speak. It needs institutions that help people become competent judges: schools, libraries, independent research, transparent statistics and journalism that shows its evidence.

The mob is created, not discovered

Political rhetoric often treats “the crowd” as naturally irrational. Condorcet’s framework suggests a better question: what conditions are shaping the crowd’s judgment?

Are people receiving diverse evidence or one synchronized message? Are they deciding independently or signalling loyalty? Are claims testable? Are mistakes corrected? Are institutions increasing competence or rewarding outrage?

A public can be made less rational by the environment in which it is asked to decide.

Majority rule is not truth

Even under favourable conditions, a vote decides what a group will do. It does not transform the winning proposition into a fact.

Scientific claims, historical events and legal evidence are not made true by popularity. Democracy requires a distinction between political authority and factual reality.

A majority can legitimately choose a policy while being mistaken about the facts used to justify it. That is why democratic systems need mechanisms that survive electoral pressure: courts, auditors, professional statistical agencies, archives and protected research.

The defence

When a claim is popular, do not ask only how many people believe it. Ask whether those people reached the belief independently and from adequate evidence.

When the same assertion appears everywhere, ask whether that represents confirmation or merely repetition from a common source.

Condorcet’s optimism is conditional. Collective intelligence is possible. So is collective error.

The difference lies not in whether the public is a noble people or a foolish mob, but in the information and institutions through which millions of individual judgments become one political decision.
