Main story · Ottawa retaliates

Canada answers with $27.6 billion in counter-tariffs.

Ottawa will impose 15%, 25% and 50% tariffs on U.S. goods beginning September 8, matching Washington “dollar for dollar, rate for rate.” The package reaches steel, aluminum, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, electronics, furniture, apparel, seafood and more — while existing auto counter-tariffs remain in place.

Alongside the tariffs, the government announced $7.5 billion in new support for workers and businesses, including liquidity, diversification funding, employment insurance flexibility, training and retention measures.

Latest developments

Canada · New lead

Ottawa publishes the tariff map — and the response is broad

Finance Canada says the new counter-tariffs cover $27.6 billion in U.S. imports and take effect at 12:01 a.m. on September 8. The top 50% rate applies to steel and aluminum products that had previously faced 25%, as well as furniture and clothing/apparel. A 25% rate applies to appliances, dairy products such as cheese, fish and seafood, and certain steel and aluminum derivatives. Other targeted items face 15%.

Existing Canadian auto counter-tariffs remain in place. Ottawa is also keeping its tariff-remission system available for exceptional cases.

Support package

$7.5 billion to cushion workers and firms

The government says the new measures include expanded regional tariff-response funding, BDC liquidity, a Canada Strong Diversification Fund, and worker-support programs built around EI flexibility, training, retention and retraining.

Foreign reaction · Midday check

Still no government cascade abroad

We found no fresh official statement from the EU institutions, United Kingdom, Mexico or China specifically responding to Canada’s August 25 counter-tariff package. The absence remains notable given the scale of the escalation.

Media and market reaction is more visible. Reuters calls the package a significant deterioration in bilateral trade relations. Asia-Pacific market commentary has linked the Canada-U.S. rupture to wider caution about global tariffs and supply-chain risk.

Sources: Reuters, Aug. 25; Asia-Pacific market reporting, Aug. 25.

Europe

European commentary is moving toward “the Carney model”

Euronews has openly asked whether the EU should “pull a Carney” and walk away from a bad U.S. trade deal if Washington pushes too far on digital, climate or regulatory sovereignty. That is media analysis, not an EU government position, but it shows Canada is becoming a European reference point.

European Parliament

Bernd Lange: Europe stands by Canada

Bernd Lange, chair of the European Parliament’s International Trade Committee, has argued that trade deals must be negotiated between equals and publicly aligned himself with Canada’s resistance to U.S. pressure. His intervention remains one of the strongest serving-European political reactions so far.

Germany

Berlin is already building the deeper partnership

Carney and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz are advancing a Canada-Germany Strategic Partnership covering defence, security, energy, AI, critical minerals, supply chains and investment — the same strategic logic Ottawa now wants to deepen at EU level.

Failed deal

The near-deal terms are getting clearer

Reuters reports that the proposed agreement would have cut U.S. tariffs on Canadian light-duty vehicles from 25% to 15% and steel and aluminum from 50% to 25%. Medium- and heavy-duty trucks remained a sticking point. Canada had also been prepared to remove corresponding counter-tariffs, encourage restored U.S. alcohol sales and make limited dairy-administration changes without abandoning supply management.

Defence + sovereignty watch

Gripen production pitch remains alive

Saab continues to pursue Canadian Gripen production and domestic final assembly. We are watching for any sign that the trade rupture accelerates Canada’s defence-industrial shift toward Europe.

F-35 remains under review

Canada has not announced an abandonment of the F-35. Any move toward a reduced buy, mixed fleet or alternative procurement would be a major strategic consequence of the political rupture.

NORAD still holds operationally

There is still no public evidence of disruption to day-to-day NORAD operations, information sharing or command arrangements.

What we watch next

Fresh U.S. retaliation; official European or Asian government responses; movement on CUSMA; visits and summits; and any sign the economic conflict begins affecting defence, intelligence or procurement.
