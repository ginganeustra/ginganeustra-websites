Lead story · Ottawa retaliates

Canada opens the next front: $27.6 billion in counter-tariffs

Beginning September 8, Canada will impose tariffs of 15, 25 and 50 per cent on hundreds of American products. But the larger story is the C$7.5-billion support package behind them: Ottawa is beginning to finance an economic war of endurance.

The new measures reach steel, aluminum, dairy, appliances, seafood, electronics, agricultural equipment, furniture, clothing and more, while existing auto counter-tariffs remain in force. Finance Canada says the response is dollar for dollar and rate for rate.

Story 2 · China watch

Beijing’s media sees a lesson in Canada’s refusal to bend

China’s government has not formally endorsed Canada’s new tariffs. But Chinese state media has moved decisively into the argument. A new Global Times report says Canada’s firm response to American “maximum pressure” may actually improve Ottawa’s bargaining position and portrays Washington’s tariff strategy as a policy that is generating resistance among its own traditional allies.

The timing is important. The report appeared as Canada converted its promise of retaliation into C$27.6 billion of actual countermeasures. It follows an even sharper Global Times editorial describing Canada’s approach as a “Chinese-styled counterattack”: proportional retaliation, resistance on sovereignty and willingness to impose legitimate countermeasures rather than simply accommodate U.S. demands.

That framing is useful to Beijing because Canada is not an American geopolitical adversary. It is a NATO ally, NORAD partner and deeply integrated neighbour. If Canada concludes that Washington’s demands have crossed a line, Chinese commentators can argue that American economic coercion is creating fractures inside the Western system rather than merely disciplining China.

None of this makes Ottawa and Beijing allies. Canada continues to have serious disputes with China, and diversification is not supposed to mean replacing dependence on the United States with dependence on China. But the reaction matters because Canada’s confrontation is becoming an international case study. European commentators are asking whether the EU should “pull a Carney.” Chinese state media is asking whether resistance itself creates leverage. Different capitals are reaching the same strategic question from very different directions.

The distinction between media and government remains essential. Until China’s Ministry of Commerce or Foreign Ministry speaks directly, this is state-media and expert framing rather than formal Chinese policy. But it is the clearest Chinese narrative yet around the Canadian rupture.

Story 3 · Strategic audit

What Canada gave up — and what it can put back on the table

For eighteen months Canada has not merely fought Washington. It has repeatedly removed pressure points in order to keep negotiations alive. The clearest example is the Digital Services Tax: Ottawa killed the three-per-cent levy in June 2025 specifically to advance U.S. negotiations, eventually structuring the repeal retroactively so that it would be as though the tax had never existed.

The CRTC streaming file is more nuanced but belongs in the ledger. After the regulator imposed new spending and discoverability requirements on large streaming services, the government directed it to revisit the approach, citing affordability and flexibility. It would be inaccurate to claim without evidence that this was solely to please Trump, but the regulatory softening happened while Washington and U.S. digital companies were pressing Canada over cultural and digital policy.

Tariff leverage has repeatedly been raised and lowered. Canada delayed a C$125-billion second retaliatory wave in 2025, later removed most counter-tariffs to create negotiating space, and in the failed 2026 deal was prepared to remove remaining steel, aluminum and auto retaliation if Washington cut its own rates. Ottawa was also prepared to encourage provinces to restore American alcohol and make limited dairy-administration changes without surrendering supply management.

Ontario provided the cleanest demonstration of dormant leverage. It imposed a 25-per-cent surcharge on electricity exports serving roughly 1.5 million American homes and businesses — and suspended it a day later when Washington escalated and negotiations reopened. The mechanism remains an example of something Canada can switch back on.

Then there are the weapons Canada has mostly declined to use: oil, electricity, potash, uranium, critical minerals, procurement and other strategic resources. And there is defence. The F-35 has not been cancelled, but the procurement remains politically consequential while Saab continues to pitch Gripen production in Canada. Any shift would have to be justified as defence policy, not punishment, but the deteriorating U.S. relationship inevitably changes the assumptions behind major American procurement.

Can Canada afford a long confrontation? The federal numbers suggest considerable capacity, though not immunity. The Spring Economic Update projected a C$66.9-billion deficit, 2.1 per cent of GDP, and federal debt around 41.1 per cent of GDP. Canada retains AAA-equivalent ratings from Moody’s, S&P and DBRS and AA+ from Fitch; Ottawa says it has the lowest net-debt-to-GDP ratio in the G7. Debt charges remain manageable by historical standards.

That financial room is now being used. Tuesday’s C$7.5-billion package builds on nearly C$25 billion of earlier tariff support. The purpose is not to make every company whole indefinitely. It is to keep viable firms alive, retain skilled workers, finance retooling and prevent temporary political barriers from causing permanent industrial destruction.

Canada is more trade-dependent on the United States than the reverse, and autos and central Canadian manufacturing remain deeply vulnerable. But this is not a country approaching a sovereign financing crisis. Ottawa has borrowing capacity, globally credible institutions, deep banks and pension capital, and physical resources the United States needs. The question after the failed negotiations is no longer only what Canada can concede. It is which pieces of leverage it deliberately chooses to rebuild.

Europe: Canada becomes a test case

European commentary is increasingly asking whether governments should emulate Carney’s willingness to walk away from U.S. demands that intrude on regulatory sovereignty. Bernd Lange, chair of the European Parliament’s trade committee, has publicly aligned himself with Canada, while Germany is advancing a strategic partnership with Ottawa across defence, energy, AI, minerals and supply chains.

CUSMA danger grows

Reuters’ global analysis now treats the confrontation as a potential threat to the North American trade architecture itself. The question is no longer just what tariff applies to a particular product, but whether repeated unilateral action gradually hollows out the assumptions that make CUSMA valuable.

F-35 / Gripen watch

There is still no Canadian decision abandoning the F-35. Saab’s Canadian Gripen-production proposal remains strategically relevant, particularly if Ottawa accelerates defence-industrial diversification toward Europe.

NORAD remains operational

Despite the political rupture, there is still no public evidence of disruption to day-to-day NORAD operations, command arrangements or intelligence sharing. That separation between political conflict and military integration remains one of the most important lines to watch.
