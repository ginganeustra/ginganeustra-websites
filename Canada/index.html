Story 2 · The political tariff strategy

Canada is targeting Trump’s political map.

Mélanie Joly has said Ottawa’s C$27.6-billion retaliation package was designed not only to protect Canadian businesses, but to put political pressure on particular American states before the November 3 midterm elections.

Maine lobster, integrated Michigan industry and politically sensitive American exporters show how the strategy works. Canada cannot match the United States in economic scale, so Ottawa is attempting something more surgical: make continuation of the trade war expensive for constituencies, companies, governors and senators whose pressure ultimately travels back to Washington.

Story 3 · Ottawa retaliates

Canada opens the next front: $27.6 billion in counter-tariffs

Beginning September 8, Canada will impose tariffs of 15, 25 and 50 per cent on around 700 American product categories. The package reaches steel, aluminum, dairy, appliances, seafood, electronics, agricultural equipment, furniture, clothing and more, while existing auto counter-tariffs remain in force.

The C$7.5-billion support package behind the tariffs is equally important: Ottawa is beginning to finance an economic war of endurance, helping firms maintain liquidity, retain workers and survive concentrated U.S. tariff shocks while Canada tries to diversify markets.

Story 4 · China watch

Beijing’s media sees a lesson in Canada’s refusal to bend

China’s government has not formally endorsed Canada’s new tariffs. But Chinese state media has moved decisively into the argument, portraying Washington’s tariff strategy as generating resistance among traditional U.S. allies.

The distinction remains essential: this is state-media framing, not a formal Chinese government declaration. But Canada is becoming an international case study.

Story 5 · Strategic audit

What Canada gave up — and what it can put back on the table

For eighteen months Canada has repeatedly removed pressure points to keep negotiations alive: killing the Digital Services Tax, delaying and later removing major retaliatory tariffs, softening parts of the digital policy environment, suspending Ontario’s electricity surcharge and exploring compromises on alcohol, dairy, steel, aluminum and autos.

The strategic question after the collapse is which pieces can be restored. Canada still has much larger forms of unused leverage in energy, electricity, potash, uranium, critical minerals and procurement.
