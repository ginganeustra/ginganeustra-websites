The trade and political confrontation — not an armed conflict. Canada, the United States and the changing world around them.
Main story · Europe answers

A senior EU trade figure has publicly stood with Canada.

Bernd Lange, chair of the European Parliament’s International Trade Committee, says trade deals must be negotiated between equals, accuses Washington of using pressure and blackmail, calls Canada a friend and says Europe stands by it. At almost the same moment, Canada and Germany are actively building a strategic partnership across defence, energy, technology, raw materials and supply chains.

This is the clearest political sign yet that Mark Carney’s attempt to turn the rupture with Washington into a deeper European alignment is finding an audience inside Europe.

EUROPE WATCH: Lange is the first senior EU political figure we have found making an explicit public statement of solidarity with Canada after the August 22 rupture. Germany, meanwhile, is already negotiating the kind of security-economic framework Carney says he wants to deepen with Europe.

The European break in the silence

European Parliament · Bernd Lange

“We stand by Canada”: the first senior EU trade voice breaks cover

For several days after Mark Carney announced that Canada would turn toward Europe for a much stronger security and economic partnership, the striking feature of the European response was the absence of an obvious public answer from the top of the EU system. There was analysis, there was sympathy, there were existing negotiations — but no senior European office-holder had plainly stepped forward and said, in effect, Canada is right to resist.

Bernd Lange has now done that. Lange is the chair of the European Parliament’s Committee on International Trade, one of the Parliament’s most consequential positions whenever Europe is negotiating trade rules, market-access arrangements or major commercial agreements. His words therefore carry a different weight from an editorial, a former official’s comment or a sympathetic academic analysis.

In a public LinkedIn statement, Lange framed the dispute as a question of power and political order. He argued that trade agreements must be negotiated on an equal footing rather than imposed by a stronger partner. He said the United States had again tried to put pressure on a partner — Canada — during negotiations, and he explicitly said Europe stands with Canada. He recalled the negotiation of CETA nearly a decade ago, including his dealings with then-trade minister Chrystia Freeland, and presented that agreement as an example of a partnership negotiated between equals.

Lange’s argument is bigger than tariffs: countries that believe in democracy and the rule of law, he says, have to cooperate rather than accept “the law of the strongest.”

That last element is what makes the statement politically interesting. Lange is not only objecting to a particular U.S. tariff or defending an existing trade agreement. He is placing Canada’s confrontation with Washington inside the same strategic debate that has been running through Europe: whether middle powers and democratic allies can retain meaningful sovereignty when a much larger country uses market access as leverage.

His institutional role matters. The International Trade Committee — usually known by its acronym INTA — handles the European Parliament’s work on the EU’s common commercial policy, including trade agreements and the parliamentary scrutiny surrounding them. Lange has been deeply involved in transatlantic trade questions for years and was closely associated with the parliamentary side of CETA. He has also been critical of the EU’s own accommodation of U.S. economic pressure.

That does not mean Lange can negotiate a new Canada-EU treaty on his own, and it does not amount to a formal European Commission commitment to Carney’s August 22 proposal. Ursula von der Leyen, António Costa and the Commission leadership have still not issued a new statement explicitly adopting Carney’s precise post-rupture formulation. The distinction matters.

But politically, Lange has crossed a line that senior serving European figures had so far been reluctant to cross in public: he has named the conduct as coercive, sided with Canada, and linked that solidarity to a broader democratic coalition against raw power politics.

Germany · Government-to-government

Germany is not merely applauding Canada — it is already building the deeper partnership

The second development is more concrete. On August 24, Prime Minister Mark Carney spoke directly with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz. The official Canadian readout says they discussed deepening cooperation in defence, security, energy and artificial intelligence. It also points directly back to negotiations already under way for a Canada-Germany Strategic Partnership Agreement.

That agreement was launched in July and is designed as an umbrella framework for cooperation in security and defence, technology and AI, space, supply chains, critical minerals, energy and investment. Carney and Merz asked their foreign ministers to advance the negotiations with the intention of finalising the agreement before the end of 2026.

This is important because the substance almost perfectly overlaps with the language Carney used on August 22 when he said Canada would begin discussions with the European Union for a “much stronger and deeper security and economic partnership.” In other words, Berlin is already negotiating a bilateral version of precisely the kind of strategic integration Ottawa now wants to broaden at EU level.

The relationship has moved beyond diplomatic language. Canada and Germany have signed or launched arrangements involving AI, quantum technology and batteries, created a Sovereign Technology Alliance, assembled more than six critical-mineral partnerships, and expanded defence-industrial cooperation. Earlier this summer, the two leaders also highlighted German commercial interest in Canadian LNG and agreements intended to facilitate defence procurement.

Germany matters far beyond the bilateral relationship. It is the EU’s largest economy and one of the central states in any European decision about industrial policy, strategic autonomy, defence procurement, energy security or economic relations with the United States. A German government that is simultaneously deepening bilateral integration with Canada makes a future EU-level Canada package easier to imagine, even though Berlin alone cannot dictate the EU negotiating mandate.

The signal is not “Germany has accepted Carney’s new EU proposal.” The stronger, more defensible statement is: Germany is already implementing the same strategic logic.

That distinction may turn out to be more important than a supportive quote. Governments reveal priorities not only through statements but through agreements, negotiating mandates, procurement decisions, technology partnerships and capital flows. On those measures, Canada-Germany integration is already moving.

Italy · Former prime minister

Paolo Gentiloni: Europe should hear a Carney-style speech

Former Italian prime minister Paolo Gentiloni, who later served as the European Union’s economy commissioner, shared Carney’s August 22 address and wrote that it was the sort of speech he would like to hear in Europe. That is a compact reaction, but the political meaning is clear: a former head of government and former senior EU commissioner believes Carney’s refusal to submit to U.S. economic pressure speaks directly to Europe’s own strategic problem.

Gentiloni is not currently setting EU policy, so his comment cannot be treated as a negotiating commitment. But he represents the European centre-left establishment and knows the internal mechanics of both national and EU economic policy. His intervention adds to the sense that Canada is becoming a reference point in Europe’s argument over how much deference Washington should receive.

Sources: Washington Post, Aug. 24, 2026; Associated Press reporting carried by the Los Angeles Times and other outlets, Aug. 23–24.

Sweden · Former prime minister

Carl Bildt predicts a quiet EU rethink

Former Swedish prime minister Carl Bildt went one step further analytically. He told the Washington Post that Canada and China were demonstrating that countries can stand up to the United States in trade disputes, and he predicted that Canada’s approach would produce a discreet discussion inside both the European Union and Japan over whether they had made the right choices in their own dealings with Washington.

That is significant because it identifies the real international test of Carney’s policy. Canada does not have to “defeat” the United States in a conventional sense for the episode to matter. If Ottawa can preserve core sovereign choices while absorbing U.S. retaliation, it may change the bargaining assumptions of other governments that had concluded accommodation was the least costly option.

Source: Washington Post, Aug. 24, 2026.

What it adds up to

Not yet an EU commitment — but no longer European silence

There is still no fresh statement from Ursula von der Leyen, António Costa or the European Commission explicitly saying that Brussels accepts Carney’s exact August 22 proposal as a new negotiating mandate. That remains the next major signal to watch.

But the picture has changed materially. We now have a serving senior European Parliament trade figure explicitly backing Canada; Germany actively negotiating a strategic economic-security framework with Ottawa; and prominent former European leaders treating Carney’s stand as an example Europe should study or emulate.

That is not yet a treaty. It is the beginning of a political constituency.

Why Europe was already primed

The deeper Canada-EU project did not begin on August 22

Carney’s new wording landed on ground that had already been prepared. Canada and the EU previously created a Strategic Partnership of the Future, signed a Security and Defence Partnership, brought Canada into the EU’s SAFE defence procurement architecture, launched Digital Trade Agreement negotiations, expanded work on critical minerals and industrial policy, and tied energy-security discussions directly to the next leaders’ summit.

The useful question is therefore not whether Europe has suddenly discovered Canada. It is whether the existing patchwork of defence, digital, minerals, energy and industrial arrangements is about to be pulled together into a more explicit political project after the rupture with Washington.

Ottawa + Washington

Ottawa · Tuesday decision point

Canada is preparing its next U.S. tariff response

Finance Canada has scheduled a technical briefing and a joint ministerial press conference for Tuesday. Ottawa says its September 8 dollar-for-dollar counter-tariffs remain on track and that a substantial package for Canadian workers and businesses is being prepared.

Provinces and territories have been asked for input on products to be targeted, the countermeasures themselves and the support required by affected firms and workers. The final retaliation list has not yet been published.

Tariff map

The 50% U.S. duty reaches hundreds of Canadian goods

Associated Press reporting shows the latest U.S. tariff action extends far beyond cars and steel, touching hundreds of Canadian product categories and a wide range of industrial and consumer goods. The breadth matters because it spreads the cost across sectors and increases the likelihood that American importers pass at least part of the duty on to U.S. buyers.

Political rupture

“Fall in line” meets a sovereignty argument

President Donald Trump has told Canada to “fall in line,” while Carney says Canada will not accept a subordinate relationship or terms that allow Washington to dictate core Canadian economic choices. The dispute is now openly framed by Ottawa as one of sovereignty as well as trade.

Negotiations

No obvious negotiating off-ramp

U.S. officials say Washington believed it had the basis of a deal before Canada sought or rejected additional terms; Ottawa says the final written U.S. language diverged materially from what Canada believed had been agreed. No new negotiating round has been publicly scheduled.

Europe + the world

Carney’s European opening now has visible political traction

On August 22, Carney said Canada would begin discussions with the European Union this fall for a much stronger and deeper security and economic partnership. Lange’s intervention and the ongoing Germany negotiations do not yet equal an EU-wide commitment, but they show that the proposition is entering Europe’s political debate from both parliamentary and government directions.

Source: Carney speech, Aug. 22.

China turns Canada into an argument about U.S. power

Chinese commentary has portrayed Canada’s resistance as evidence that U.S. trade pressure does not distinguish cleanly between rivals and allies. Some Chinese commentators explicitly argue that if Canada can force concessions, other U.S. partners may reassess their own willingness to resist. That is commentary, not a new Chinese government commitment to Canada, but it illustrates how closely the confrontation is being watched abroad.

Defence + sovereignty watch

European defence integration is already real

Canada is participating in the EU’s SAFE procurement architecture and is expanding bilateral defence-industrial ties with European states. Germany’s strategic partnership negotiations add another layer to a relationship increasingly built around supply security, procurement and sovereign industrial capacity rather than trade alone.

Gripen production pitch remains alive

Saab continues to pursue a Canadian Gripen E/F order and domestic final assembly. A Canadian production line could become part of a broader European defence-industrial realignment if Ottawa decides to reduce reliance on U.S.-centred supply chains.

F-35 remains under review

Canada has not announced that it is abandoning the F-35. We are watching whether the political rupture changes fleet size, a mixed-fleet option, procurement timing or industrial conditions.

NORAD still holds operationally

There is no public evidence today of disruption to day-to-day NORAD operations, information sharing or command arrangements. The economic and political conflict remains severe without yet becoming a rupture in continental military command.

What we watch next

The next major signals are a direct response from von der Leyen, Costa or the Commission; language from sitting EU heads of government; evidence that Carney’s “much stronger and deeper” proposal is being folded into the October Canada-EU summit; and any concrete new mandate covering industrial procurement, digital trade, critical minerals, energy or defence.

The emerging strategic question

Canada’s gamble is that diversification can become leverage before U.S. economic pressure becomes overwhelming. Europe’s question is the mirror image: whether Canada can become a sufficiently integrated economic-security partner to give both sides more room to say no to Washington when their interests diverge.
