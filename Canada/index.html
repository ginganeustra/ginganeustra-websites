Story 2 · The political tariff strategy

Canada is targeting Trump’s political map.

Mélanie Joly has said openly that Ottawa’s C$27.6-billion retaliation package was designed not only to protect Canadian businesses, but to put political pressure on particular American states before the November 3 midterm elections.

Maine lobster, integrated Michigan industry and politically sensitive American exporters show how the strategy works. Canada cannot match the United States in economic scale, so Ottawa is attempting something more surgical: make continuation of the trade war expensive for constituencies, companies, governors and senators whose pressure ultimately travels back to Washington. Energy and potash remain largely in reserve, suggesting the government is trying to escalate politically without immediately reaching for the tools that could produce the largest economic shock on both sides of the border.

Story 3 · Ottawa retaliates

Canada opens the next front: $27.6 billion in counter-tariffs

Beginning September 8, Canada will impose tariffs of 15, 25 and 50 per cent on around 700 American product categories. The package reaches steel, aluminum, dairy, appliances, seafood, electronics, agricultural equipment, furniture, clothing and more, while existing 25-per-cent auto counter-tariffs remain in force under their current rules.

The C$7.5-billion support package behind the tariffs is equally important. Ottawa is beginning to finance an economic war of endurance, helping firms maintain liquidity, retain workers and survive concentrated U.S. tariff shocks while Canada tries to diversify markets. The package is therefore not simply retaliation; it is an attempt to prevent temporary American trade barriers from permanently destroying Canadian industrial capacity.

Story 4 · China watch

Beijing’s media sees a lesson in Canada’s refusal to bend

China’s government has not formally endorsed Canada’s new tariffs. But Chinese state media has moved decisively into the argument. Global Times says Canada’s firm response to American pressure may improve Ottawa’s bargaining position and portrays Washington’s tariff strategy as generating resistance among traditional U.S. allies.

The distinction remains essential: this is state-media framing, not a formal Chinese government declaration. But Canada is becoming an international case study. European commentators are asking whether the EU should “pull a Carney,” while Chinese commentary argues that resistance itself can create leverage. Beijing has an obvious interest in portraying U.S. pressure as alienating allies, but the fact that Canada — a NATO member, NORAD partner and deeply integrated neighbour — is now its example gives that argument unusual force.

Story 5 · Strategic audit

What Canada gave up — and what it can put back on the table

For eighteen months Canada has repeatedly removed pressure points to keep negotiations alive: killing the Digital Services Tax, delaying and later removing major retaliatory tariffs, softening parts of the digital/streaming policy environment, suspending Ontario’s electricity surcharge and exploring compromises on alcohol, dairy, steel, aluminum and autos.

The strategic question after the collapse is which pieces can be restored. Canada still has much larger forms of unused leverage in energy, electricity, potash, uranium, critical minerals and procurement. Defence adds another unresolved dimension through the F-35 review and Saab’s Gripen proposal.

Canada also has substantial financial capacity to support a long confrontation. Ottawa retains strong sovereign ratings, deep capital markets and relatively low net public debt by G7 standards. Tuesday’s C$7.5-billion package builds on nearly C$25 billion in previous tariff support, giving the government room to cushion workers and viable businesses even though no fiscal program can fully neutralize the cost of losing access to such a large neighbouring market.

U.S. politics: Maine becomes a pressure point

Republican Senator Susan Collins says new Canada tariffs are a mistake and warns of damage to Maine families, farmers, businesses and lobstermen. AP and the Washington Post identify Maine, Michigan and other trade-dependent states as potential midterm vulnerabilities.

Europe: Canada becomes a test case

European commentary is increasingly asking whether governments should emulate Carney’s willingness to walk away from U.S. demands that intrude on regulatory sovereignty.

F-35 / Gripen watch

There is still no Canadian decision abandoning the F-35. Saab’s Canadian Gripen-production proposal remains strategically relevant as Ottawa considers defence-industrial diversification.

NORAD remains operational

Despite the political rupture, there is still no public evidence of disruption to day-to-day NORAD operations, command arrangements or intelligence sharing.
