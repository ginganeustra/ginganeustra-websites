“When the Americans stop doing memes, stop throwing shade, stop trying to be tough and start being serious about having those discussions, we can have those discussions”

Carney leaves the negotiating door open — but says Washington has to stop performing and start negotiating.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 4:52 p.m. ET

OTTAWA — Mark Carney has set a blunt condition for restarting Canada-U.S. trade talks: Washington must stop treating a high-stakes economic dispute as a performance.

The prime minister told reporters Tuesday that negotiations could resume when the American side stops the memes, the taunts and the effort to project toughness and begins approaching the dispute seriously.

It was unusually undiplomatic language from a leader who has often described the changed Canada-U.S. relationship in controlled institutional terms. But it was also an offer. Carney did not say talks were impossible. He said a mutually beneficial agreement remained possible if it respected Canadian sovereignty and if Washington returned with terms Canada could regard as durable.

The immediate backdrop is the collapse of negotiations on Aug. 21. Canada said late changes to the U.S. terms were unacceptable and suspended the process rather than accept an agreement Ottawa believed would damage strategic industries and constrain Canadian decision-making.

Canada’s countermeasures are scheduled to take effect Sept. 8. The published list uses different rates — 15, 25 and 50 per cent — across targeted American products, matching U.S. measures on $27.6 billion of Canadian goods.

That makes Carney’s reference to memes more than a complaint about tone. Since the talks broke down, Trump has escalated the symbolic side of the dispute, including the “Lake America” order and repeated public attacks on Canada and its leadership. Those gestures generate political attention. They do not solve the questions that ended the negotiations: market access, integrated auto production and the extent to which Canada can make industrial and trade choices without an American veto.

Carney argued Tuesday that the proposed U.S. terms went to the heart of that last question. His government’s position is that trade can be negotiated, but the survival of Canadian industries and the country’s capacity to make sovereign policy choices are not bargaining chips.

Washington disputes that account. U.S. officials say Canada walked away from a favourable offer and argue that the economic pressure is greater on Canada than on the United States. That remains the central factual and political divide: Washington depicts the impasse as a Canadian choice; Ottawa says an agreement that subordinates key sectors or sovereignty is not an acceptable agreement.

Carney’s message is therefore both rebuke and test. The rebuke is aimed at the public theatre surrounding a dispute with real consequences for workers, prices and continental supply chains. The test is whether the United States can put a reliable and economically credible proposal back on the table.

For now, there is no announced date for renewed talks. The door is open, but Carney has attached a condition to it: stop performing and start negotiating.
