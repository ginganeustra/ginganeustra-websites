How lying works · Part 1

Emotional transference: How the Nazis made fear point at the wrong target

The emotion can be real. The connection can be completely false. That gap is where one of propaganda’s most powerful political tactics operates.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · Plain-language analysis

A political lie does not always begin with a false statement.

Sometimes it begins with a feeling.

Fear. Anger. Humiliation. Disgust. Grief.

The feeling may be real and entirely justified. The lie enters when a propagandist takes that emotion and attaches it to something—or someone—unconnected to its actual cause.

That is emotional transference.

Find an emotionally charged issue. Transfer the feeling onto a politically useful target. Repeat the connection until the feeling itself appears to be evidence.

Rinse and repeat.

Find the emotion

Germany after the First World War offered propagandists an enormous supply of emotional material.

The country had lost the war. The Treaty of Versailles imposed humiliating terms. Inflation destroyed savings. Unemployment spread. Governments failed. Street violence became common. Many Germans feared communism and believed their country was collapsing.

These were real experiences.

People were genuinely frightened, angry and humiliated.

The Nazis took those emotions and redirected them.

Choose the target

Jewish people had not caused Germany’s military defeat, inflation, unemployment or political instability.

That did not matter to Nazi propagandists.

They repeatedly placed Jewish people beside every emotionally charged problem confronting Germany.

Germany lost the war: blame the Jews.

The economy collapsed: blame the Jews.

Communism threatened Germany: blame the Jews.

International finance exploited Germany: blame the Jews.

German culture was changing: blame the Jews.

The accusations contradicted one another. Nazi propaganda claimed Jews controlled both international capitalism and Soviet communism. It portrayed them as socially inferior but secretly all-powerful.

Logic was unnecessary because the argument was emotional, not factual.

The propagandist’s task was to ensure that whenever Germans felt fear, humiliation or anger, they were shown the same target.

Join the target to the emotion

Nazi newspapers, posters, films, radio broadcasts, speeches and school materials repeated the association constantly.

Images of economic disorder were connected to Jews.

Images of Soviet communism were connected to Jews.

Images of wartime enemies were connected to Jews.

Images of disease and contamination were connected to Jews.

The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents how Nazi propaganda linked Soviet communism with European Jewry and presented Germany as the defender of Western culture against a supposed “Judeo-Bolshevik threat.” Its propagandists also blamed Jews for exploitative capitalism.

The accusations did not have to be proved. They only had to be repeated.

Germany suffers. Show the target.

Germany is frightened. Show the target.

Germany is angry. Show the target.

Rinse and repeat.

The emotion was real. The connection was manufactured.

The feeling becomes the evidence

Eventually, the emotional association begins to feel like knowledge.

People may forget where they first heard the accusation. They may be unable to identify any evidence supporting it. But the emotional reaction remains.

The target feels dangerous.

That feeling is then mistaken for proof.

This is why emotional transference is so effective. It does not ask people to follow an argument. It trains them to experience a reaction.

The Nazi regime turned that reaction into government policy.

Propaganda identified the supposed enemy. Laws isolated that enemy. Police power removed the enemy’s rights. Violence became acceptable because years of emotional conditioning had taught the public to see persecution as protection.

Propaganda did not create the Holocaust by itself. But it helped prepare ordinary people to accept, assist or ignore persecution and mass murder.

The Reichstag fire showed the method in action

When the German parliament building burned in February 1933, Adolf Hitler and Joseph Goebbels portrayed the fire as the beginning of a Communist uprising.

One fire became proof of an enormous national conspiracy.

Fear of chaos was transferred onto the Nazis’ political opponents. The supposed emergency was then used to suspend civil liberties, arrest opponents and establish what became a permanent dictatorship.

The emotion was fear.

The target was communism and political opposition.

The result was power.

The method was the same: take a frightening event, attach it to a much larger target, repeat the connection and act before the public stops to examine the evidence.

The essential trick

Emotional transference works because the original emotion does not need to be invented.

A crime may really have occurred.

A factory may really have closed.

A war may really have begun.

A child may really have died.

The lie concerns who is responsible.

The propagandist takes the emotional force of the real event and redirects it toward a politically useful target. The target may have no meaningful connection to what happened.

That is the point.

How to defend yourself

When a political message makes you intensely afraid or angry, ask one question:

What evidence connects the target being blamed to the event that produced this emotion?

Not what images appeared beside each other.

Not what a politician implied.

Not what has been repeated a thousand times.

What is the proven connection?

The lesson

Recognizing this technique does not mean every modern politician using it is a Nazi. It means the method has a documented history—and that history demonstrates what can happen when manufactured emotional associations go unchallenged.

The emotion may be honest.

The target may be false.

That is how the lie works.
