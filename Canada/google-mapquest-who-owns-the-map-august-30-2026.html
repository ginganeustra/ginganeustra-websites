Who owns the map? Google chose ‘Lake America.’ MapQuest chose not to.

Published Aug. 30, 2026 · 10:40 p.m. ET

Donald Trump can tell the United States government what to call Lake Ontario.

He cannot tell Google what to call it.

That is the fact that cuts through most of the slogans, indignation and political theatre surrounding the sudden appearance of “Lake America” on Google Maps for American users.

On Aug. 27, Trump signed an executive order directing the U.S. secretary of the interior and the U.S. Board on Geographic Names to replace Lake Ontario with “Lake America” in the federal Geographic Names Information System, or GNIS, and to use the new name across federal maps, documents and communications.

That made Lake America the official name for U.S. federal purposes. It did not create a law requiring private mapmakers to follow.

Google followed anyway.

MapQuest did not.

That difference is the story.

Google’s defence is a policy. The policy is still a choice.

Google’s public explanation is direct. The company says the U.S. Geographic Names Information System formally changed Lake Ontario to Lake America, and that Google Maps updates names to reflect changes in official government sources. American users therefore see Lake America, Canadian users continue to see Lake Ontario, and users outside the two countries see both names.

Google describes that as a longstanding policy for bodies of water whose names vary between countries.

There is nothing inherently improper about having a policy. A global mapping service has to decide how to handle thousands of disputes over names, borders, languages and sovereignty. Using recognized government naming databases provides a consistent rule.

But consistency does not make the decision compulsory.

Google chose GNIS as an authoritative source for U.S. display names. Google chose the logic that determines what name an American user sees. Google chose to show only Lake America to Americans rather than Lake Ontario alongside the U.S. federal designation. Google is technically capable of showing both names, because that is exactly what it says it will do for users outside Canada and the United States.

Those are corporate design and policy decisions.

Trump did not order Google to rename Lake Ontario. His executive order binds the federal government, not Google. Google chose a policy that makes the Trump administration’s federal rename flow into the map seen by American users.

MapQuest demonstrates the point with unusual clarity. It was confronted with the same federal change. It did not adopt it. Its public response was blunt: “We’re not changing it.”

If the federal database legally compelled private mapmakers, MapQuest could not simply decline. The fact that one company changed and another refused establishes the basic rule more clearly than any political argument can.

Both companies made choices.

What the federal naming system actually controls

The U.S. Board on Geographic Names exists to standardize names used by the federal government. The relevant federal statute speaks of uniform geographic nomenclature “throughout the Federal Government” and of standardized names for federal publications.

The U.S. Geological Survey has also stated that federal geographic-name decisions do not compel state governments or private mapping companies such as Google and Apple.

That is why New York State can continue to call the lake Lake Ontario. It is why Canada can continue to call it Lake Ontario. It is why MapQuest can refuse.

Trump’s order has real consequences. Federal maps and agencies now use the new name. GNIS is one of the most important geographic reference systems in the United States. But the order does not turn a U.S. executive-branch decision into a universally binding geographic fact.

There is no global naming ministry that makes it so.

The academics say naming is not neutral

The larger implications are not speculative. They are a long-established field of academic study known as political toponymy: the study of how place names express history, identity and power.

Reuben Rose-Redwood, University of Victoria
Rose-Redwood, whose research includes the politics of place naming, has emphasized that naming is not a neutral act. It carries an assertion about who possesses the authority to name and map the world.
Derek Alderman, University of Tennessee
Alderman and Rose-Redwood have described place naming as a “world-making practice”: names help structure how people understand territory, history and political authority rather than merely labelling an objective landscape.
Clancy Wilmott, University of California, Berkeley
Wilmott has stressed the institutional distinction at the heart of the Lake Ontario dispute: a U.S. president can cause the American federal government to adopt a name, but that does not require another sovereign country to follow.

That distinction becomes more important when a private technology company has a larger audience for its map than most governments have for theirs.

Google does not have legal naming authority. It has distribution authority.

Google says more than two billion people use Google Maps every month.

That changes the practical meaning of a naming decision.

Fifty years ago, if Washington changed the name used on a federal map, the decision could spread gradually through government publications, school materials, newspapers and commercial atlases. Local usage might resist it. Editors might ignore it. Other governments might reject it. The public would encounter a mixture of competing conventions.

Today a federal database can change and one of the largest mapping systems in history can transmit that change almost immediately to hundreds of millions of screens.

Google does not acquire statutory naming authority by doing that. But it does acquire something that can be almost as consequential in everyday life: the ability to determine which name people repeatedly see.

That is distribution power.

It extends beyond the Google Maps app itself. Google mapping data and interfaces appear inside websites belonging to companies, institutions and governments. Ontario discovered the consequences this weekend when Canadian sites using third-party mapping technology began displaying Lake America even though Canada had made no such naming decision.

The province began reviewing affected websites.

That is a remarkable inversion of the traditional hierarchy. Ontario has its own statutory naming institutions. Canada has the Geographical Names Board of Canada and a national official database. Yet a Canadian institutional webpage can display terminology rejected by Canadian authorities because the underlying mapping layer is controlled by an American technology company.

That is not sovereignty in the legal sense.

It is infrastructure power.

The 1989 rules make the contrast sharper

Canada and the United States already had a framework specifically for shared geographical features.

In 1989, after years of work by naming officials on both sides of the border, the chairs of the two national naming authorities signed a document of understanding on the treatment of transboundary names. It recognized that the countries can legitimately use different names when their cultural histories differ. It encouraged common names where local usage and cultural conditions support them. Most importantly, it established coordination between the appropriate naming authorities.

Congressional Research Service analysis of the U.S. system says that for features crossing the Canadian border, the Board on Geographic Names’ domestic policy requires coordination with the appropriate Canadian authority pursuant to the 1989 agreement.

That does not mean Canada possesses a legal veto over every U.S. federal label. It does mean the normal system for a shared feature is built around consultation rather than unilateral assumption.

Trump’s executive order ordered the federal change. It did not say Canada agreed. Canada immediately rejected the rename.

And then Google made the U.S. government’s unilateral federal label the name displayed to its American users.

That sequence—presidential order, federal database, private platform—is what makes this more than a story about symbolism.

Google had other options

The most important criticism of Google is not that it should somehow ignore all official naming authorities. A global map needs rules.

The criticism is that Google’s particular rule makes a consequential choice while presenting it as a neutral technical process.

For American users, Google could have displayed:

Lake Ontario (U.S. federal name: Lake America)

It could have displayed:

Lake Ontario / Lake America

It could have retained the internationally familiar name while identifying the new U.S. federal designation in secondary text.

Instead it presents Lake America to Americans as the primary name.

That matters because interfaces create hierarchy. The largest label looks like the fact; the footnote looks like the qualification.

Google’s own global treatment proves that multiple-name display is technically possible. Outside Canada and the United States it says it will show both.

So the American display is not a technological necessity. It is a policy result.

Apple’s position matters for the same reason

Apple has similar practical influence because Apple Maps is built into hundreds of millions of phones and devices. As of Sunday evening, Apple Maps was still displaying Lake Ontario in reported checks.

That could change. Apple followed the U.S. federal rename of the Gulf of Mexico after Trump’s earlier “Gulf of America” order. But unless and until Apple changes Lake Ontario, the contrast is useful: private mapping platforms are not executing a single unavoidable legal command. They are making their own decisions about how much weight to give a federal naming database.

Microsoft and other map providers face the same question.

The more concentrated digital mapping becomes, the more consequential those corporate decisions become.

No one owns the world’s official map

There is no international authority with the power to declare a universal official name for every lake, river, mountain or sea on Earth.

The United Nations Group of Experts on Geographical Names promotes national standardization and international co-operation, but it does not legislate one compulsory vocabulary for the planet. Countries maintain their own official naming systems. Shared features can have different recognized names. Disputed names can coexist.

Historically, that pluralism meant mapmaking power was dispersed among governments, national survey agencies, atlas publishers, schools, newspapers and local communities.

Digital mapping has concentrated an extraordinary portion of that practical power inside a few corporations.

Google is not the official naming authority for the United States. The Board on Geographic Names is.

Google is not the official naming authority for Canada. Canadian naming authorities are.

But if Google controls the map a person consults every day, the company can influence which official system that person experiences as reality.

That is why the Lake Ontario case matters even if “Lake America” disappears under a future president.

The durable issue is not Trump.

The durable issue is the map.

MapQuest belongs at the top of the story

MapQuest’s refusal has been treated in some coverage as a funny internet subplot: an older map company trolling a president.

It deserves to be understood as something more useful.

It is the control experiment.

Trump issued one order. GNIS made one change. Two private mapping companies faced that same federal fact.

Google adopted the name for Americans.

MapQuest refused.

That comparison strips away the claim that Google merely “had to” follow the president.

It did not.

Google can defend the consistency of its policy. It can argue that users are best served by maps aligned with their own government’s official databases. It can point out that it uses similar location-sensitive conventions elsewhere in the world.

Those are legitimate arguments.

But they are arguments for a choice.

They are not evidence that no choice existed.

The question after Lake Ontario

The argument over the lake is unlikely to end with cartography. It touches a broader democratic problem: how much quasi-public authority should flow to private platforms because they have become the infrastructure through which people encounter facts?

Search engines influence what information is visible. Social platforms influence what speech reaches an audience. App stores influence which software reaches devices. Mapping platforms now influence the everyday vocabulary of physical geography.

None of those companies became a government ministry.

But scale can produce a form of practical authority that resembles public power without the same formal mechanisms of accountability.

That does not mean Google should be forbidden from following GNIS. It means the decision should be described accurately.

Trump changed the U.S. federal name.

Google chose to make that the name shown to Americans.

MapQuest chose not to.

Canada chose not to.

New York chose not to.

And the 1989 Canada–U.S. system for shared geographic names had been built around coordination long before any of them were asked to make those choices.

Once those facts are separated from the slogans, the central question becomes much clearer:

When governments disagree about what a place is called, who gets to decide what appears on the map most people actually use?

Legally, Google does not.

Practically, it increasingly does.
