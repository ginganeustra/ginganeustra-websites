MAGAHEADS: TRUMP MOVES FROM TARIFFS TO TALK OF ENDING TRADE WITH CANADA

The new development is not another repetition of the week’s attacks. A broad Friday-morning threat became explicitly Canadian later in the day, when Trump said Washington could simply stop trading with Canada. Jamieson Greer also carried the dispute into a fresh weekend interview.

Published Sept. 5, 2026 · 8:25 a.m. ET

WASHINGTON — Donald Trump took the week’s public pressure on Canada into new territory Friday when he moved from threatening countries with trade deficits in general to explicitly describing an end to trade with Canada.

At an Oval Office media availability later Friday, Trump said the United States had the right not to trade with other countries and used Canada as his example. “If we were playing hardball, all we’d do is say we’re going to do no trading with Canada,” he said. He claimed the United States would save as much as US$90 billion and said Canada would be in “a heap of trouble.”

That matters because the Canada-specific language came after the morning threat already reported. Trump had demanded that the Federal Reserve cut interest rates and said he could stop trade with countries running surpluses against the United States if it did not. In the later appearance, Canada was no longer an inference drawn from trade statistics. Trump named it.

Trump’s arithmetic became part of the message

Trump said Canada conducts almost all of its business with the United States, later putting the figure at 95 per cent. The latest Canadian merchandise-trade figures do not support that number if he meant exports. Statistics Canada data reported Thursday put the U.S. share of Canadian goods exports at 66.35 per cent in July and 68 per cent for the year to date.

He also described a trade deficit as money the United States could “save” by ending trade. A bilateral deficit is not a federal expenditure. It records the difference between the value of imports and exports, and ending bilateral commerce would also end U.S. exports to Canada.

Greer carries the dispute into Saturday

A separate fresh appearance came from U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer. In an extended Financial Times interview published Saturday, Greer defended the administration’s tariff policy, discussed the breakdown in negotiations with Canada and criticized Canadian retaliation and rhetoric.

Much of Greer’s underlying argument is already familiar and has been reported: he says Washington offered Canada unusually favourable terms and Ottawa chose not to take them. The new element is the continuation of that public case into Saturday while Trump’s own rhetoric has escalated from tariffs to the possibility of eliminating bilateral trade.

Analysis: Similar arguments from several administration figures do not by themselves establish a centrally scripted communications campaign. The observable fact is narrower: Canada continues to appear in senior Trump officials’ public interviews even after formal negotiations stopped, while the president is adding new threats of his own.

No matching Canadian response to the embargo language yet

Prime Minister Mark Carney’s last substantial public answer located before Saturday morning came Thursday in Thunder Bay. He rejected Howard Lutnick’s and Scott Bessent’s account that domestic politics caused Canada to leave the talks and repeated that Canada remained open to a stable, credible and mutually beneficial agreement.

On Friday, Intergovernmental Affairs Minister Dominic LeBlanc chaired a meeting of the Advisory Committee on Canada-U.S. Economic Relations with Canada’s chief negotiator and senior ministers. The government readout discussed tariffs, countermeasures, supports for workers and businesses, diversification and continued engagement. It was issued before Trump’s later remarks about ending trade with Canada.

As of early Saturday, no new public response from Carney, LeBlanc or another senior federal minister to the explicit “no trading with Canada” threat had been located. Canada’s government is still acting: its new counter-tariffs remain scheduled to take effect Tuesday. But the latest rhetorical escalation is, for now, unanswered publicly.

What to watch this weekend

Trump is spending the weekend at his Bedminster property, with no formal public rally on the schedule located for Saturday or Sunday. That makes Truth Social posts and unscheduled exchanges with the travelling press the likeliest places for another Canada intervention.

Sunday’s political programs offer two other points to watch. Energy Secretary Chris Wright is booked for both ABC’s This Week and CBS’s Face the Nation. CBS also lists House Republican Conference chair Lisa McClain of Michigan. Neither network has said Canada will be a subject, but a threat to stop trade with a country that supplies large volumes of oil to U.S. refineries creates an obvious Canada-related question if interviewers choose to ask it.

The other fixed point is Tuesday. Canada’s announced counter-tariffs on C$27.6 billion of U.S. goods are due to take effect Sept. 8. Unless something changes over the weekend, that is the next scheduled Canadian move in the dispute.
