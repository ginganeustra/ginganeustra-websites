Washington wants the world to put up more walls against China. Canada has a choice to make.

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is taking a blunt message to the G20: other countries should reconsider the terms on which they trade with China. For Canada, the demand lands at an awkward moment — just as Ottawa is trying to build alternatives to overwhelming dependence on the United States.

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has opened another front in Washington’s attempt to reshape the world trading system — and this one could put Canada directly between the United States and China.

In an interview with Reuters ahead of the G20 finance ministers and central bank governors meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, Bessent said he would encourage member countries to re-examine their terms of trade with China. His argument is that China’s export machine has become too large for the rest of the world to absorb while Chinese domestic demand remains comparatively weak.

“The world cannot have a China with a $1.2 trillion trade surplus,” Bessent told Reuters. He said the United States has already insulated itself from many Chinese exports through tariffs and outright restrictions, including on automobiles, with the result that more Chinese goods are flowing into Europe, Latin America and other markets.

The important Canadian question is not whether Washington has a case against Chinese industrial policy. It is whether Washington expects to decide Canada’s China policy too.

Bessent’s proposed answer is international pressure. Other countries, he said, will have to give Beijing an incentive to move away from export-led growth and toward stronger domestic consumption. “The rest of the world is going to have to examine their terms of trade with China,” he said.

That is more than an argument about Chinese economics. It is an attempt by Washington to organize other major economies around a U.S. diagnosis of the problem and, potentially, around additional barriers to Chinese trade.

Canada’s dilemma

For Canada, the timing is extraordinary. The country is already fighting a severe trade confrontation with the United States while trying to reduce the strategic vulnerability created by sending such a large share of its exports to one market. Europe, the United Kingdom and the Indo-Pacific have therefore become more important to Ottawa. So has the question of what a workable economic relationship with China might look like.

That does not mean Canada has suddenly become pro-Beijing. Ottawa has its own disputes with China, including over electric vehicles, industrial subsidies, agricultural trade, national security and foreign interference. Canada can independently conclude that particular Chinese trade practices require a response.

But Bessent’s remarks introduce a different issue: whether Canada’s diversification strategy will itself face pressure from Washington.

If Canada expands commercial ties with China while the United States is trying to assemble a broader front against Chinese exports, Ottawa could find itself confronting American demands on two levels at once. Washington is already using tariffs to influence where companies manufacture and invest. A coordinated G20 campaign against China would raise the possibility that U.S. trade policy will increasingly seek to influence with whom its allies trade as well.

That distinction matters. Canada deciding that a subsidized Chinese product threatens a Canadian industry is Canadian trade policy. Canada restricting trade because Washington requires alignment is something else: a sovereignty question.

The U.S. case

Bessent’s argument is not occurring in a vacuum. European governments are also worried about rising Chinese exports, particularly in industries such as automobiles and advanced manufacturing. China’s large industrial capacity, subsidies and weak domestic demand have generated concern well beyond Washington.

The U.S. Treasury has made global imbalances a formal priority of its 2026 G20 presidency. In February, Treasury said the finance track would focus on what it called “excessive global imbalances.” In July, Treasury again singled out China in its report on major trading partners, criticizing the lack of transparency surrounding Chinese exchange-rate policy while keeping China on its monitoring list.

There is therefore a legitimate multilateral economic debate here. The danger for Canada is allowing that debate to collapse into a binary geopolitical instruction: trade with the United States on Washington’s terms, or risk consequences for trading with China.

A world of blocs

Bessent’s intervention also illustrates the larger transformation taking place in international trade. The old assumption that governments would steadily remove barriers and allow production to follow comparative advantage is giving way to industrial policy, national-security screening, strategic tariffs, subsidies and economic blocs.

Canada is particularly exposed to that change because its economy was built around exceptionally deep integration with the United States. Diversification is much easier to announce than to accomplish. Geography, infrastructure, existing supply chains and decades of continental integration cannot be rewritten quickly.

Yet the current confrontation with Washington has made diversification less an abstract policy preference than a form of insurance.

That is why Bessent’s comments matter here. Canada does not have to choose China over the United States. It does have to preserve the ability to decide for itself what economic relationship it wants with China, Europe and every other major market.

The G20 meeting in Asheville will show whether Bessent’s appeal becomes a shared statement, a loose consensus, or simply another American demand that other governments are unwilling to accept. Canada should be watching the wording closely.

Because beneath the argument over China’s trade surplus is a larger question that Canada is already being forced to answer: in a world reorganizing itself into economic blocs, how much freedom will middle powers retain to choose their own partners?
