Capital Doesn’t Have a Personality: What Marx Would Say About Billionaires, Populism, and the Machines That Watch You Back

Marx did not think history was driven by villains. He thought it was driven by a system whose internal logic produces certain kinds of people, certain kinds of behavior, and certain kinds of crises, almost regardless of who’s occupying the roles. That distinction is the single most useful — and most frequently misused — idea in his entire body of work.

The character is not the cause

The most common misreading of Marx, made by admirers and critics alike, is to treat him as a moralist cataloguing greedy individuals. He wasn’t. His central claim, laid out across Capital and sharpened in the Communist Manifesto, is that capitalism is a system with its own internal compulsion — the endless accumulation of capital — and that the people who rise to the top of it are less the system’s authors than its most successful executors. A capitalist, Marx writes, is “capital personified,” someone who has internalized the system’s single imperative so completely that accumulation stops being a means to some other end and becomes the end itself. He is explicit that he is not primarily interested in blaming individual capitalists for greed, since greed is not a personality quirk here; it is closer to a job requirement, a survival trait selected for by competition. A capitalist who chooses long-term stability, generosity, or restraint over growth gets outcompeted by one who doesn’t. The system doesn’t reward good character. It reward accumulation, and it produces the character that accumulates best.

This reframing matters enormously for any attempt to apply Marx to figures who combine extreme personal wealth with extreme political influence today. The Marxist question is not “is this particular billionaire a bad person,” which is a question about individual psychology Marx mostly declined to answer. The Marxist question is: what does this scale of private capital compel its holder to do, structurally, to protect and grow itself — and what does it produce in the wider society as a side effect of that compulsion, regardless of the holder’s private intentions?

Accumulation without a natural stopping point

Marx’s account of capital’s basic drive gives a specific, mechanical answer to a question that moralizing language usually leaves vague: why does great wealth keep seeking more wealth, past any plausible point of personal need? His formula — money (M) invested to produce more money (M’) in an endlessly repeating cycle he writes as M-C-M’ — describes capital as value that has, in his phrase, become a kind of “automatic subject,” moving through the economy specifically to expand itself, with no resting point built into the process at all. A regular commodity gets produced, sold, and consumed — the transaction ends. Capital, by contrast, doesn’t have an ending point where it’s “enough”; each cycle of profit becomes the seed capital for the next cycle, and the process has no internal signal that says stop.

Applied to a landscape of trillion-dollar technology fortunes, this is the part of Marx’s argument that resists being waved away as archaic nineteenth-century economics: it isn’t a claim about factories or steam engines, it’s a claim about the logic of capital itself, and that logic doesn’t change based on whether the product is textiles or targeted advertising. A company or founder sitting on unprecedented wealth, by Marx’s account, is not behaving strangely by continuing to seek more influence, more market share, more political protection for that wealth. They are behaving exactly as the logic of capital predicts, and the scale of wealth involved simply means the compulsion now operates at a civilizational scale rather than a household one.

Alienation, but make it a feed

Marx’s early writings on alienation (Entfremdung) describe workers as separated, under capitalism, from several things at once: from the product of their labor (which belongs to someone else), from the process of their labor (which they don’t control), from their own human potential (reduced to whatever function extracts the most value), and from each other (competing rather than cooperating). The 1844 manuscripts describe this as a kind of self-loss — the worker doesn’t feel like himself at work, and only feels free in his purely animal functions of eating, drinking, and procreating, while feeling like an animal in the one activity, labor, that should be distinctly human.

It’s worth being careful about extending this concept, since Marx built it around industrial factory labor specifically. But the underlying structure — a person’s own activity and attention being extracted, packaged, and sold back to systems whose profit logic they don’t control or fully understand — maps unusually well onto the modern attention economy. A user scrolling a feed engineered by someone else’s optimization targets, generating data that gets sold to advertisers, is not factory labor in Marx’s literal sense, but the underlying relationship shares a family resemblance: activity performed by one party, value extracted by another, with the first party often unaware of how much of the transaction is happening at all, and with a growing sense — well documented outside of Marx entirely, by public health researchers studying phone use — of alienation from one’s own time and attention as a byproduct.

Ideology and “false consciousness”

Marx’s collaborator Engels coined the phrase “false consciousness” to describe a specific phenomenon Marx himself analyzed without always using that exact term: the way a ruling class’s interests get presented, and often genuinely experienced, as universal common sense rather than as the interest of a particular class. In The German Ideology, Marx and Engels write that the ruling ideas of any age are, as a rule, the ideas of its ruling class — not because the ruling class is uniquely dishonest, but because it controls the means of producing and distributing ideas (in their era: the press, the schools, the pulpits) the same way it controls the means of producing goods.

The mechanism here doesn’t require conscious conspiracy to function, which is one of the more sophisticated and frequently missed parts of Marx’s argument. A media environment overwhelmingly owned by a small number of extremely wealthy individuals does not need an explicit editorial memo instructing writers to protect billionaire interests in order to produce content that, in aggregate, treats extreme wealth concentration as normal, treats regulation of that wealth as radical, and treats a populist backlash against economic precarity as either a dangerous mob or a marketing opportunity, depending on the outlet. It only needs owners who hire editors who hire writers who have all, independently and often sincerely, absorbed the assumptions of the environment that employs them. Marx’s point is that ideology is most effective exactly when it doesn’t feel like propaganda to the people producing or consuming it — when it feels like simply describing reality.

The demagogue as capital’s insurance policy

Marx wrote directly about a phenomenon strikingly relevant to any modern populist figure who is himself extraordinarily wealthy: in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, his analysis of Louis Napoleon’s rise to power in France, Marx describes a strongman who won broad popular support, including from struggling farmers and workers, by presenting himself as standing outside and above the corrupt establishment parties — while, once in power, ruling in ways that ultimately protected and stabilized the propertied classes’ interests far more reliably than the fractious parliamentary system he replaced ever had. Marx’s famous, biting phrase for this — that history repeats itself, “the first time as tragedy, the second time as farce” — sits inside a broader argument that a demagogic strongman, however genuinely popular his rhetoric of anti-establishment grievance, tends in practice to function as capital’s most effective protector, because a single strongman is far easier for concentrated wealth to negotiate with, lobby, or capture than a genuinely contested, pluralistic democracy full of competing factions and independent institutions.

This is arguably the single most directly transferable piece of Marx’s own writing to a modern configuration in which extraordinary personal wealth and anti-establishment populist rhetoric coexist in the same political movement, rather than opposing each other. Marx would not find that combination paradoxical. His analysis of Bonapartism predicts it as close to the default outcome: a figure who channels real grievances against a distant, incompetent, or corrupt elite while quietly assembling a base of support from a different fraction of the wealthy elite — one that benefits from instability, deregulation, personal loyalty networks, and the weakening of independent courts, legislatures, and press, all of which pluralistic democracy is generally better than any single strongman at maintaining.

Technology as the newest means of production — and surveillance

Marx spent considerable energy on what he called the “forces of production” — tools, machinery, technology — and argued that whoever controls the dominant forces of production in an era controls, in large part, the shape of that era’s social relations. In the nineteenth century, that meant the factory owner controlling the machine the worker had to operate to survive. Marx would very plausibly extend the same logic to whoever controls the infrastructure of communication, attention, and increasingly automated decision-making in the current era — the platforms determining what billions of people see, the systems determining who gets a loan or a job interview, the infrastructure a modern economy and a modern political campaign alike now depend on.

His concept of the “reserve army of labor” — a pool of unemployed or precarious workers whose existence disciplines employed workers into accepting worse terms, because they’re replaceable — has an unusually direct descendant in automation and AI-driven labor displacement, where the threat of replacement by a machine or an algorithm disciplines wages and working conditions even before, or instead of, mass displacement actually occurring. A small number of firms controlling the frontier of that technology, in Marx’s framework, isn’t merely a commercial advantage; it’s control over a lever that shapes the leverage every other worker in the economy has, or doesn’t have, in every negotiation they’ll ever have.

Commodity fetishism: when the product hides the relationship

One of Marx’s more difficult but directly applicable ideas is “commodity fetishism” — his term for the way a market economy obscures the human relationships and labor embedded in the things it sells, so that a commodity appears to have value in itself, as a kind of independent object with its own intrinsic worth, rather than as the congealed result of specific people’s specific labor, extracted under specific conditions. A shirt on a shelf doesn’t announce the wages, hours, or safety conditions of the person who sewed it; the price tag stands in for that entire relationship and replaces it with a simple, depersonalized number.

The digital version of this concept is almost uncomfortably direct. A “free” app or platform presents itself as a product with intrinsic value — convenience, connection, entertainment — while obscuring the actual transaction underneath: a user’s attention, behavioral data, and psychological responses are the real commodity being extracted and sold, to advertisers and data brokers, in a relationship the user rarely sees clearly and never negotiates the terms of. The interface is friendly precisely because a hostile-looking interface would make the underlying extraction legible, and legibility is bad for the business model. Marx’s insight isn’t that this is a new form of dishonesty; it’s that markets are structurally good at hiding the relationships of production and extraction behind a clean, appealing surface, and a sufficiently sophisticated technology platform can do this with a person’s own attention and psychology more thoroughly than any nineteenth-century factory could do with a bolt of cloth.

Where this argument has to stop

It would be dishonest to present Marx’s framework as a clean, uncomplicated fit for the present moment, and it’s worth being explicit about where it strains. Marx predicted capitalism’s internal contradictions would produce its own revolutionary overthrow by an organized industrial working class; that specific prediction, in the form he made it, has not held up across a century and a half of subsequent history, and serious economists and historians across the political spectrum — not just capitalism’s defenders — regard significant parts of his economic forecasting as wrong. His framework is also frequently invoked by political actors who use “the billionaires” and “the establishment” as targets while building their own concentrated, unaccountable personal power in the process — precisely the Bonapartist pattern Marx himself warned about, which means a movement borrowing Marx’s rhetorical targets is not automatically exempt from Marx’s own sharpest warning.

It’s also worth being honest that Marx’s own preferred alternative — centralized state control over the means of production — produced, in every twentieth-century attempt at large-scale implementation, its own extraordinary concentrations of unaccountable power, frequently worse in scope and violence than the capitalist concentrations they replaced. Reading Marx usefully today means taking his diagnostic tools — alienation, ideology, the logic of accumulation, the Bonapartist function of a “populist” strongman — seriously as analysis, without assuming his prescribed cure has a clean track record, or that “anti-billionaire” rhetoric from any political direction is automatically evidence of the systemic analysis Marx was actually making rather than simply a newer vehicle for the same concentration of power under a different flag.
