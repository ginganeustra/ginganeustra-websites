🇨🇦 CANADA AT WAR
China watch · August 25, 2026

Beijing’s media sees a lesson in Canada’s refusal to bend

A new Global Times report argues that Canada’s harder line may strengthen its bargaining position — and presents the confrontation as evidence that Washington’s tariff strategy is generating resistance even among traditional U.S. allies.

China’s official government has not formally endorsed Canada’s new counter-tariffs. That distinction is essential. But a striking narrative is emerging in Chinese state media, where Canada’s confrontation with the United States is increasingly being presented as proof that Washington’s use of tariffs and market power can provoke resistance rather than compliance.

On Tuesday, the Global Times published a report headlined around the proposition that Canada’s “firm response” to U.S. trade pressure could improve Ottawa’s negotiating position. The paper cited Chinese trade expert Gao Lingyun of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, who argued that the American approach reflects a familiar pattern: begin with maximum pressure, seek concessions, and assume the other side’s dependence on the U.S. market will eventually force it to yield. Canada’s decision to suspend negotiations rather than accept the final American terms challenges that assumption.

The timing matters. The article appeared as Ottawa unveiled C$27.6 billion in new counter-tariffs at rates of 15, 25 and 50 per cent. That converts Canada’s resistance from rhetoric into an economic policy with real costs. Global Times frames the move not simply as retaliation but as a bargaining strategy: by demonstrating that pressure has a price, Canada may make future U.S. demands less attractive to Washington.

This follows an even more pointed Global Times editorial published after the talks collapsed. It described Canada’s response as a “Chinese-styled counterattack,” by which the paper meant a combination of proportional retaliation, refusal to concede on sovereignty and willingness to use rules-based countermeasures. The phrase is revealing. Chinese commentary is effectively incorporating Canada into Beijing’s own long-running argument that states should not accept unilateral American economic coercion.

That does not make Canada and China strategic allies. Ottawa has major disagreements with Beijing over security, foreign interference, human rights, critical infrastructure and trade. Canada’s diversification strategy is also explicitly designed to broaden options across Europe and Asia rather than exchange dependence on Washington for dependence on Beijing. But Chinese analysts can still see utility in Canada’s stand. Every U.S. ally that resists American tariff pressure strengthens Beijing’s contention that the dispute is not simply China versus the West, but a wider argument over how the United States uses access to its market.

Why China is paying attention

Canada is an unusually interesting case for Chinese observers because it is not a geopolitical adversary of the United States. It is a NATO ally, NORAD partner, deeply integrated industrial neighbour and one of America’s largest trading partners. If a country this close to the United States concludes that some demands are unacceptable, Beijing can point to the episode as evidence that American pressure is creating friction inside the Western alliance system itself.

The Global Times report also connects Canada to a broader pattern of pushback. Its argument is that frequent tariff threats against multiple partners are encouraging governments to reconsider whether accommodation actually produces stability. That overlaps with a debate now visible in Europe, where commentators are asking whether the European Union should emulate Carney’s willingness to walk away rather than accept conditions that intrude into domestic regulatory policy.

For Canada, there is both opportunity and danger in this Chinese attention. Beijing is a vast market and an obvious component of any serious Canadian diversification strategy, particularly for agriculture, energy and natural resources. Yet Washington is likely to scrutinize any rapid Canadian opening toward China, especially after reports that the failed U.S. proposal sought constraints on Canada’s freedom to make future trade arrangements.

That is why the most important part of the Chinese reaction is not whether Beijing “supports” Ottawa. It is that Canada’s decision is now being interpreted internationally as a test of whether a middle power can withstand American economic pressure without capitulating. Chinese state media wants the answer to be yes. European commentators are beginning to ask the same question for very different reasons.

The official Chinese government remains quieter than its media ecosystem. Until China’s Ministry of Commerce or Foreign Ministry directly addresses Canada’s August 25 package, this should be classified as state-media framing and expert commentary rather than a Chinese government policy declaration. But the framing is unmistakable: in Beijing’s telling, Canada has discovered that refusing maximum pressure may itself create leverage.
