The crisis of names

Confucius understood that public order depends partly on words keeping contact with the duties and realities they are supposed to name.

Confucius · Political language · Permanent reference essay

What happens when a government can call failure success, cruelty order or private advantage public service?

One answer appears in a famous passage of the Analects. Asked what he would do first if entrusted with government, Confucius answers that he would correct—or rectify—the names.

The wording and interpretation vary across translations and centuries of commentary. The underlying problem is clear: when names do not fit realities, speech becomes unreliable, action becomes confused and public standards begin to break down.

Names carry duties

For Confucius, a title is not merely a label. To be a ruler, parent, child, minister or friend is to stand inside a relationship with obligations.

If someone possesses the title while rejecting the obligations, the name becomes hollow. A ruler who treats office as personal property may still be called a ruler, but the word no longer describes the moral function it is supposed to carry.

This is why political language matters. Words tell people what kinds of actions are normal, legitimate and expected.

When the word survives but its meaning is emptied out, language can conceal disorder instead of describing it.

Euphemism is political power

Modern institutions constantly rename things. Sometimes this is harmless or useful. Sometimes the new label hides responsibility.

A civilian death becomes collateral damage. A firing becomes restructuring. A lie becomes a misstatement. A bribe becomes access. An attack on an institution becomes reform.

The test is not whether the new phrase sounds softer. The test is whether it helps us describe what actually happened.

If language systematically removes the actor, the victim or the consequence, the public can lose the vocabulary needed to judge conduct.

Confusion can be useful

Not every misuse of language is accidental. Political actors may benefit when the same word means different things depending on who acts.

Violence by one group is terrorism; similar violence by an ally is defence. Spending by opponents is waste; spending by supporters is investment. Information we dislike is propaganda; information that flatters us is common sense.

Once definitions become tribal, arguments become difficult to test. People appear to debate facts while actually switching meanings.

Rectification is not censorship

The Confucian idea is sometimes caricatured as a demand that authorities impose official language. That would miss the value of the concept for a modern reader.

The useful principle is accountability between word and thing. If a government calls a policy successful, what measurable condition does “successful” describe? If a politician calls an opponent corrupt, what conduct does “corrupt” identify? If a movement claims to defend freedom, whose freedom and from what constraint?

Clear definitions do not settle every dispute. They make disputes inspectable.

The discipline of naming

A practical defence against political manipulation is to translate abstractions back into actions.

When you hear “security,” ask what power is being granted. When you hear “efficiency,” ask who loses a service or job. When you hear “the people,” ask which people are included and which are silently excluded.

Then reverse the test. Would you use the same word if the opposing party performed the same act?

Confucius’ insight is not that perfect language creates perfect politics. It is that a society loses an important form of control when words no longer constrain the people who use them.

If anything can be called anything, power gains freedom and citizens lose a common language for judgment.
