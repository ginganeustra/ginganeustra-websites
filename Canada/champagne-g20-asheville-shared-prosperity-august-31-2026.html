Champagne takes Canada’s diversification case to the G20.

Canada’s finance minister arrived in Asheville emphasizing shared prosperity, critical minerals, energy and potash — and preparing for the first senior Canada–U.S. face-to-face contact since the trade talks collapsed.

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 5:56 p.m. ET

ASHEVILLE, N.C. — Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne arrived at the G20 finance ministers meeting Monday with a deliberately different message from the confrontation coming out of Washington: Canada wants collaboration, a level playing field and more trading options.

Champagne’s office said his bilateral meetings in Asheville would emphasize “collaboration and shared prosperity” while advancing the trade diversification Canadian businesses are seeking amid global uncertainty. The Canadian pitch is built around things other countries need: critical minerals, energy and potash, as Ottawa tries to turn Canada’s resource base into leverage in a less predictable trading system.

The message comes only 10 days after Canada–U.S. trade negotiations broke down. Washington imposed 50 per cent tariffs on roughly $28 billion of Canadian goods on Aug. 22, and Canadian counter-tariffs are scheduled to begin Sept. 8. Champagne’s spokesperson, John Fragos, reiterated Ottawa’s explanation for the rupture: Canada suspended negotiations after the United States introduced terms the government considered unfair and uneconomic.

That makes Champagne’s expected meeting with U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent the most closely watched Canadian encounter in Asheville. It is believed to be the first face-to-face meeting between senior Canadian and American officials since the negotiations collapsed. Neither man is the principal trade negotiator, but both sit close enough to the economic consequences to matter.

Bessent set a much sharper tone earlier Monday. He accused Prime Minister Mark Carney of turning the dispute into a “political shouting match,” argued that Canada could not sustain a true tit-for-tat confrontation with an economy roughly 13 times its size and claimed Ottawa had walked away from the best trade deal offered to any country. Canada disputes that account.

Champagne, by contrast, is using the G20 to make the case that Canada has alternatives. Diversification does not mean replacing the enormous U.S. market overnight. It means reducing the vulnerability that comes from relying overwhelmingly on one customer — particularly when that customer is willing to use tariffs as political and economic leverage.

The broader G20 meeting is itself tense. Bessent is pressing governments to address global trade imbalances, increase growth and intensify economic pressure on Iran. Other participants are arriving with concerns about American tariffs, high energy prices, U.S. debt and the growing flood of Chinese exports into world markets.

For Canada, however, the immediate question is simpler: whether Champagne’s language of shared prosperity can produce anything concrete when he sits down with Bessent. A meeting is not a resumed negotiation. But after 10 days of escalating tariffs and rhetoric, it is at least a channel.
