TRUMP NOW NAMES CANADA IN THREAT TO END TRADE AS HE PRESSURES FED

Friday morning, Trump threatened deficit countries without naming Canada. Later in the Oval Office, he did: “all we’d do is say we’re going to do no trading with Canada.” He claimed the U.S. would save as much as $90 billion and Canada might not continue to exist.

Published Sept. 4, 2026 · 11:50 a.m. ET · Updated Sept. 5, 2026 · 8:25 a.m. ET

WASHINGTON — President Donald Trump’s Friday threat to stop trade with countries that run trade deficits with the United States became explicitly Canada-specific later in the day. In an Oval Office media availability, Trump said the United States could play “hardball” by declaring that it would do no trade with Canada.

“If we were playing hardball, all we’d do is say we’re going to do no trading with Canada,” Trump said. He claimed that ending the relationship would save the United States between US$60 billion and US$90 billion a year and said Canada would be in “a heap of trouble.” He added: “I don’t think they’d exist.”

Trump also said Canada conducts 95 per cent of its business with the United States. The latest merchandise-trade data do not support that figure if he meant exports: Statistics Canada data reported for July put the U.S. share of Canadian goods exports at 66.35 per cent and the year-to-date share at 68 per cent.

The later remarks materially changed the statement Trump made Friday morning. His initial threat connected access to the American market to a monetary-policy decision made inside the United States while leaving unanswered which countries, products or legal mechanism he had in mind. At that point, he had not named Canada.

U.S. trade figures show a substantial American goods deficit with Canada that is partly offset by a U.S. surplus in services. That makes the president’s description of a US$60-billion to US$90-billion “saving” misleading. A trade deficit is the difference between imports and exports, not a federal expenditure.

Ending imports would also mean Americans no longer receive the goods and services they were buying, while U.S. exporters would lose Canadian customers. The bilateral relationship includes crude oil, autos and auto parts, machinery, agricultural products, electricity, minerals and large volumes of intermediate goods used in production on both sides of the border.

The Federal Reserve sets U.S. monetary policy and foreign governments have no role in its interest-rate decisions. Trump has repeatedly pressed the central bank for lower rates, but Friday’s statements put a foreign-trade consequence directly behind that demand.

Trump also invoked the Supreme Court’s February ruling on his emergency tariffs and characterized it as recognizing an “absolute right” for the president to stop trade. The decision itself was narrower: in Learning Resources v. Trump, the court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs.

IEEPA separately contains powers to regulate and, in qualifying emergencies, block or prohibit certain import and export transactions. The court did not hold that a president has an unrestricted power to terminate trade with any country simply because the United States runs a deficit with it.

For Canada, the threat arrives inside an already active tariff dispute. Washington imposed 50-per-cent tariffs on C$27.6 billion of Canadian goods on Aug. 22, and Ottawa has announced counter-tariffs on C$27.6 billion of U.S. imports beginning Sept. 8.

The two governments have also given different accounts of why formal trade talks stopped Aug. 21. Senior U.S. officials say Canada sought additional concessions after being offered unusually favourable terms; Prime Minister Mark Carney says the U.S. position remained unacceptable on several issues and that any agreement must provide stability and credibility.

Trump had already added a Canada-specific warning Thursday, saying Canadian politicians could make him “the enemy” until Canada’s economy collapsed. Friday’s later remarks went further by describing the elimination of bilateral trade itself as leverage that he said the United States could use.

What remains unknown is whether the threat will become policy, what authority the administration believes would permit a complete Canada trade cutoff, what transactions would be covered and whether the statement is intended as an actual policy option or as pressure on the Federal Reserve. The White House had not publicly supplied those details by early Saturday.
