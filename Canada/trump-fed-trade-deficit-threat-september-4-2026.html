TRUMP THREATENS TO STOP TRADE WITH DEFICIT COUNTRIES UNLESS FED CUTS RATES

Trump did not name Canada, but U.S. government figures show the United States ran an overall goods-and-services deficit with Canada in 2025. He did not explain how a trade cutoff would work.

Published Sept. 4, 2026 · 11:50 a.m. ET

WASHINGTON — President Donald Trump said Friday that he would stop trading with countries that run trade deficits with the United States unless the Federal Reserve lowers interest rates. The threat connected access to the American market to a monetary-policy decision made inside the United States, while leaving unanswered which countries, products or legal mechanism he had in mind.

Trump did not name Canada. U.S. Trade Representative figures nevertheless show that the United States ran a US$48.3-billion goods deficit with Canada in 2025, partly offset by a US$27.7-billion surplus in services, leaving an overall bilateral deficit of about US$20.6 billion on those figures.

That distinction matters because Trump did not say whether his threat applies to goods alone, goods and services combined, or some other measure. He also gave no timetable, deficit threshold or description of what “stop trading” would mean for goods already moving under existing agreements.

The Federal Reserve sets U.S. monetary policy and foreign governments have no role in its interest-rate decisions. Trump has repeatedly pressed the central bank for lower rates, but Friday’s post put a foreign-trade consequence directly behind that demand.

The statement followed a stronger-than-expected U.S. employment report. Reuters reported that the economy added 162,000 jobs in August, compared with a forecast of about 56,000, while unemployment held at 4.1 per cent; traders responded by increasing bets that the Fed could raise rather than cut rates later in September.

Trump also invoked the Supreme Court’s February ruling on his emergency tariffs and characterized it as recognizing an “absolute right” for the president to stop trade. The decision itself was narrower: in Learning Resources v. Trump, the court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs.

IEEPA separately contains powers to regulate and, in qualifying emergencies, block or prohibit certain import and export transactions. The court did not hold that a president has an unrestricted power to terminate trade with any country simply because the United States runs a deficit with it.

For Canada, the new threat arrives inside an already active tariff dispute. Washington imposed 50-per-cent tariffs on C$27.6 billion of Canadian goods on Aug. 22, and Ottawa has announced counter-tariffs on C$27.6 billion of U.S. imports beginning Sept. 8.

The two governments have also given different accounts of why formal trade talks stopped Aug. 21. Senior U.S. officials say Canada sought additional concessions after being offered unusually favourable terms; Prime Minister Mark Carney says the U.S. position remained unacceptable on several issues and that any agreement must provide stability and credibility.

Trump added a Canada-specific warning Thursday, saying Canadian politicians could make him “the enemy” until Canada’s economy collapsed. Friday’s deficit-country threat was broader, but Canada falls inside at least one ordinary reading of the group he described because the U.S. goods deficit with Canada exceeds its services surplus.

What remains unknown is whether the post will become policy, which trade balance the administration would use, what authority it would invoke and what action by the Federal Reserve would satisfy Trump’s demand. The White House had not publicly supplied those details by late Friday morning.
