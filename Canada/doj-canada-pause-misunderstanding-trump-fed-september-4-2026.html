DOJ CALLS ‘PAUSE ON CANADA’ A MISUNDERSTANDING — AS WASHINGTON LURCHES THROUGH A DAY OF ERRATIC POLICY

A written antitrust instruction told staff to stop cooperation with Canada and inventory every area of joint work. The Justice Department now says the message only meant to delay a meeting. Hours earlier, Trump threatened to halt trade with deficit countries unless the independent Federal Reserve cut rates.

Published Sept. 4, 2026 · 4:52 p.m. ET

WASHINGTON — The U.S. Justice Department says a written instruction telling Antitrust Division staff to stop cooperative work with Canadian authorities was a misunderstanding, not a change in U.S. policy toward Canada. The Wall Street Journal, which reviewed the internal emails, reported Friday that Justice Department spokeswoman Emily Covington said the message had misunderstood an instruction to postpone a scheduled meeting and that there is no formal pause in antitrust cooperation.

That clarification came after an unusually broad internal directive had already circulated. According to the Journal, Lynda Marshall, who heads the Antitrust Division’s international section, sent an email Wednesday with the subject line “Pause on Canada” directing officials to stop cooperative case work and policy engagement with Canadian authorities. On Friday, section chiefs were also told to identify every area in which the division cooperates with Canada.

The distinction is important. The United States has not, on the evidence now available, severed antitrust cooperation with Canada, and there is no evidence that the entire Justice Department or the Federal Trade Commission stopped working with Canadian counterparts. What did happen is narrower but still significant: a senior official sent a written instruction that staff understood as a broad Canada pause, and the department later said the instruction did not mean what it appeared to say.

Two days of confusion over a decades-old relationship

Competition-law cooperation between the two countries is not a recent political arrangement. Canada and the United States signed a formal antitrust cooperation agreement in August 1995, building on an earlier 1984 understanding. The agreement says it is in both countries’ common interest to cooperate in detecting anticompetitive conduct, sharing information where law permits, locating evidence and witnesses, coordinating enforcement and avoiding unnecessary conflicts.

That architecture has survived governments of different parties in both countries. Canada’s Competition Bureau, the U.S. Justice Department and the Federal Trade Commission have also published joint merger-cooperation practices describing years of coordination when transactions affect both markets, precisely because cross-border cases can become slower, more expensive and less coherent when agencies work in isolation.

What the reversal says — and does not say

Analysis: A mistaken email is not proof of a deliberate policy to dismantle Canada–U.S. law-enforcement ties, and the evidence does not establish that Donald Trump, the White House or senior Justice Department leadership ordered a Canada cutoff. But a misunderstanding that can generate a written halt instruction at this level is itself a governance problem: countries, companies and investigators depend not only on the substance of U.S. policy but on confidence that consequential instructions mean what they say.

For Canada, that makes predictability part of the bilateral risk. A relationship built over decades can absorb disagreements, tariffs and sharp rhetoric; it becomes harder to manage when officials must also determine whether a sweeping instruction is policy, an error, a meeting postponement or something that may be reversed after it reaches the press.

Hours earlier, Trump threatened trade to pressure the Fed

Friday’s antitrust confusion arrived on the same day President Donald Trump made a separate threat aimed at the Federal Reserve. After a stronger-than-expected U.S. jobs report increased market expectations that the Fed could raise rates later this month, Trump wrote on Truth Social that the United States should have the lowest interest rate in the world and threatened to stop trading with countries with which the United States runs a trade deficit unless the Fed lowers rates.

The threat would use foreign commerce as leverage in a dispute between the president and an American institution. The Federal Reserve says its monetary-policy decisions do not require approval from the president or anyone else in the executive or legislative branches, and that Congress structured the system so those decisions can be based on long-term economic goals rather than short-term political pressure.

The July Federal Open Market Committee meeting illustrates the conflict Trump was trying to influence. The committee kept rates unchanged, while Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan dissented because they preferred a quarter-point increase. Friday’s strong employment report then pushed traders toward greater expectations of a September hike, directly opposite the cut Trump demanded.

A day of policy by threat, then reversal

Analysis: The Justice Department episode and Trump’s Fed threat are separate events, and nothing in the reporting shows a common cause. They nevertheless produced, within one news cycle, two striking displays of policy disorder: one arm of government circulated a consequential Canada instruction and then said it had been misunderstood, while the president threatened disruption of international trade as leverage against a domestic institution designed to resist presidential direction.

Calling that a coherent anti-Canada strategy would go beyond the evidence. Calling it a serious deterioration in administrative discipline and policy predictability does not. The relevant facts are the breadth of the antitrust instruction, its subsequent disavowal, the lack of an initial explanation, and a presidential trade threat whose stated purpose was to force an independent central bank toward the rate decision he prefers.

The danger for Canada is not only a particular tariff or a particular insult. It is the possibility that major U.S. decisions affecting Canadian businesses and institutions can now arrive through abrupt threats, internally confused instructions and rapid reversals, leaving Ottawa to plan around a government whose position may be difficult to distinguish from its latest message.

What remains unknown

The Justice Department has not publicly explained how an instruction to postpone a meeting became an email directing staff to pause Canada cooperation, who supplied the original instruction, or whether any active case was delayed while the message stood. Nothing reviewed for this report shows that the FTC received a comparable instruction, and no Canadian government response to the episode had appeared in the public sources reviewed by late Friday afternoon.

Trump has likewise not explained how a trade cutoff tied to Federal Reserve policy would be implemented, which measure of bilateral deficits he would use, or whether the threat will become policy. The strongest conclusion available Friday is therefore not that Washington actually broke antitrust ties with Canada — according to the Justice Department, it did not — but that the United States produced two consequential episodes in one day that made its decision-making look substantially less predictable.
