🇨🇦 CANADA AT WAR
Strategic audit · August 25, 2026

What Canada gave up — and what it can put back on the table

From the digital-services tax to electricity, streaming regulation, tariffs and defence procurement, Ottawa has spent eighteen months removing pressure points in pursuit of a deal. With negotiations broken, the question changes: which pieces of leverage can Canada restore — and can the country afford a long fight?

Canada entered the second Trump era with an extraordinary problem. Its largest customer, closest military ally and indispensable industrial partner was also threatening to use access to the American market as leverage over Canadian domestic policy. Ottawa’s response, first under Justin Trudeau and then under Mark Carney, was not simply retaliation. It was a repeated cycle of pressure, concession, suspension and negotiation.

Now that the negotiations have collapsed, that history deserves an audit. Some measures were genuine Canadian climb-downs. Others were retaliatory weapons used briefly and then holstered. Still others — energy, critical minerals and defence procurement — have never been fully deployed. They are not all legally or politically interchangeable, but together they describe the bargaining position Canada has built, surrendered and could potentially rebuild.

The clearest concession: the Digital Services Tax

The most unambiguous example is the Digital Services Tax. Canada created the three-per-cent levy to tax certain revenues earned from Canadian users by large digital businesses, including online marketplaces, targeted advertising and social-media businesses. Washington and major U.S. technology companies strongly opposed it.

On June 29, 2025, the Carney government announced that Canada would rescind the tax specifically to advance negotiations with the United States. Collection was halted just before the first major payment deadline. The repeal was retroactive: federal briefing documents later described the effect as though the DST had never existed, with payments refunded with interest. Finance Canada had projected roughly C$2.3 billion in initial revenue and C$900 million in each of the following four years.

That is not ambiguous diplomatic choreography. Canada surrendered a tax policy, and revenue, in order to reopen negotiations. If Ottawa ever considered restoring a comparable digital levy, it would be one of the most obvious examples of putting previously surrendered leverage back on the table — though doing so would reopen legal, trade and investment questions.

The CRTC and streaming: a softer retreat

The Online Streaming Act is more complicated. In May 2026, the CRTC set new requirements for large broadcasters and online streaming services, including spending on Canadian programming and measures supporting discoverability. In June, the federal government directed the regulator to revisit its approach and promised new policy directions emphasizing affordability, consumer choice and flexibility. Ottawa also committed public money to support Canadian cultural production during the transition.

The government’s stated reason was the risk that regulatory costs would be passed to Canadian subscribers. It would therefore be too strong to state as fact that Carney changed the rules solely to satisfy Trump. But the change occurred against a backdrop in which U.S. digital and streaming companies had objected to Canadian regulation and Washington was scrutinizing Canadian cultural policy. It belongs in the ledger as a Canadian regulatory position softened during the wider confrontation.

Tariffs repeatedly raised — then lowered

Canada has also repeatedly moderated retaliation to create negotiating space. In 2025 Ottawa delayed a planned second wave of counter-tariffs worth roughly C$125 billion after the United States temporarily exempted CUSMA-compliant goods. Later, Canada removed most of the retaliatory tariffs imposed on American imports while preserving measures in the most sensitive steel, aluminum and automotive sectors.

During the negotiations that collapsed this month, Carney says Canada was prepared to go further. Ottawa would remove remaining counter-tariffs on steel, aluminum and autos if Washington substantially reduced its corresponding tariffs. Canada was also prepared to encourage provinces to restore American alcohol to their retail systems and make limited administrative changes around dairy while preserving supply management, existing U.S. quota access and over-quota tariff rates.

Those offers never became a final agreement. That distinction matters: an offer made in negotiation is not the same as a concession implemented. But the failed package tells us what Ottawa had already decided it could live without in exchange for U.S. relief.

Ontario showed what electricity leverage looks like

In March 2025, Ontario imposed a 25-per-cent surcharge on electricity exported to Michigan, Minnesota and New York, affecting power supplied to roughly 1.5 million homes and businesses. The measure was dramatic precisely because it moved retaliation away from consumer goods and toward a strategically important cross-border resource.

It lasted barely a day. After U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick contacted Premier Doug Ford and Washington threatened still-higher steel and aluminum tariffs, Ontario suspended the surcharge. The market rule enabling it remained available with the charge set to zero. Electricity therefore provides a near-perfect example of dormant leverage: Canada has demonstrated the mechanism, demonstrated that Washington notices it, and demonstrated that it can be switched off for negotiations.

Alcohol, procurement and the provincial arsenal

Provincial liquor boards removed American products from shelves during earlier phases of the dispute. Restoring access later became part of Canada’s settlement discussions. Provinces also control enormous procurement budgets and infrastructure spending. “Buy Canadian” rules, provincial purchasing preferences and decisions over which foreign suppliers receive public contracts can become important leverage even when Ottawa itself is trying to keep retaliation narrowly targeted.

These tools are politically attractive because they can be aimed. They are also economically dangerous because Canadian businesses depend on American inputs. Every measure has to be judged not by how satisfying it sounds but by how much pain it imposes on the United States relative to Canada.

The unused heavy weapons: energy and resources

Canada has largely declined to weaponize its strongest physical exports. Oil, natural gas, electricity, potash, uranium and critical minerals feed American factories, farms, grids and strategic supply chains. Ontario has repeatedly mentioned electricity and critical minerals. Nationally, however, Ottawa has avoided broad export restrictions on energy and essential commodities.

There are good reasons. Resource restrictions would hurt Canadian producers and provincial revenues, raise questions about contracts and trade obligations, and potentially encourage U.S. customers to accelerate substitution. But in a prolonged confrontation, the existence of these supplies gives Canada bargaining power that ordinary tariffs cannot replicate.

F-35 and Gripen: procurement as strategic leverage

The F-35 is another unresolved file, but it should not be mislabelled as a concession already withdrawn. Canada has not cancelled the fighter purchase. The government has been reviewing the procurement while Saab continues to pitch the Gripen, including Canadian production and final assembly. A reduced F-35 purchase, mixed fleet or switch toward a European platform would carry consequences far beyond trade: industrial jobs, aerospace supply chains, interoperability, NORAD and Canada’s strategic relationship with Europe.

That makes defence procurement unusually powerful leverage — and unusually costly leverage to use carelessly. The question is not whether Canada should buy a fighter aircraft to punish Washington. It is whether the deterioration of the relationship changes the national-security assumptions on which the original procurement decision rested.

Can Canada afford a prolonged trade war?

The fiscal answer is more encouraging than the trade-exposure numbers suggest. Canada is not fiscally invulnerable, but the federal government enters the confrontation with substantial borrowing capacity. The 2026 Spring Economic Update projected the 2025-26 federal deficit at C$66.9 billion, about 2.1 per cent of GDP, with federal debt around 41.1 per cent of GDP. Ottawa projected debt charges of C$58.7 billion in 2026-27, about 1.7 per cent of GDP.

Canada also retains one of the strongest sovereign credit profiles in the industrialized world. The federal government says Canada has the lowest net-debt-to-GDP ratio in the G7 and is one of only two G7 countries with AAA-equivalent ratings from Moody’s, S&P and DBRS; Fitch rates Canada AA+. Rating agencies cite institutional stability, economic diversity, regulated financial markets and fiscal and monetary flexibility.

That does not mean Canada can spend without limit. It means Ottawa has room to borrow in order to prevent a trade shock from becoming a wave of otherwise avoidable bankruptcies and permanent industrial destruction.

The government is already using that capacity. Tuesday’s C$7.5-billion package adds liquidity through regional agencies and the Business Development Bank of Canada, a C$2-billion diversification fund and C$3.5 billion in rapid-response supports for workers and employers. Finance Canada says this builds on nearly C$25 billion of support deployed since U.S. tariffs began.

The purpose of fiscal support in a trade war is not to reimburse every lost dollar forever. It is to bridge viable companies through a political shock, finance retooling and new markets, keep skilled workforces attached to strategic industries and prevent temporary tariff barriers from permanently relocating production. Canada’s large banks, pension funds, resource companies and institutional investors add private financial depth, although they cannot substitute for government when entire sectors are hit by politically imposed barriers.

The vulnerabilities are real. Canada is far more dependent on U.S. trade than the United States is on Canada. Autos and manufacturing in Ontario and Quebec are especially exposed. A weaker Canadian dollar can raise import costs. Persistent uncertainty can suppress investment even in sectors not directly tariffed. Provinces also have very different fiscal positions and exposure to the U.S. market.

But the central conclusion is equally real: Canada is not approaching this confrontation as a country on the edge of a sovereign financing crisis. It can finance substantial temporary support. It controls resources the United States needs. It has access to global capital, deep pension assets and functioning institutions. And it has already demonstrated a long list of policies it can either restore, intensify or reconsider.

The strategic question after the collapse of negotiations is therefore no longer simply what Canada can concede to get a deal. It is what Canada previously removed from the bargaining table, which of those choices still make sense, and which pieces of leverage should be deliberately rebuilt.
