“Embarrassing”: the attacks on Canada are drawing fire from inside the American establishment.

Republican politicians from border states and retired senior U.S. military leaders are warning that the administration's rhetoric is damaging an alliance whose economic and military ties are far deeper than the insults suggest.

The most important feature of the new American criticism is where it comes from. This is not simply Democratic opposition to Donald Trump. The Financial Times reports that Republicans and former senior military officers are recoiling from the administration's escalating attacks on Canada.

Border-state Republicans have something immediate to lose

Republican senators from states economically connected to Canada have raised concerns about the consequences of the confrontation and the tone of the administration's approach. Border economies do not experience Canada as an abstract foreign competitor. They experience it through factories, energy flows, tourism, agriculture, trucking, local commerce and supply chains.

That makes these politicians an important pressure point. They can support a tougher national trade policy in principle while still concluding that a particular confrontation with Canada is economically self-defeating or politically excessive.

The military backlash is about memory — and credibility

The sharper moral criticism is coming from retired U.S. military leaders responding to administration figures who have belittled Canada's defence contribution. Former officers have pointed to decades of operational cooperation and, particularly, Canada's sacrifices in Afghanistan.

Canada fought alongside the United States after the September 11 attacks. Canadian forces sustained 158 military deaths in the Afghanistan mission. The two countries also share NORAD, a binational aerospace-defence command whose daily functioning rests on an extraordinary level of military trust.

For former commanders, dismissing Canada is therefore not merely impolite diplomacy. It risks rewriting the history of an ally that fought and died alongside Americans and still participates in the defence of North American airspace.

Why the word “embarrassing” matters

The Financial Times headline captures the tone: “Embarrassing.” The criticism is not yet a revolt capable of changing White House policy. But it reveals a widening gap between the administration's public portrayal of Canada and the lived institutional experience of Americans whose careers or constituencies are closely tied to the country.

No policy reversal — yet

There is no evidence that this backlash has caused the White House to retreat from its tariff threats or its broader political rhetoric. That distinction is essential. Institutional discomfort is not the same thing as a change of policy.

But alliances are partly sustained by constituencies inside each country that believe the relationship is valuable. The emerging criticism shows that Canada still has such constituencies in the United States: manufacturers, unions, border-state politicians and former military leaders. In a prolonged confrontation, those groups could become increasingly important.

The larger pattern

Taken together with Detroit's objections to auto tariffs and Shawn Fain's labour criticism, the military and Republican response suggests the Canada confrontation is beginning to generate resistance across several parts of the American system. The common argument is not sentimental affection for Canada. It is that the two countries are so integrated — economically, militarily and geographically — that attempts to punish Canada can impose costs on Americans and on institutions the United States itself built.
