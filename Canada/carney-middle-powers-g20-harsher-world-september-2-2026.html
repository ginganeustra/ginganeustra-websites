A SMILING CARNEY IN A HARSHER WORLD: CANADA’S MIDDLE-POWER TEST BEGINS

Canada’s answer to U.S. coercion is taking shape: build practical ties with countries that still value trust, rules and reliable supply chains.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 12:28 a.m. ET

The photograph is almost too neat for the moment. Mark Carney is smiling, composed and formally dressed at the White House in May 2025. Behind the image is a relationship that has since become far harsher: a trade fight, tariffs, sovereignty taunts and a Canadian government openly accepting that the old dependence on Washington is no longer safe.

Canada’s response cannot consist only of retaliation. Retaliation is necessary when a country is targeted, but it does not create a future. The larger Carney strategy is to make Canada harder to isolate and less vulnerable to economic coercion by building practical alliances with countries that still value reliable rules, trusted supply chains and democratic institutions.

That is the meaning of the middle-powers idea now being tested around the G20. Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne has said Canada is being viewed as a preferred transatlantic partner at a time of global uncertainty. It is a claim that deserves scrutiny, but it is also a serious opening. Canada has assets that partners need: energy, critical minerals, food, advanced manufacturing capacity, public institutions and an economy linked to both Europe and the United States.

The question is whether Ottawa can convert the new interest into signed projects, investment, transport links and dependable markets. A middle-power strategy is not a slogan. It succeeds only when a country can offer something tangible and follows through quickly enough that companies and governments make decisions before the next shock.

The current rupture with the United States gives that work urgency. Washington’s 50 per cent tariffs on a major group of Canadian goods and Canada’s announced countermeasures have shown businesses that market access can be used as political leverage. Canada cannot replace the U.S. market overnight. It can, however, stop treating that market as the only strategic horizon.

Champagne’s transatlantic language points toward a specific kind of diversification. It is not an argument for turning away from the United States or pretending integration does not matter. It is an argument for giving Canada more than one serious option. A country with alternative buyers, investors and supply partners negotiates differently from a country that has nowhere else to go.

Europe is the natural first place to test that proposition. Canada has a trade agreement with the European Union, growing security relationships and a profile as a stable supplier in a world preoccupied with energy security and critical minerals. But the challenge is operational. Good speeches about shared values will not build a processing plant, a port link or a supply chain. Canadian governments will need to make it easier for partners to invest and easier for Canadian firms to sell.

The G20 is also a reminder that the United States is not Canada’s only difficult economic partner. The meeting has been dominated by disputes over Chinese exports, industrial capacity and critical inputs. Canada cannot simply choose between Washington and Beijing. It must develop the ability to cooperate with democracies and other reliable partners while keeping the capacity to make its own decisions about its economic future.

That is the tougher world behind the smiling Carney image. The old model assumed that economic integration with the United States made Canada safer and richer. It did make Canada richer. It no longer guarantees political safety. A neighbour can be indispensable and still use that indispensability against you.

Carney’s middle-power approach begins from that recognition. It asks Canada to become useful in several directions at once: a reliable energy and minerals partner for Europe, a serious security partner in the Atlantic and Arctic, a place where allied manufacturers can invest, and a country willing to work with other middle powers when larger states treat trade and finance as instruments of pressure.

There is no easy version of this plan. Diversification costs money, requires infrastructure, and takes years. It will sometimes collide with domestic arguments about regulation, projects and foreign investment. But the alternative is increasingly clear. If Canada does not build more economic and diplomatic options, every future dispute with Washington begins with the same question: how much can Canada afford to refuse?

The smiling photograph should not be read as nostalgia for a relationship that has vanished. It is better read as contrast. Canada can be confident without being naive, open to negotiation without accepting humiliation, and allied with the United States without organizing its entire future around U.S. goodwill. The test now is whether the government can turn that posture into a durable network of partners before the next crisis arrives.
