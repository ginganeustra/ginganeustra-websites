World Watch: The trade war is widening into an industrial-bloc fight

Canada’s tariff battle now sits inside a wider contest over Chinese manufacturing, European industrial policy and protected markets.

Published Aug. 28, 2026 · Canada at War

Global developments reviewed Aug. 28, 2026. This report excludes the widely covered GDP-growth story.

The Canada–United States rupture is no longer an isolated North American dispute. A fresh review of developments around the world points to a broader shift: governments and industrial lobbies are increasingly treating trade not as a set of rules for lowering barriers, but as a tool for protecting domestic capacity, forcing concessions and reorganizing supply chains by political bloc.

For Canada, that makes the immediate tariff fight more consequential. It is taking place while Europe debates stronger defences against Chinese industrial competition, while its closest non-member economic partners worry about being shut out of European procurement and subsidy rules, and while Washington adds symbolic pressure to an already damaged relationship with Ottawa.

Canada–U.S.: a tariff fight becomes a sovereignty fight

Reuters reported Aug. 22 that the United States imposed 50-per-cent tariffs on some Canadian goods after negotiations failed. The U.S. Trade Representative said the measures applied to nearly US$20 billion of imports from Canada—about 5.2 per cent of U.S. imports from Canada in 2025—and, unlike earlier measures, did not retain the usual USMCA exemption for covered goods.

The immediate commercial effects are real, especially for exposed manufacturers. But the political conflict continued to move beyond trade on Aug. 27, when President Donald Trump ordered U.S. federal agencies to use the name “Lake America” for Lake Ontario. The order cannot change the name in Canada, nor does it alter international geography. Its significance is political: it turns an already hostile tariff conflict into a public assertion of unilateral American power over a shared border and a shared lake.

That does not itself change a customs rate. It does make a negotiated return to ordinary relations harder, because it reinforces the Canadian view that the dispute is not only about market access.

Germany presses the EU to toughen up on China

The other major development is in Europe. Reuters reported Aug. 28 that German industry groups are pressing Chancellor Friedrich Merz for a stronger policy response to Chinese imports and subsidies. Germany’s trade deficit with China reached €89.3 billion in 2025, according to the Reuters report, and the pressure is particularly acute in automobiles and manufacturing equipment.

Business groups are asking for a “level playing field,” including faster use of EU trade-defence tools and potentially tariffs or local-content rules. Merz has asked cabinet ministers for proposals and has left open a common EU response if October discussions with China do not produce progress.

This is not yet an EU decision to impose new tariffs. It is a warning that the European market Canada hopes to deepen ties with may become more protective of European production. For Ottawa, that raises a practical question for the October Canada–EU summit: can a deeper partnership secure reciprocal procurement, minerals and defence-industrial access before “Made in Europe” rules harden into barriers for partners?

Europe’s industrial turn is straining its closest partners

The Financial Times reported Aug. 28 that Norway, Switzerland, Iceland and Liechtenstein are increasingly concerned that new EU subsidies, import restrictions and local-content requirements will exclude them despite their deep integration with the European market.

Norway’s concern has immediate relevance to Canada. Oslo is debating whether its existing relationship offers enough influence as EU decisions increasingly shape steel, energy and industrial policy. The country has been affected by EU steel-import restrictions, while Switzerland has warned that exclusionary rules can weaken, rather than strengthen, European supply chains.

Canada is not in the European Economic Area and will have less automatic access than those countries. The lesson is not that closer Canada–EU ties are unwise; it is that political warmth and market access are different things. Any October package needs concrete, enforceable arrangements—especially on defence procurement, critical minerals, energy and standards—rather than broad language about partnership.

What this means for Canada

The world is not dividing neatly into free-trading democracies on one side and protectionist states on the other. The United States is using tariffs against a treaty ally. Europe is considering stronger industrial defences against China while its neighbours fear exclusion. China remains central to the competition over manufacturing capacity and supply chains.

Canada’s task is therefore not simply to replace the U.S. market. It is to secure durable access to several markets without accepting political dependence on any one of them. That means treating trade agreements as security arrangements, insisting on enforceable reciprocity, and moving quickly while the rules of the next industrial order are still being written.
