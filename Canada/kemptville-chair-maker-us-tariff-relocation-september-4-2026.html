THE TARIFF IS PUSHING A KEMPTVILLE CHAIR MAKER TOWARD THE U.S.

DFC Woodworks has made outdoor furniture in eastern Ontario since 1955 and exports about 70% of it to the United States. Its president says the 50% duty has put relocation on the table.

Published Sept. 4, 2026 · 11:50 a.m. ET

KEMPTVILLE, Ont. — A family-owned eastern Ontario furniture manufacturer that has operated since 1955 is considering moving most of its production to the United States after its exports were hit with a 50-per-cent U.S. tariff. DFC Woodworks Inc. makes outdoor furniture, including Muskoka and Adirondack chairs, and sends about 70 per cent of its production south of the border.

President François Bruneau told The Canadian Press that the company is now weighing three choices: absorb the tariff, raise U.S. prices and risk losing sales, or move the bulk of manufacturing into the United States. For a company whose American customers account for most exports, each option changes either its costs, its prices or where the work is done.

The tariff is collected when the product enters the United States, but the commercial cost can move through the supply chain in different ways. An importer can pay it and pass some or all of the cost to customers, an exporter can cut its price to offset some of the duty, or the two sides can split the burden.

Bruneau’s account is one company’s response, not evidence that Canadian manufacturers as a group are relocating. It does, however, show how a border tariff can turn a trade-policy dispute into a location decision for a small exporter whose largest market is on the other side of that border.

The problem is particularly difficult for smaller firms because they generally have fewer plants, less financing and less capacity to develop new foreign markets quickly. Carleton University professor Fen Hampson told The Canadian Press that small and medium-sized companies often lack the resources larger businesses can use to diversify or reorganize supply chains.

Ottawa has announced a broader package of tariff-response programs, including regional development funding and support intended to help companies retool, diversify and find new markets. Those programs can reduce adjustment costs, but they do not remove the 50-per-cent duty DFC faces when a chair crosses the U.S. border.

Canada is also preparing counter-tariffs on C$27.6 billion of U.S. imports beginning Sept. 8. Those measures are designed as retaliation for the U.S. action; they do not directly lower the tariff on a Canadian manufacturer’s exports.

DFC’s decision is therefore a business calculation under a policy that remains in force. If the company keeps production in Kemptville, it must decide how much of the new cost can be absorbed or passed on; if it shifts production, the tariff would have contributed to moving Canadian manufacturing closer to the market imposing it.
