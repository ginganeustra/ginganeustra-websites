CANADA AT WAR
Auto war · Japan · Ontario · North American production

Toyota and Honda built Canada into their North American system. Trump’s 50% tariff could force them to unwind it.

The tariff is aimed at Canada. The companies facing some of the hardest choices are Japanese: Toyota and Honda together account for more than three-quarters of the vehicles made in Canada.

Donald Trump’s proposed 50% tariff on Canadian-built cars is usually described as a weapon against Canada’s auto industry. That is true, but incomplete.

It is also a direct shock to two of Japan’s largest industrial companies.

Reuters reported Monday that Toyota and Honda account for more than three-quarters of all cars manufactured in Canada. Analysts warned that if the 50% tariff takes effect Jan. 1 as proposed, both companies could face pressure to close some Canadian assembly lines.

Neither company told Reuters it planned to do that. Both declined to comment on the report. The point is therefore not that plant closures have been decided. It is that the economics of decades of integrated North American production could be deliberately made much worse.

The strategic contradiction: Washington says it is taxing Canadian production. Much of the production it is making more expensive belongs to foreign companies—especially Japanese companies—that built plants in Canada precisely because North America was supposed to function as an integrated market.

Canada is not a side operation for either company

Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada has three Ontario assembly plants, two in Cambridge and one in Woodstock. Toyota says its Canadian operation can produce more than half a million vehicles a year. Woodstock builds the RAV4 and RAV4 Hybrid; Cambridge plants make Lexus vehicles and other Toyota production.

Honda’s Canadian manufacturing base is in Alliston, Ontario. Honda’s own manufacturing information lists two auto plants producing the Civic family and CR-V, including hybrid versions. The operation dates to 1986 and has been part of Honda’s North American manufacturing network for four decades.

These plants are not simply making vehicles for Canadians. They are deeply tied to the American market.

Reuters, citing Barclays analysts, said Canadian-built vehicles accounted for 17% of Toyota’s U.S. sales last year and almost one-quarter of Honda’s. Toyota exports Canadian-built RAV4s to the United States, while Honda exports CR-Vs.

A tariff can redraw a supply chain without moving a border

A 50% import tariff does not physically prevent a vehicle from crossing into the United States. It changes the price calculation so severely that a company has to ask whether the existing production map still makes sense.

Toyota and Honda would have several theoretical options: absorb some of the tariff, raise prices, redirect Canadian production to other markets, shift U.S.-bound production to American factories, or reduce Canadian output. None is simple.

Vehicles are configured for specific markets. Factories already have capacity constraints. Suppliers have built their own networks around existing plants. Moving a model is not the same as moving a spreadsheet entry.

That is why the Jan. 1 date matters even though a negotiated settlement remains possible. Industrial planning takes years, while tariff decisions can alter the economics in months.

The pressure reaches Tokyo

There is another layer to the story. Toyota and Honda are already trying to navigate a global car market transformed by Chinese electric-vehicle competition, high investment costs and shifting demand.

Reuters reported that Toyota has committed to invest up to US$10 billion over five years to expand U.S. operations. Honda, meanwhile, has warned that uncertainty over the North American trade framework could affect whether it builds another assembly plant on the continent.

That gives Washington leverage—but it also creates friction with companies headquartered in a major U.S. ally.

A Canadian tariff story can therefore become a Japanese industrial-policy story. If a Japanese company is forced to rethink a profitable Canadian plant because Washington has made cross-border sales uneconomic, the cost of the Canada-U.S. confrontation does not stop at the Canadian border.

What the tariff is really testing

For decades, the central premise of the North American auto industry was that a vehicle could be designed, sourced and assembled across borders within a common production system. Canadian, American, Mexican, Japanese, Korean and European companies all invested around that premise.

Trump’s proposed tariff tests whether that premise still exists.

If it goes into effect at 50%, Toyota and Honda would face an unusually stark decision: preserve Canadian production and accept a huge penalty on access to their largest market, or reorganize production around a political border that their investment decisions were designed to make less important.

For Canada, that is the danger. The auto war is not merely about losing exports tomorrow. It is about whether the next generation of capital spending concludes that Canada is still safely inside the North American manufacturing system.
