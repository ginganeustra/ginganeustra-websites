Trump’s 50% Canada tariffs rest on a law never tested in court.

Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 sat virtually untouched for nearly a century. Now it is the legal foundation for a major escalation against Canada—and lawyers are questioning whether that foundation can survive its first serious court challenge.

Published Aug. 29, 2026 · 10:55 a.m. ET · Reporting and analysis

President Donald Trump’s latest tariff offensive against Canada has opened a second front beyond the economic confrontation itself: whether the extraordinary legal authority Washington is relying on actually reaches as far as the administration says it does.

The new 50 per cent duties were imposed under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, a Depression-era provision authorizing a president to respond when another country discriminates against American commerce. The White House has invoked it against Canadian treatment of U.S. motor vehicles, dairy and other commerce.

What makes the move remarkable is not simply the age of the statute. According to legal experts interviewed by The Associated Press, Section 338 has never previously been used and has never been tested in court. That leaves the central legal questions surrounding Trump’s tariffs largely unanswered.

A blank canvas in American trade law

Lawyers are examining whether subsequent U.S. trade statutes displaced or constrained the broad presidential discretion apparently contained in the 1930 provision. Modern American trade law normally operates through elaborate investigative and administrative procedures. Section 338 predates much of that architecture.

The administration’s proclamations say Canadian measures burden or disadvantage U.S. commerce. But the tariff lists reach beyond the headline disputes over dairy, autos and alcohol, exposing a wider range of Canadian goods to punitive duties.

That does not mean the tariffs are unlawful. No court has ruled that they are, and the administration can point to statutory language expressly granting the president tariff authority in response to discrimination. The significance is that the authority is now being exercised at enormous scale without a body of judicial precedent explaining its limits.

Why this matters to Canada

Ottawa has already chosen retaliation rather than capitulation. Canada says it will match Washington’s new tariffs dollar for dollar and rate for rate, with counter-tariffs taking effect September 8 on products in sectors including steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper and electronics.

Prime Minister Mark Carney has framed the confrontation as a question of sovereignty as well as commerce. In announcing the suspension of negotiations, he said Canada would not accept U.S. demands that compromised its independence or key domestic policies.

A successful American legal challenge could therefore change the battlefield. Canadian governments cannot simply sue in U.S. courts whenever they dislike an American trade measure; standing and jurisdiction matter. But American importers, businesses or other parties directly injured by the duties may have stronger routes to litigation.

The immediate question is whether such a plaintiff will emerge. The larger question is whether a 96-year-old statute that has never been litigated can sustain one of the most consequential tariff actions in modern Canada–U.S. relations.

Until a court answers that question, Section 338 is simultaneously a powerful weapon and an untested one.
