CARNEY PUTS BARTON AND GREWAL ON THE HUNT FOR $1 TRILLION

Published Aug. 31, 2026 · 5:15 p.m. ET

← Canada at War

Mark Carney is putting two people with deep connections to global capital in charge of the federal agency that is supposed to persuade global capital to choose Canada.

Ottawa announced Monday that Dominic Barton, the former global managing partner of McKinsey & Company and former Canadian ambassador to China, will become chair of Invest in Canada. Gurinder Grewal, an investor whose career has ranged across energy, infrastructure, industrials and technology, will become the agency’s chief executive officer.

The appointments matter because Invest in Canada is being handed a much larger job at precisely the moment Canada is trying to reduce its economic vulnerability to the United States. The Carney government says it wants to catalyse $1 trillion in total investment over five years, with Invest in Canada helping move international investors from an initial look at the country to actual projects in energy, critical minerals, artificial intelligence and infrastructure.

That makes this more than a personnel announcement. It is part of the machinery behind the government’s attempt to answer the central economic problem exposed by the trade war: Canada cannot become less dependent on one enormous customer simply by announcing that it intends to diversify. Somebody has to find the capital, connect it to projects, navigate governments and regulations, and get things built.

The Barton choice

Barton is an unusually consequential appointment for a part-time board chair. He spent more than 30 years at McKinsey, including nine as global managing partner and six as Asia chair. He later served as Canada’s ambassador to China from 2019 to 2021 and currently chairs Rio Tinto and LeapFrog Investments. He has also chaired the federal Advisory Council on Economic Growth.

That résumé gives Ottawa something it clearly wants: direct access to the international corporate and institutional-investment networks Canada is now trying to court. It also guarantees scrutiny. Barton’s long McKinsey career and his time in Beijing have made him a familiar and sometimes controversial figure in Canadian public life. In this job, however, the useful question is measurable: can the new leadership convert contacts and government ambition into projects that actually diversify Canadian economic power?

Grewal will have the operational job. Ottawa says he founded MEM Growth Partners after more than a decade at Advent International, where he became a partner and co-head of industrials, infrastructure and energy. His appointment is full-time for five years; Barton’s chairmanship is a three-year, part-time term.

A much bigger mandate

Invest in Canada was established under federal legislation in 2018 to attract and facilitate foreign direct investment. Its own 2026–27 plan says it will concentrate on energy, advanced manufacturing, defence, life sciences, value-added agriculture and critical minerals while expanding relationships with economies that share Canada’s strategic outlook. The agency explicitly describes reducing reliance on any single trading partner as part of that work.

The numbers are already substantial. Ottawa says the stock of foreign direct investment in Canada reached $1.6 trillion at the end of 2025, up 6.9 per cent in a year, while 2025 inflows reached $96.8 billion — the highest annual level since 2007. The government says roughly $280 billion in public capital investment and incentives over five years is intended to help enable more than $1 trillion in combined public, private and institutional investment.

There is a tension here worth watching. Economic sovereignty does not mean replacing dependence on American capital with indiscriminate dependence on somebody else’s capital. Foreign investment can expand Canadian productive capacity, open export routes and create leverage; it can also transfer ownership and strategic control. The important question is therefore not simply how many billions Barton and Grewal can attract, but what Canada gets in return and which capabilities remain Canadian.

Carney’s language makes clear that diversification is the objective. The Prime Minister said Monday that Canada is trying to build an economy that is “not reliant on any single trading partner.” That is the correct test for this appointment as well.

If Invest in Canada becomes a more powerful front door for energy, minerals, AI and infrastructure investment, its success should eventually be visible in things more concrete than announcements: mines financed, power built, factories opened, ports expanded, export customers added and Canadian companies given more than one market in which they can survive.

Washington has spent months demonstrating what happens when one trading relationship becomes powerful enough to be used as leverage. Ottawa’s answer now includes Barton and Grewal, a federal investment agency with a larger mandate, and a very large number — $1 trillion — attached to the door.
