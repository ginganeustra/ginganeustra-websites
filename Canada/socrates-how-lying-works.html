The flattery of the soul

Socrates’ warning about rhetoric is simple: a message can feel good, sound powerful and win applause while doing nothing to make its audience wiser.

Socrates · Rhetoric · Persuasion · Permanent reference essay

There is a kind of persuasion that tries to discover what is true. There is another kind that tries to discover what an audience wants to hear.

In Plato’s Gorgias, the character Socrates treats that distinction as a moral problem. Rhetoric, when detached from knowledge and justice, can become a form of flattery: it gives people the pleasure of feeling persuaded without doing the harder work of understanding.

That is why Socrates belongs in a library about lying even though his central concern is not simply the false statement. His concern is the relationship between speech and the health of the person listening.

Flattery imitates a real craft

Plato has Socrates compare genuine arts with imitations that produce pleasant effects. Medicine aims at health. Cookery can aim merely at taste. Physical training aims at the body’s condition. Cosmetics can create the appearance of that condition.

The point is not that food or appearance is evil. The point is that an imitation can borrow the surface of a genuine good while avoiding its discipline.

Socrates makes the same charge against manipulative rhetoric. Political speech can imitate the appearance of civic wisdom while concentrating on approval. The speaker learns the audience’s fears, resentments and desires, then feeds them back in a satisfying form.

The audience experiences recognition: this person understands us. But recognition is not evidence that the speaker’s explanation is true.

Flattery asks, “What will make them agree with me?” Inquiry asks, “What would make this claim true?”

The danger is greater when the speaker is skilled

A clumsy lie can be checked. A gifted rhetorician can make checking feel unnecessary.

The techniques are familiar across centuries: confidence, repetition, vivid examples, ridicule of opponents, emotional contrast and the suggestion that doubters are weak or corrupt. None of those techniques determines whether the underlying claim is true.

Socrates’ method moves in the opposite direction. He asks for definitions. He separates one claim from another. He looks for contradictions. He forces the speaker to say what would count as evidence and what principle is being applied.

This can feel irritating because it removes the emotional momentum of a speech. That irritation is useful. A claim that survives only while the audience is excited may be a claim that cannot survive examination.

Persuasion without knowledge

One of the Gorgias’ central questions is whether a persuasive speaker can convince people about subjects the speaker does not actually understand.

The answer is plainly yes. An audience often cannot independently master every technical subject placed before it. The skilled speaker can therefore sound more convincing than the expert.

That creates a permanent political vulnerability. Citizens must make judgments about economics, war, public health, law and technology without becoming specialists in all of them. A politician or propagandist does not need to know more than the expert. The propagandist needs to know more about the audience.

Socratic questioning offers a defence: ask what the speaker knows, how the speaker knows it, what evidence could change the conclusion and whether the same standard is applied to allies and opponents.

Why flattery works

Flattery rarely feels like manipulation from the inside. It feels like agreement.

A speaker tells us that our anger is justified, our group is innocent, our opponents are contemptible and our preferred solution is obvious. The message may contain truth, falsehood or a mixture of both. Its psychological power comes from relieving us of the need to examine ourselves.

Socrates repeatedly makes that examination the price of serious thinking. It is easier to expose a rival’s contradiction than one’s own. It is easier to demand evidence from an enemy than from a friend.

The deepest defence against political flattery is therefore not cynicism. It is intellectual symmetry: the willingness to question a pleasing claim as aggressively as an offensive one.

The test

When a political message feels almost perfectly tailored to what you already want to believe, ask four questions.

What exactly is being claimed? What evidence would establish it? What evidence would disprove it? Would I accept the same reasoning from the other side?

If the message collapses under those questions, the problem is not that it failed to persuade. The problem is that persuasion was doing work that evidence should have done.

That is the Socratic warning. A society can be talked into error not only by people who invent facts, but by people who learn to flatter the soul.
