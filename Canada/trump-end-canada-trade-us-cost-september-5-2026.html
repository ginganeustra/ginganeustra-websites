TRUMP SAYS AMERICA COULD STOP TRADING WITH CANADA. HERE’S WHAT AMERICA WOULD LOSE.

No major economic study located for this analysis models the literal zero-trade scenario Donald Trump described Friday. Studies of much smaller disruptions already point in one direction: the United States would damage its own factories, energy system, farms, exporters and household incomes.

Published Sept. 5, 2026 · 8:25 a.m. ET

WASHINGTON — Donald Trump did not merely threaten another tariff Friday. In an Oval Office exchange, he said the United States could play “hardball” by doing no trade with Canada at all. He claimed the United States would save as much as US$90 billion a year and said Canada, deprived of what he called 95 per cent of its business, would be in “a heap of trouble.”

Canada would unquestionably face a severe shock if its largest trading relationship vanished overnight. The Canadian economy is much smaller, and the United States remains by far its largest export market. But the other half of Trump’s proposition — that the United States could simply stop trading and come out richer — runs against the evidence from every serious model of a smaller Canada–U.S. rupture located for this analysis.

Analysis: There is an important limit to what can be claimed. No reputable model located here assumes that every truck, pipeline, rail car, power line, service contract and commercial transaction between Canada and the United States suddenly goes to zero. It would therefore be irresponsible to manufacture a precise GDP or jobs number for Trump’s literal scenario by multiplying the results of tariff studies. The available research instead tells us what happens under disruptions that are substantially less extreme.

The newest model starts with tariffs far below an embargo

In August, Oxford Economics published a study commissioned by the Canadian American Business Council examining three possible futures for the bilateral relationship: the mid-2026 tariff status quo, a successful USMCA renegotiation and a breakdown of the agreement with much broader duties. Its harshest case is not an embargo. Trade continues.

Even that produces substantial American losses. Oxford estimates that a USMCA breakdown would leave the United States with about 214,000 fewer jobs in 2027 than under the status quo and reduce cumulative U.S. GDP by about US$1 trillion over 2026 to 2035. U.S. exports would be 4.5 per cent lower by 2035. Those figures describe a tariff escalation, not the elimination of trade that Trump floated Friday.

The study was commissioned by a business group that supports the cross-border relationship, which matters when assessing its provenance. Oxford says it used a multi-model quantitative approach to examine macroeconomic, industry and regional effects. Its direction is consistent with older academic and policy research: dismantling North American integration hurts all participants, though the percentage damage is larger in Canada and Mexico than in the much larger U.S. economy.

Oxford’s conclusion also cuts directly against the proposition that reducing Canadian trade automatically strengthens American manufacturing. In the breakdown scenario, higher input costs and weaker Canadian demand leave U.S. manufacturing smaller and the U.S. trade balance worse than under the status quo.

Two-thirds of the goods trade is not a finished product on a shelf

The central fact is what actually crosses the border. Oxford calculates that roughly 65 per cent of bilateral goods trade consists of intermediate inputs: parts, materials and components used to make something else. The Canada–U.S. border often runs through a production process rather than between a foreign seller and an American consumer.

That is why a cutoff would not resemble Americans simply deciding to buy a different brand. A U.S. factory can be a customer of a Canadian supplier in the morning and a supplier to a Canadian plant in the afternoon. Remove one side of that chain and the American plant may lose both an input and a market.

The auto industry is the clearest example. North American vehicle production has been integrated for decades. Engines, transmissions, seats, steel, aluminum, electronics and partly assembled vehicles move back and forth among plants centred on Ontario, Michigan, Ohio and surrounding states. Some components cross the border repeatedly before a finished vehicle leaves the line.

There is a real-world preview of what happens when just one major crossing stops. During the 2022 Ambassador Bridge blockade, manufacturers on both sides cut production because parts could not reach plants. Public Safety Canada later estimated that one week of disruption at the Windsor–Detroit crossing cost U.S. workers and businesses in the automotive and transportation industries about US$51 million. That was one bridge for one week, not a prohibition on commercial traffic across the continent.

The Midwest cannot replace Canadian oil with a mouse click

Energy makes the zero-trade scenario still harder. The U.S. Energy Information Administration says Canada remained by far the largest foreign crude-oil supplier to the United States in 2025, averaging about 3.9 million barrels a day. That was roughly three-fifths of all U.S. crude imports.

The physical system matters more than the headline number. Alberta heavy crude flows through pipelines to Midwestern refineries, many of which were built or modified to process those grades. An embargo would not mean that millions of identical barrels simply arrive from another country the next morning.

Refineries are configured for particular crude types. Pipelines point in particular directions. Replacement barrels require available supply, tanker or pipeline capacity, terminals and refinery compatibility. Some substitution would eventually occur, but the first effect of an abrupt cutoff would be disruption and higher adjustment costs inside the United States.

American farmers would lose Canada twice

Trump’s proposition also runs into potash, one of the three fundamental nutrients used in fertilizer. The U.S. Geological Survey’s 2026 Mineral Commodity Summary puts American net import reliance for potash at 92 per cent of apparent consumption. Canada supplies the overwhelming majority of those imported tonnes.

A cutoff would therefore hit American agriculture from two directions. Farmers would lose access to the dominant foreign source of an essential fertilizer input while U.S. agricultural exporters would lose Canadian customers. Alternative suppliers exist, but replacing Canadian volumes would expose U.S. farms to longer supply lines and greater reliance on countries including Russia.

That is a recurring feature of the bilateral relationship. Canada is not simply a supplier to the United States. It is also a large customer. An embargo closes both loading docks at once.

Lumber, uranium and electricity expose different kinds of dependence

Canadian sawmills supply a substantial share of lumber consumed in the United States. The two countries have fought over softwood lumber for decades, but duties are different from removing the supply entirely. A sudden cutoff would tighten an input used in American home construction and renovation while builders and wholesalers tried to replace it.

Nuclear power presents a slower-moving but strategically important exposure. U.S. civilian reactors are heavily dependent on imported uranium, and Canada is one of the largest secure suppliers. Reactors would not go dark the day after an embargo because fuel is procured on long timelines, but Washington would be voluntarily eliminating a major friendly source while simultaneously trying to reduce dependence on Russian uranium.

Electricity dependence is more regional. The United States as a whole does not depend on Canadian power for a large share of its electricity, but northeastern markets can rely heavily on imports during periods of high demand. EIA reported that Canada supplied an average 14 per cent of New England electricity demand in January 2025. A cutoff would therefore produce concentrated reliability and price questions rather than one uniform national blackout.

Then come the American exporters

Trump described trade as though an American deficit were money disappearing from the Treasury. It is not. A trade deficit measures the difference between the value of imports and exports; imports are goods and services Americans receive in exchange for payment. Ending trade would also end sales made by American companies to Canadians.

Oxford estimates that roughly 1.4 million U.S. jobs are tied to the bilateral commercial relationship. Exposure is uneven. Great Lakes manufacturing is tied to autos and machinery. Gulf Coast producers sell chemicals and industrial products north. Upper Midwest farmers sell grain, meat and processed food. Border states sell electricity, services and manufactured products into Canadian markets.

That geographic unevenness matters politically. The national U.S. economy is enormous, so a bilateral shock can look modest as a percentage of total GDP while being severe for particular plants, towns and states.

Older research reaches the same direction from different models

An academic model published by the National Bureau of Economic Research examined revoking NAFTA. Economists Raphael Auer, Barthélémy Bonadio and Andrei Levchenko estimated a U.S. welfare loss of about 0.2 per cent, compared with losses around 2 per cent in Canada and Mexico. The aggregate American number was smaller because the U.S. economy is much larger, but sectoral effects inside the United States varied widely.

The researchers also found that U.S. congressional districts that gave Trump a higher vote share in 2016 tended to experience larger real-wage reductions in their model. The national average concealed local pain.

Brookings modelled another near-neighbour: a 25 per cent U.S. tariff on imports from Canada and Mexico. It estimated about US$45 billion in lost U.S. output without retaliation and roughly US$75 billion if Canada and Mexico retaliated. U.S. employment fell in both cases. Again, trade still continues in that scenario.

The US$90-billion arithmetic is not how trade works

Trump said the United States would “save” as much as US$90 billion annually by not trading with Canada. The claim treats a bilateral deficit like a bill the U.S. government pays Canada. It is neither a government expenditure nor the gross cost of the relationship.

U.S. government figures put the 2025 U.S. goods deficit with Canada at roughly US$48 billion, partly offset by a large U.S. services surplus. Even if the bilateral deficit were US$90 billion, eliminating imports would not turn that amount into federal savings. Americans would also give up the goods they were buying, while American exporters would lose Canadian sales.

Trump’s assertion that Canada does 95 per cent of its business with the United States is also unsupported by the latest merchandise-trade data. Statistics Canada figures reported for July put the U.S. share of Canadian goods exports at 66.35 per cent, with the year-to-date share at 68 per cent. Canada remains highly exposed to the U.S. market. It is not 95 per cent exposed on that measure.

Canada would be hurt more. That does not make America a winner.

Every model cited here says Canada bears the larger proportional shock. That is unsurprising: a much larger share of Canadian exports goes to the United States than of U.S. exports goes to Canada. Canada would have to redirect production, find new buyers and endure painful adjustment on a scale the larger U.S. economy would not.

But economic interdependence is not a contest in which the larger country keeps the other side’s losses. The United States would lose Canadian supplies, lose Canadian customers, strand investments, interrupt factories and force costly substitutions. Prices would rise in affected goods. Some production would move; some would simply shrink.

Analysis: The defensible conclusion is therefore narrower and stronger than pretending to know the exact cost of a literal embargo. No credible zero-trade estimate located for this report can tell us the precise GDP loss from Trump’s proposal. What the available evidence does show is that policies dramatically less severe than a full trade cutoff already produce substantial American losses.

The first consequences would probably not appear as one dramatic national GDP number. They would appear as missing auto parts in Michigan, crude-supply problems in Midwestern refineries, fertilizer pressure on farms, tighter lumber markets, lost export orders and companies trying to rebuild supply chains that took decades to create.

Trump can threaten to stop trade with a sentence. The physical economy on both sides of the border cannot be separated with one.
