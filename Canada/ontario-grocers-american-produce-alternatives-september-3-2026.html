WHY ARE ONTARIO GROCERS STILL STOCKING SO MUCH AMERICAN PRODUCE?

Ontario farms are harvesting apples, grapes, peaches, peppers, tomatoes, cucumbers, lettuce, onions and watermelon. Mexico, South America, South Africa and other suppliers can provide much of what Canada cannot grow. Yet shoppers trying to avoid American goods still frequently find U.S.-grown produce in the bins.

Published Sep. 4, 2026 · 1:14 a.m. ET

Walk through an Ontario supermarket produce department in September and the mismatch is difficult to miss. Canadians have spent more than a year telling pollsters they are checking labels and avoiding American goods. Ontario is simultaneously in one of the fullest months of its own harvest calendar. And for fruits Canada cannot grow commercially, the United States is far from the only country capable of supplying Canadian stores.

Yet U.S.-grown produce remains a familiar presence on Ontario shelves.

That does not mean every American strawberry, head of lettuce or bunch of grapes can simply be replaced tomorrow. Price, trucking distance, existing contracts, distribution-centre requirements, packaging, consistency and the timing of harvests all shape what large grocery chains buy. For some products and some weeks, the United States is the most practical large-scale supplier.

But the seasonal calendar also shows many cases in which American origin cannot be explained by an absence of alternatives.

Foodland Ontario’s official availability guide lists apples, blueberries, cranberries, grapes, muskmelon, nectarines, peaches, pears, plums, raspberries, field strawberries and watermelon as available in September. Ontario vegetables available during the month include beans, beets, broccoli, cabbage, carrots, cauliflower, celery, corn, cucumbers, eggplant, garlic, kale, leeks, lettuce, onions, peppers, potatoes, spinach, squash, tomatoes and zucchini. Greenhouse cucumbers, peppers, tomatoes, lettuce and strawberries extend the domestic season much further, with several available year-round.

An American apple sitting beside an Ontario apple in September is therefore not there because Canada cannot grow apples. The same point applies to Ontario peppers, field tomatoes, onions, potatoes, cucumbers, lettuce and watermelon while local crops are available.

Tomatoes growing in a greenhouse in San Pablo Huitzo, Oaxaca, Mexico. Photo: Nsaum75 / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Imported produce presents a different question. Canada will always import large quantities of fresh fruits and vegetables. Bananas cannot be grown commercially here at meaningful scale. Nor can oranges or avocados. But importing those products does not require buying them from the United States.

Mexico is already deeply integrated into Canada’s produce supply and is a dominant source of avocados as well as a major supplier of berries, peppers and other vegetables. Tropical Latin American countries supply bananas. Southern Hemisphere producers including Chile, Peru and South Africa ship large quantities of fruit into Northern Hemisphere markets during their counter-season.

Shine Muscat grapes identified by the photographer as from Peru. Photo: Dinkun Chen / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

The scale of the alternative supply network is not theoretical. University of British Columbia researchers who built the Canada Food Flows dataset traced fruit and vegetable movements from U.S. states and 149 other countries. Their 2022 data show U.S. states supplied 36% of Canada’s fruit imports and 67% of its vegetable imports. The remaining imports came from elsewhere, alongside substantial interprovincial trade.

Those numbers also show why changing grocery sourcing is easier in some categories than others. U.S. growers are especially entrenched in Canadian vegetable supply. UBC’s analysis found that in British Columbia, for example, 81% of spinach, 70% of lettuce and 75% of onions came from the United States in 2022. That reflects a continental supply system built over decades around trucking routes, contracts and year-round production regions such as California.

Other categories already demonstrate that the U.S. can be bypassed. UBC found 97% of British Columbia’s avocados came from Mexico in 2022. Bananas entering Canada overwhelmingly originate in tropical countries rather than the continental United States.

Citrus illustrates the sourcing choice particularly well. Canada grows no commercial orange crop, but that does not make an American orange inevitable. South Africa, Chile, Egypt and other major citrus exporters serve international markets, and Southern Hemisphere citrus seasons overlap Canadian demand during parts of the year.

Orange harvest in Egypt. Photo: Doaa Adel / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

The question for grocery chains is therefore not whether the entire produce department can become Canadian. It cannot. It is whether retailers are changing their buying decisions where domestic or non-U.S. supply is commercially available.

That question matters because Canadian shoppers have made their preferences unusually clear. An Angus Reid Institute survey conducted July 23-25 found 40% of Canadians who buy groceries said they actively check product origin in stores. Most of those who check said they put American products back when they can find a Canadian or other non-U.S. alternative. The survey included 1,790 Canadian adults.

Fresh produce gives retailers more power over that consumer choice than many packaged-goods aisles do. A shopper can often choose among several brands of cereal, ketchup or detergent. If a supermarket carries only one origin of oranges or one supplier of lettuce, the shopper’s options are to buy what is there or leave without it.

Canada’s major grocery chains therefore make part of the country’s diversification decision before customers arrive. A shopper cannot choose an Ontario watermelon over an American one if the domestic fruit never reaches the bin. A shopper cannot choose Mexican peppers or South African citrus if the retailer’s procurement system continues ordering from long-established U.S. suppliers.

Harvesting Sémillon grapes at Gisborne Peak in Victoria, Australia. Photo: Nathan Jones / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

There are legitimate reasons a grocery company may decide that an alternative supplier is not workable in a particular week: insufficient volume, a higher delivered price, food-safety requirements, grading standards, transportation time or contracts already signed. Public trade statistics cannot tell shoppers which of those reasons explains a specific American item in a specific Ontario store.

What the public data also do not show is how aggressively Loblaw, Empire/Sobeys, Metro, Walmart Canada, Costco and other large retailers have changed their fresh-produce sourcing since the Canada-U.S. trade dispute intensified. The companies do not publish a weekly origin breakdown for their produce departments that would allow consumers to see whether their dependence on U.S. fruit and vegetables is falling.

That leaves a straightforward information gap. Canadians can see the country-of-origin sticker on an apple, orange or tomato. They generally cannot see why the retailer chose that country, what alternatives were considered or whether the chain has set targets for reducing U.S. sourcing where substitutes exist.

The contrast is sharpest in September because Ontario’s own list of available produce is so long. This is not February, when climate sharply narrows the domestic field crop. It is a month when Ontario farms are shipping a broad range of fruits and vegetables at the same time Canadian consumers are telling pollsters that origin affects what they buy.

The global market also offers Canada options for food it cannot grow. Mexico can sell Canada tomatoes, peppers, avocados and berries. Latin American countries can sell bananas. Peru and Chile are major fruit exporters. South Africa and Egypt can sell citrus. Australia and other counter-season suppliers can fill some gaps later in the year.

The existence of those suppliers does not prove that every U.S. shipment should be replaced, or that doing so would always reduce prices. It does mean the choice is wider than Canada versus the United States.

Canadian consumers have shown that many of them want that wider choice. Whether it appears in the produce aisle depends heavily on the companies deciding what goes into it.
