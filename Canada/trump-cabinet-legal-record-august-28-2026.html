The legal record of Trump’s Cabinet

A fact-checked guide separates charges, lawsuits, investigations and settlements—and records their actual outcomes.

Published Aug. 28, 2026 · Canada at War

A fact-checked guide to the publicly documented legal matters involving the current Trump Cabinet, updated Aug. 27, 2026.

The phrase “Trump’s Cabinet has legal problems” is too loose to be useful. A criminal charge, a civil complaint, an administrative investigation, a settlement by a government agency and an allegation never tested in court are not the same thing. They do not carry the same evidentiary weight, and they do not produce the same outcome.

This report separates them.

It examines the current officials listed on the White House’s Cabinet page as of Aug. 27, 2026. The list has changed since the administration began: Todd Blanche is attorney general; Jay Clayton is director of national intelligence; Markwayne Mullin is secretary of homeland security; and Keith Sonderling is acting labour secretary. The president and vice-president are not included in the roster used here, because this is a record of Cabinet and Cabinet-level officials rather than a retelling of Donald Trump’s separate and unusually extensive personal legal history.

The standard is deliberately conservative. An entry appears only where a public record shows one of the following:

  • a criminal charge, conviction or plea;
  • a civil lawsuit naming the official personally, with its present result;
  • a formal government or regulatory investigation, with its stated outcome; or
  • a disclosed settlement that names the official or, in the case of a government office-holder, a settlement by the government that the official led or was sued in an official capacity.

That distinction matters. A settlement is not automatically an admission of wrongdoing. A civil lawsuit is an allegation until a court finds otherwise. And an investigation closed without charges is not an exoneration by a court — but neither is it a criminal case.

The result is more limited than an opposition-research file, and more useful. It identifies what is documented, says what happened to it, and does not turn proximity to a controversy into an accusation.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr.: a criminal conviction, followed by treatment

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has the clearest criminal record of any present Cabinet official identified in this review.

In 1983, Kennedy was arrested in South Dakota for heroin possession. He pleaded guilty to a felony charge. A judge imposed two years’ probation rather than jail and required treatment; accounts of the case report that the probation term was later reduced to one year after he entered treatment.

Kennedy has not disputed the underlying history. He has spoken publicly for years about his addiction and recovery, saying he stopped using drugs after the arrest and treatment. That public account does not erase the conviction, but it is relevant context: this was a 43-year-old drug-possession case, not a current allegation or an unresolved prosecution.

There is no basis for describing the case as merely an accusation. It ended in a guilty plea and a criminal sentence. There is equally no basis for inflating it into a claim of current misconduct. The documented outcome was a conviction, probation and treatment.

Pete Hegseth: no charge after police investigation; a private settlement followed

Secretary of War Pete Hegseth was investigated by police in Monterey, California, after a woman alleged that he sexually assaulted her in 2017.

Hegseth has denied the allegation and said the encounter was consensual. The Monterey County district attorney did not file criminal charges, stating that the evidence did not meet the standard required to prove a case beyond a reasonable doubt. That is the criminal outcome: no charge and no criminal conviction.

The matter did not end there. During his confirmation process, Hegseth disclosed that he had made a settlement payment to the woman. In a written response to Sen. Elizabeth Warren, he put the amount at $50,000. His lawyer has said the settlement was reached after the woman raised the prospect of civil litigation; the public record does not show a civil trial or a court finding that Hegseth committed an assault.

The careful description is therefore important. A woman made a sexual-assault allegation; police investigated; prosecutors declined to charge; Hegseth denied the allegation; and he later made a reported $50,000 settlement. The settlement is a fact. It is not, by itself, a legal admission of liability.

Howard Lutnick: civil disputes and a shareholder settlement, not criminal charges

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s record is chiefly one of business litigation, not criminal prosecution.

The most significant recent case concerned a $50-million compensation award from Newmark Group, the commercial-real-estate company where Lutnick was executive chairman. Shareholders sued in Delaware, challenging the bonus. In 2025, the parties settled; reporting on the settlement and a subsequent Delaware fee ruling described the full $50 million as returned to the company. The shareholder lawyers received a separate fee award.

The settlement resolved a corporate-governance dispute. It did not produce a criminal charge, a criminal finding or an admission by Lutnick that he committed a crime. For readers, the meaningful fact is that the bonus was challenged in court and was given up through a settlement.

Lutnick’s FINRA BrokerCheck report also records historic civil-litigation disclosures from his securities-industry career. One entry reports an $80,000 settlement; another says remaining claims were settled without a payment by Lutnick. BrokerCheck disclosures are regulatory-record summaries, not a substitute for the underlying pleadings, and they do not establish criminal liability. They are included here because they are official, publicly available disclosures and because the request is for settlements as well as charges.

Lutnick has also been associated with major lawsuits brought by Cantor Fitzgerald and other firms he led. Those corporate cases are not included as personal legal matters unless he was individually named or a public record identifies his personal settlement. A chief executive’s company being sued is not evidence that the executive was personally charged with wrongdoing.

Linda McMahon: a pending civil case, not a criminal charge

Education Secretary Linda McMahon and her husband, former WWE executive Vince McMahon, were named with WWE in civil litigation brought by former WWE “ring boys.” The plaintiffs allege that the company and its leaders failed to protect minors from sexual abuse by a former employee during the 1980s and 1990s.

The allegations are serious, but the legal status must be described precisely. This is a civil lawsuit, not a criminal prosecution of Linda McMahon. The plaintiffs seek damages; the case has faced procedural delays and litigation over where and how claims should proceed. There has been no merits verdict holding Linda McMahon liable, and this review found no reported personal settlement by her of those claims.

McMahon and WWE have disputed the claims. That response belongs in any fair account, as does the difference between Linda McMahon’s position and separate allegations or investigations involving Vince McMahon. This article does not assign his alleged conduct, his personal settlements or any regulatory action involving him to her.

That is a basic but necessary discipline. Shared ownership of a company and marriage to another executive do not dissolve the legal distinction between defendants, claims and outcomes.

Kristi Noem: settlements by South Dakota, not personal criminal cases

Kristi Noem is no longer the current secretary of homeland security, but the events below occurred during her Cabinet tenure and her prior governorship. They are included because the subject is the current administration’s Cabinet record.

Several lawsuits involved actions by the State of South Dakota while Noem was governor. They should not be described as criminal cases against Noem, nor as personal settlements by her.

In 2024, South Dakota settled litigation brought by the Transformation Project, a transgender advocacy organization, after the state cancelled the organization’s grant-funded contract. The state agreed to pay $300,000 and issue an apology. The settlement involved the state and its health department. It did not include a judicial finding that Noem personally committed a legal wrong, and it was not a criminal matter.

That same year, South Dakota paid $42,000 to settle an open-records lawsuit related to documents sought concerning the governor’s border-deployment programme. Earlier reporting also described a $200,000 settlement with a former state employee who alleged that she had been forced to retire. Each is relevant to the legal record of an administration Noem headed. None is a conviction, criminal charge or personal payment by Noem.

Noem has also faced ethics complaints and reporting concerning travel, promotion of a dental practice and an undisclosed payment to a personal company. Those are not listed here as established legal violations because, on the sources reviewed, they are allegations, complaints or reporting rather than a completed charge with an adjudicated outcome. Reporting a complaint as if it were a conviction would be false.

Kelly Loeffler: a federal inquiry closed without charges

Small Business Administration Administrator Kelly Loeffler was among several U.S. senators whose stock trades drew scrutiny early in the COVID-19 pandemic.

The Justice Department investigated trades made on Loeffler’s behalf after a private Senate briefing and before the market crash. In May 2020, the department closed its inquiry. No criminal charge was filed against Loeffler, and no SEC enforcement action against her emerged from the inquiry.

Loeffler said the closure cleared her of wrongdoing. The more exact formulation is narrower: prosecutors closed the investigation without bringing a case. A decision not to charge is not a judicial declaration of innocence; it is nevertheless materially different from a criminal charge or conviction.

Other current Cabinet officials: no verified personal case meeting this report’s standard located

The White House’s current roster includes Scott Bessent, Todd Blanche, Doug Burgum, Jay Clayton, Doug Collins, Sean Duffy, Jamieson Greer, Markwayne Mullin, John Ratcliffe, Brooke Rollins, Marco Rubio, Keith Sonderling, Scott Turner, Russ Vought, Chris Wright and Lee Zeldin, in addition to the officials addressed above.

For those officials, this review did not locate a public record of a personal criminal charge, criminal conviction, named civil settlement or concluded personal regulatory enforcement action that meets the standard used in this article. That wording is intentional. It is not a sweeping personal endorsement, and it does not imply that none has ever been criticized, sued in an official capacity or associated with a company that faced litigation.

Several have been named as defendants in policy litigation because of their offices. Cabinet secretaries are routinely sued over executive actions, regulations or agency decisions. Such suits test the legality of government action. They do not, without more, establish personal misconduct by the office-holder.

Similarly, some officials have worked for companies or organizations that have faced litigation. Business litigation against a corporation is not a criminal charge against every executive, board member or lawyer connected with it. A serious legal record has to keep those categories separate.

Why the distinctions matter

Public discussion of political figures often collapses four questions into one:

1. Was there an allegation?

2. Was there an investigation or a filed lawsuit?

3. Was there a charge, judgment, plea or settlement?

4. What did the final record actually establish?

The answer to one is not automatically the answer to the others.

Kennedy’s 1983 case ended in a criminal conviction. Hegseth’s 2017 matter produced no criminal charge but did lead to a reported settlement. Loeffler was investigated but not charged. McMahon faces a pending civil claim, not a criminal prosecution. Noem’s state-government settlements should not be inaccurately turned into personal admissions. Lutnick’s business disputes are civil matters with their own documented resolutions.

Those are not technicalities. They are the difference between reporting and insinuation.

The public has a legitimate interest in the legal history of powerful officials, especially those entrusted with the Justice Department, national security, health, education, commerce and the federal budget. But that interest is best served by a record that is scrupulous about what happened, who was named, what was alleged, what was denied and how a case ended.

The fact that an official has no charge on this list does not decide whether their policies are wise. And the fact that an official has faced a charge, lawsuit or investigation does not eliminate the need to state the outcome. Accountability begins with accuracy.
