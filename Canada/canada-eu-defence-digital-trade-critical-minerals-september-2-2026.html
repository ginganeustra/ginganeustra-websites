CANADA AND EUROPE ARE BUNDLING DEFENCE, DIGITAL TRADE AND CRITICAL MINERALS

The latest Anand–Kallas readout shows something larger than another diplomatic meeting: Ottawa and Brussels are assembling security, industry, technology and trade into a single strategic relationship.

Published Sept. 2, 2026 · 9:23 p.m. ET

Canada and the European Union are moving several relationships that once sat in separate policy boxes into the same strategic package. In a readout released Wednesday, Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand and EU foreign-policy chief Kaja Kallas pointed to progress on the Canada–EU Security and Defence Partnership, Canadian participation in Europe’s SAFE defence-financing framework, negotiations toward a digital trade agreement, critical-minerals cooperation and broader economic security.

The list is important because of what it combines. Defence procurement is no longer being discussed in isolation from industrial capacity. Digital trade is no longer simply an extension of conventional commerce. Critical minerals are being treated as security assets as well as export commodities. Canada and the EU are increasingly talking about all of them as parts of the same problem: how democratic partners can become more resilient when supply chains, technology platforms and strategic industries are exposed to political pressure.

Anand met Kallas during the informal meeting of EU foreign ministers, known as the Gymnich, in Wicklow, Ireland. Global Affairs Canada had already described Anand’s participation as historic: it was the first time a Canadian foreign minister had taken part in a Gymnich meeting. The Sept. 2 readout now gives a clearer picture of what Canada wants to build from that access.

The most immediate defence component is SAFE, the European Union’s Security Action for Europe financial instrument. Canada and the EU are working on closer defence-industrial cooperation through the program. For Canada, that matters because access to European defence procurement and financing could give Canadian companies another large market and another set of industrial relationships at a moment when Ottawa is trying to reduce strategic dependence on the United States.

The digital component may prove just as important. Canada and the EU are negotiating a digital trade agreement intended to complement CETA. A previous Canada–EU Digital Partnership Council statement said the two sides want to modernize digital trade rules while also cooperating on digital credentials, standards, online-platform governance and foreign information manipulation. That makes the eventual agreement potentially relevant not only to trade volumes but to Canada’s ability to shape rules for data, platforms and emerging technologies with partners other than Washington.

Critical minerals sit between the defence and digital files. Copper, nickel, lithium, rare earths and other strategic materials are necessary for military systems, grids, batteries, semiconductors and advanced manufacturing. Canada has reserves and mining expertise; Europe has large industrial demand and an explicit interest in reducing vulnerable dependencies. The Anand–Kallas readout says cooperation on critical minerals and economic security has already intensified.

The political language is also notable. The ministers described Canada and the EU as “trusted partners” and emphasized resilience on both sides of the Atlantic. That is standard diplomatic language, but the policy content underneath it is concrete. Canada is looking for durable alternatives and supplements to a continental relationship in which access to the U.S. market, technology and defence system can also become leverage. Europe is looking for reliable suppliers, defence partners and technology relationships of its own.

The relationship will get another test soon. Anand and Kallas said preparations are under way for a Canada–EU summit in Canada on Oct. 29 and 30. That gives Ottawa and Brussels less than two months to convert broad strategic alignment into specific agreements, procurement access, financing rules and timelines.

For Canada, the central question is whether Europe becomes a genuine second strategic pillar or remains primarily a diplomatic counterweight. The newest readout suggests Ottawa is trying to make the relationship operational. Defence finance, digital trade, critical minerals and economic security are now being discussed together because the same sovereignty question runs through all of them: how many essential Canadian capabilities can be diversified away from dependence on a single foreign system.
