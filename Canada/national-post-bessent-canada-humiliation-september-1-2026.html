WASHINGTON MOCKED CANADA. THE NATIONAL POST MADE THE HUMILIATION THE HEADLINE.

Scott Bessent's insult was news. The editorial choice was how to package it. Canada at War believes that choice deserves scrutiny when U.S. officials are openly trying to portray Canada as weak, small and unable to resist.

Published Sept. 1, 2026 · 3:04 p.m. ET

There it was Monday evening, delivered by one of Canada's largest newspapers: “U.S. Treasury secretary taunts Canadian navy's lack of firepower: ‘Two submarines from the Edmonton mall.’”

The words belonged to U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. The headline belonged to the National Post. Those are different facts, and the distinction matters.

Bessent really did mock Canada in his CNBC interview. The CNBC transcript records him saying that Canada could not engage in a tit-for-tat fight with a country 13 times larger, dismissing Ottawa's claim that it was weathering the dispute, blaming Prime Minister Mark Carney for a political shouting match and making the Edmonton Mall submarine joke. He also accused Carney of acting in his own and his party's interests and claimed Canada had walked away from the best trade deal offered to any country.

The National Post did not invent those remarks. It was entitled to report them. Other Canadian outlets reported them too. Global News, for example, framed its story around Bessent's statement that the countries were “not at war”, while Edmonton coverage naturally focused on his bizarre use of the mall's former submarine attraction as a punchline.

Canada at War's concern is narrower and more specific: what happens when an American official's attempt to humiliate Canada becomes the principal promotional hook used by a Canadian news organization?

Journalism is not only the collection of facts. It is also selection, hierarchy and framing. A newsroom chooses what goes in the headline, what gets pushed on social media, what becomes the photograph, and which part of a long interview becomes the reason to click.

In this case, the National Post chose the humiliation itself: a Trump administration official taunting the Canadian navy's “lack of firepower.” That does not establish that the newspaper agrees with Bessent. It does not establish that its editors intended to help the Trump administration. Canada at War is making neither allegation.

What it does establish is visible on the screen: a senior American official tried to portray Canada as militarily insignificant and incapable of standing up to the United States, and the National Post marketed that taunt to Canadians as the centrepiece of the story.

Carney answers the memes and taunts

Hours later, Carney addressed the wider style of American pressure. The Guardian reported that he said serious trade discussions could resume when Americans “stop doing memes, stop throwing shade, stop trying to be tough” and begin treating the negotiations seriously. His point was that theatrical provocation is now occurring alongside a dispute with enormous economic consequences.

Carney also described the behaviour as “beneath their office” and said, “It's not constructive, but that's their democracy.” The complaint reaches beyond one Bessent joke: the Canadian government is increasingly treating mockery, memes and public belittlement as part of the environment surrounding stalled trade negotiations, rather than as harmless background noise.

Why the framing matters

The Trump administration's pressure campaign against Canada is not conducted only through tariff schedules and negotiating texts. Its public rhetoric repeatedly carries a second message: Canada is smaller; Canada is weaker; Canada is dependent; resistance is pointless.

Bessent's “13 times larger” line made that theory explicit. The submarine joke turned it into entertainment.

That creates an uncomfortable editorial problem for Canadian journalism. Ignoring remarks by the U.S. Treasury secretary would be irresponsible. Canadians need to know what powerful American officials are saying about their country. But reporting an attempt at humiliation is not identical to packaging the humiliation itself as the attraction.

Different outlets can legitimately make different choices. The point is not that there is a forbidden headline. The point is that those choices are worthy of criticism and comparison — particularly during a confrontation in which the larger country is openly trying to convince the smaller country that resistance cannot work.

A case study, not proof of a conspiracy

This National Post headline should not be asked to prove more than it can prove.

It is not evidence that a New Jersey hedge fund ordered an editor to write a particular line. It is not evidence that every Postmedia journalist shares one political view. It is not evidence of coordination with Washington.

It is a case study in amplification.

The United States Treasury secretary supplied the belittling message. A Canadian newspaper chose the belittling message as the headline. Readers can see both facts and decide what they think of the editorial judgment.

There is even a final absurdity. Bessent's shopping-mall intelligence appears to have been off. West Edmonton Mall's attraction operated four submarines, not two.

That factual miss is trivial compared with the trade confrontation. But it is an apt ending to a performance built around the idea that Canada should accept its own insignificance.

Washington supplied the insult.

The National Post supplied the headline.

Canada at War believes Canadians should pay attention to both.
